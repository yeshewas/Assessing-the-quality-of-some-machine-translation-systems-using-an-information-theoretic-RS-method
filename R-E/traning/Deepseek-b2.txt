The Lady with the Dog

I

    People were saying that a new face had appeared on the embankment: a lady with a dog. Dmitry Dmitrich Gurov, who had already been living in Yalta for two weeks and had gotten used to it there, also began to take an interest in new faces. Sitting in the pavilion at Vernet's, he saw a young lady walking along the embankment, a short blonde in a beret; a white Pomeranian was running behind her.

    Later he met her in the municipal garden and in the square several times a day. She walked alone, always in the same beret, with the white Pomeranian; no one knew who she was, and they simply called her: the lady with the dog.

    "If she's here without her husband and without acquaintances," Gurov reasoned, "then it wouldn't be a bad idea to get acquainted with her."

    He was not yet forty, but he already had a twelve-year-old daughter and two sons who were high school students. He had been married early, when he was still a second-year student, and now his wife seemed half as old again as he was. She was a tall woman, with dark eyebrows, straight, imposing, solid, and, as she called herself, a thinking woman. She read a lot, did not use the hard sign in letters, called her husband not Dmitry but Dimitri, while he secretly considered her limited, narrow, ungraceful, was afraid of her, and did not like being at home. He had begun being unfaithful to her long ago, was unfaithful often, and probably for that reason he spoke almost always badly of women, and when women were talked about in his presence, he referred to them as:

    — "The lower race!"

    It seemed to him that he had been sufficiently taught by bitter experience to call them whatever he liked, but still without the "lower race" he could not have lived even two days. In the company of men he was bored, ill at ease; with them he was taciturn and cold, but when he found himself among women, he felt free and knew what to talk to them about and how to behave; and even being silent with them was easy for him. In his appearance, in his character, in his whole nature, there was something attractive and elusive that disposed women to him, drew them to him; he knew this, and some force also drew him to them.

    Repeated experience, indeed bitter experience, had long ago taught him that every intimacy, which at first so pleasantly diversifies life and appears to be a sweet and light adventure, inevitably grows, among decent people, especially among Muscovites who are slow to act and indecisive, into an entire problem, an extremely complicated one, and the situation finally becomes burdensome. But at every new meeting with an interesting woman, this experience somehow slipped from memory, and he wanted to live, and everything seemed so simple and amusing.

    And so one day towards evening he was dining in the garden, and the lady in the beret was approaching unhurriedly to take the neighboring table. Her expression, her walk, her dress, her hairstyle told him that she was from decent society, married, in Yalta for the first time and alone, and that she was bored here... In the stories about the impurity of local morals there was much falsehood; he despised them and knew that such stories were mostly invented by people who themselves would have gladly sinned if they had known how, but when the lady sat down at the neighboring table three steps away from him, he recalled these stories about easy conquests, about trips to the mountains, and a tempting thought of a quick, fleeting affair, a romance with an unknown woman whom you don't know by first or last name, suddenly took possession of him.

    He affectionately beckoned the Pomeranian to him, and when it came over, he shook his finger at it. The Pomeranian growled. Gurov shook his finger again.

    The lady glanced at him and immediately lowered her eyes.

    — "He doesn't bite," she said and blushed.

    — "May I give him a bone?" And when she nodded affirmatively, he asked kindly: "Did you arrive in Yalta long ago?"

    — "About five days."

    — "And I'm already finishing my second week here."

    They were silent for a little while.

    — "Time passes quickly, and yet it's so boring here!" she said, not looking at him.

    — "It's just fashionable to say that it's boring here. An ordinary person lives at home somewhere in Belev or Zhizdra — and he's not bored, but he comes here: 'Oh, how boring! Oh, the dust!' You'd think he came from Granada."

    She laughed.Then they both continued eating in silence, like strangers; but after dinner they walked side by side — and a playful, light conversation began between free, contented people who did not care where they were going or what they were talking about. They strolled and talked about how strangely the sea was lit; the water was lilac-colored, so soft and warm, and a golden strip stretched across it from the moon. They talked about how stuffy it was after the hot day. Gurov said he was a Muscovite, a philologist by education, but worked at a bank; he had once prepared to sing in a private opera but gave it up, owned two houses in Moscow... And from her he learned that she had grown up in St. Petersburg but married in S., where she had been living for two years, that she would stay in Yalta for another month, and her husband might come for her, as he too wanted a rest. She could not explain where her husband worked — in the provincial administration or the provincial zemstvo board — and that amused even her. Gurov also learned that her name was Anna Sergeyevna.

     Later, in his room, he thought about her, about how she would probably meet him the next day. It had to be so. As he went to bed, he remembered that she had so recently been a schoolgirl, studying, just like his own daughter now; he recalled how much timidity and awkwardness there was in her laughter, in her conversation with a stranger — it must have been the first time in her life that she was alone, in such a situation where she was followed, looked at, and spoken to only with a secret purpose that she could not help but suspect. He remembered her thin, delicate neck, her beautiful gray eyes.

     "There is something pitiful about her after all," he thought, and began to fall asleep.



II



     A week had passed since their acquaintance. It was a holiday. The rooms were stuffy, and on the streets dust swirled in whirlwinds, tearing off hats. Everyone was thirsty all day, and Gurov often went to the pavilion and offered Anna Sergeyevna either water with syrup or ice cream. There was nowhere to go.

     In the evening, when it had quieted down a bit, they went to the pier to watch the steamer come in. There were many strollers on the dock; people had gathered to meet someone, holding bouquets. And here, two peculiarities of the smart Yalta crowd were strikingly evident: elderly ladies were dressed like young women, and there were many generals.

     Due to the rough sea, the steamer arrived late, after the sun had already set, and it took a long time turning around before docking. Anna Sergeyevna looked through her lorgnette at the steamer and the passengers, as if searching for acquaintances, and when she turned to Gurov, her eyes sparkled. She talked a lot, her questions were abrupt, and she immediately forgot what she had asked; then she lost her lorgnette in the crowd.

     The fashionable crowd dispersed, faces were no longer visible, the wind died down completely, but Gurov and Anna Sergeyevna stood there, as if waiting for someone else to come off the steamer. Anna Sergeyevna was now silent and smelled the flowers, not looking at Gurov.

     "The weather has improved toward evening," he said. "Where should we go now? Shouldn't we go somewhere?"

     She did not answer.

     Then he looked at her intently, suddenly embraced her and kissed her on the lips, and he was enveloped by the scent and dampness of the flowers, and immediately he looked around fearfully: had anyone seen?

     "Let's go to your place..." he said quietly.

     And they both walked quickly.

     In her room it was stuffy, smelling of perfume she had bought at a Japanese shop. Gurov, looking at her now, thought: "What kinds of encounters life brings!" From his past he retained memories of carefree, good-natured women, cheerful from love, grateful to him for happiness, however brief; and of those — like his wife, for instance — who loved without sincerity, with excessive talk, affectation, hysteria, with an expression as if it were not love, not passion, but something more significant; and of two or three very beautiful, cold women, on whose faces would suddenly flash a predatory expression, a stubborn desire to take, to snatch from life more than it can give, and these were no longer young, capricious, unreasoning, domineering, unintelligent women, and when Gurov grew cold toward them,But that beauty aroused hatred in him, and the lace on her undergarments seemed to him then like scales.

But here there was the same timidity, the awkwardness of inexperienced youth, an awkward feeling; and there was an impression of confusion, as if someone had suddenly knocked on the door. Anna Sergeyevna, this "lady with the dog," took what had happened somehow especially seriously, very seriously, as if it were her downfall — so it seemed, and this was strange and out of place. Her features drooped and faded, and her long hair hung sadly on either side of her face; she sat lost in thought in a dejected pose, like a sinner in an old painting.

"It's not good," she said. "You're the first one who won't respect me now."

On the table in the room was a watermelon. Gurov cut himself a slice and began to eat it slowly. At least half an hour passed in silence.

Anna Sergeyevna was touching; she exuded the purity of a decent, naive woman who had seen little of life; the solitary candle burning on the table barely lit her face, but it was clear that she was troubled at heart.

"Why would I stop respecting you?" asked Gurov. "You don't know what you're saying."

"May God forgive me!" she said, and her eyes filled with tears. "This is terrible."

"You're almost making excuses."

"How can I make excuses? I am a bad, low woman, I despise myself and don't think about making excuses. I didn't deceive my husband, but myself. And not just now, but I've been deceiving myself for a long time. My husband may be an honest, good man, but he is a lackey! I don't know what he does there, how he serves, but I only know that he is a lackey. When I married him, I was twenty; I was tormented by curiosity, I wanted something better—after all, there is another life, I told myself. I wanted to live! To live and to live... Curiosity burned me... you don't understand this, but, I swear to God, I could no longer control myself, something was happening to me, I couldn't be held back, I told my husband I was ill and came here... And here I walked around as if in a haze, like a madwoman... and now I have become a vulgar, worthless woman whom anyone can despise."

Gurov was already bored listening; her naive tone, this repentance so unexpected and out of place, irritated him; if it weren't for the tears in her eyes, one might have thought she was joking or playing a role.

"I don't understand," he said quietly, "what do you want?"

She hid her face on his chest and pressed close to him.

"Believe me, believe me, I beg you..." she said. "I love an honest, pure life, but sin is disgusting to me; I don't know what I'm doing. Ordinary people say: the devil misled me. And now I can say of myself that the devil has misled me."

"That's enough, that's enough..." he muttered.

He looked into her fixed, frightened eyes, kissed her, spoke softly and tenderly, and little by little she calmed down, and cheerfulness returned to her; they both began to laugh.

Later, when they went out, there was not a soul on the embankment; the city with its cypresses looked completely dead, but the sea still roared and beat against the shore; one boat rocked on the waves, and on it a lantern flickered sleepily.

They found a cab and drove to Oreanda.

"I just found out your last name downstairs in the hall: it's written von Diederitz on the board," said Gurov. "Is your husband German?"

"No, I think his grandfather was German, but he himself is Orthodox."

In Oreanda, they sat on a bench, not far from the church, looking down at the sea and were silent. Yalta was barely visible through the morning mist; white clouds stood motionless on the mountain peaks. The leaves did not stir on the trees; cicadas chirped, and the monotonous, muffled sound of the sea, coming from below, spoke of peace, of the eternal sleep that awaits us. It roared below in the same way when there was neither Yalta nor Oreanda yet; it roars now and will roar just as indifferently and dully when we are gone. And in this constancy, in the complete indifference to the life and death of each of us, lies perhaps the pledge of our eternal salvation, the unceasing movement of life on earth, unceasing perfection. Sitting next to a young woman who seemed so beautiful at dawn,Calmed and enchanted by this fairy-tale scene — the sea, the mountains, the clouds, the wide sky — Gurov thought about how, in essence, if you really think about it, everything in this world is beautiful, everything except what we ourselves think and do when we forget about the higher purposes of existence, about our human dignity.

A man approached — a watchman, it seemed — looked at them, and left. And this detail seemed so mysterious and also lovely. A steamer from Feodosia was visible, illuminated by the morning dawn, already without its lights.

"Dew on the grass," said Anna Sergeyevna after a silence.

"Yes. Time to go home."

They returned to town.

Then every afternoon they met on the promenade, had breakfast together, dined, strolled, and marveled at the sea. She complained of sleeping poorly and of her heart beating anxiously, asked the same questions over and over, tormented now by jealousy, now by the fear that he did not respect her enough. And often in the square or the garden, when no one was near, he would suddenly draw her to him and kiss her passionately. Complete idleness, these kisses in broad daylight, with furtive glances and fear that someone might see, the heat, the smell of the sea, and the constant parade of idle, elegant, well-fed people before their eyes seemed to transform him; he told Anna Sergeyevna how beautiful she was, how seductive, was impatiently passionate, never left her side for a moment, while she often grew pensive and kept asking him to admit that he did not respect her, did not love her at all, but only saw her as a vulgar woman. Almost every evening, late, they would go somewhere outside town, to Oreanda or to the waterfall; and the outing was a success, the impressions were invariably magnificent and majestic each time.

They expected her husband to arrive. But a letter came from him, in which he informed her that his eyes were hurting, and begged his wife to return home quickly. Anna Sergeyevna hurried.

"It's good that I'm leaving," she said to Gurov. "It's fate itself."

She traveled by horse-drawn carriage, and he saw her off. They traveled the whole day. When she boarded the express train carriage and the second bell had rung, she said:

"Let me look at you once more... Look once more. Like this."

She was not crying, but she was sad, as if ill, and her face trembled.

"I will think of you... remember you," she said. "God be with you, stay. Don't remember me with ill will. We are parting forever, this is necessary, because we should never have met at all. Well, God be with you."

The train departed quickly, its lights soon disappeared, and within a minute the noise was no longer audible, as if everything had conspired on purpose to end this sweet oblivion, this madness, as quickly as possible. And, left alone on the platform, gazing into the dark distance, Gurov listened to the chirping of crickets and the hum of telegraph wires with a feeling as if he had just woken up. And he thought that now there was one more adventure or escapade in his life, and it too had already ended, and only a memory remained... He was moved, sad, and felt a slight remorse; after all, this young woman, whom he would never see again, had not been happy with him; he had been kind and cordial to her, but still, in his dealings with her, in his tone and caresses, there was a hint of light mockery, the coarse arrogance of a happy man who was, moreover, almost twice her age. All the while she had called him kind, extraordinary, noble; obviously, she saw him as different from what he really was, which meant he had involuntarily deceived her...

Here at the station it already smelled of autumn, the evening was cool.

"It's time for me to go north too," thought Gurov, leaving the platform. "Time!"

III

Back home in Moscow, everything was already winter-like, the stoves were lit, and in the mornings, when the children got ready for school and drank tea, it was dark, and the nanny lit a lamp for a short while. The frosts had already begun. When the first snow falls, on the first day of sleigh rides, it is pleasant to see the white earth, the white roofs, one breathes softly and sweetly, and at such times one remembers one's youthful years. The old lindens and birches, white with hoarfrost, have a good-natured expression, they are closer to the heart than cypresses and palms,and in their vicinity, one no longer wants to think of mountains and the sea.

Gurov was a Muscovite. He returned to Moscow on a fine, frosty day, and when he put on his fur coat and warm gloves and strolled along Petrovka, and when on Saturday evening he heard the ringing of bells, his recent trip and the places he had been lost all their charm for him. Little by little, he immersed himself in Moscow life, now greedily reading three newspapers a day, claiming he didn't read Moscow papers on principle. He was already drawn to restaurants, clubs, dinner parties, and anniversaries, and he was flattered that famous lawyers and actors visited him, and that he played cards with a professor at the doctors' club. He could already eat a whole portion of solyanka from a frying pan…

Another month would pass, and Anna Sergeyevna, it seemed to him, would be covered in a fog of memory, only occasionally appearing in dreams with a touching smile, as other women had. But more than a month passed, deep winter set in, and everything in his memory was as clear as if he had parted from Anna Sergeyevna only yesterday. And the memories blazed up more and more intensely. Whether in the evening silence the voices of children doing their lessons reached his study, whether he heard a romance or an organ in a restaurant, or a blizzard howled in the fireplace, suddenly everything would come back to him: what had happened on the pier, the early morning with the fog over the mountains, the steamer from Feodosia, and the kisses. He would pace the room for a long time, remembering and smiling, and then memories would turn into dreams, and the past in his imagination would mingle with what was to come. Anna Sergeyevna did not appear in his dreams, but followed him everywhere like a shadow, watching him. Closing his eyes, he saw her as if alive, and she seemed more beautiful, younger, and more tender than she had been; and he himself seemed better than he had been then, in Yalta. In the evenings, she looked at him from the bookcase, from the fireplace, from the corner; he heard her breathing, the gentle rustle of her dress. On the street, he followed women with his eyes, looking for someone who resembled her…

And a strong desire tormented him to share his memories with someone. But at home he couldn't speak of his love, and outside home there was no one. Not with the tenants, and not at the bank. And what was there to say? Had he even loved then? Was there anything beautiful, poetic, edifying, or simply interesting in his relationship with Anna Sergeyevna? So he had to speak vaguely about love, about women, and no one guessed what the matter was, and only his wife would move her dark eyebrows and say:

"You, Dmitry, aren't suited at all for the role of a ladies' man."

One night, leaving the doctors' club with his partner, a clerk, he couldn't restrain himself and said:

"If only you knew what a charming woman I met in Yalta!"

The clerk got into his sleigh and drove off, but suddenly turned around and called out:

"Dmitry Dmitrich!"

"What?"

"Well, you were right earlier: the sturgeon was a bit off!"

These words, so ordinary, suddenly for some reason outraged Gurov; they seemed humiliating, unclean. What savage manners, what faces! What senseless nights, what dull, unremarkable days! Furious card playing, gluttony, drunkenness, constant talk about the same things. Pointless business and conversations all about the same things consume the best part of the time, the best strength, and in the end, what remains is some sort of stunted, wingless life, some kind of nonsense, and there's no way to leave or escape, as if you were sitting in a madhouse or a convict's barracks!

Gurov didn't sleep all night and was outraged, then spent the whole day with a headache. On the following nights, he slept poorly, either sitting up in bed thinking or pacing from corner to corner. The children annoyed him, the bank annoyed him; he didn't want to go anywhere or talk about anything.

In December, during the holidays, he got ready for a trip and told his wife he was going to St. Petersburg to intercede for a young man, and he left for S. Why? He didn't really know himself. He wanted to see Anna Sergeyevna and talk to her, to arrange a meeting if possible.

He arrived in S. in the morning and took the best room in the hotel, where the entire floor was covered with gray army cloth and on the table stood an inkwell, gray with dust, featuring a rider on a horse.whose hand holding a hat was raised, but whose head was knocked off. And Anna Sergeyevna began visiting him in Moscow. Once every two or three months, she left S. and told her husband that she was going to consult a professor about her female ailment — and her husband believed and didn’t believe her. Upon arriving in Moscow, she stayed at the Slaviansky Bazaar and immediately sent a man in a red cap to Gurov. Gurov went to her, and no one in Moscow knew about it.

One winter morning, he was walking to her like this (the messenger had come the evening before and hadn’t found him). His daughter was with him, as he wanted to see her off to school — it was on the way. Large, wet snow was falling.

“It’s three degrees above zero now, and yet it’s snowing,” Gurov said to his daughter. “But that warmth is only on the surface of the earth; in the upper layers of the atmosphere, the temperature is completely different.”

“Papa, why isn’t there thunder in winter?”

He explained that too. He spoke and thought about how here he was going to a secret meeting, and not a living soul knew about it, and probably never would. He had two lives: one open, which everyone who needed to could see and know, full of conventional truth and conventional deceit, exactly like the lives of his acquaintances and friends; and another — flowing in secret. And by some strange twist of circumstances, perhaps by chance, everything that was important, interesting, necessary for him, in which he was sincere and did not deceive himself, which formed the core of his life, happened in secret from others; while everything that was his lie, his shell in which he hid to conceal the truth — such as his job at the bank, arguments at the club, his “inferior race,” going with his wife to anniversary celebrations — all of that was in the open. And judging others by himself, he did not believe what he saw, and always assumed that every person’s real, most interesting life passes under the cover of secrecy, as under the cover of night. Each personal existence is sustained by secrecy, and perhaps that is partly why the cultured person is so nervously concerned about respecting personal privacy.

After seeing his daughter off to school, Gurov went to the Slaviansky Bazaar. He took off his fur coat downstairs, went up, and knocked softly on the door. Anna Sergeyevna, dressed in his favorite gray dress, tired from the journey and the waiting, had been expecting him since the previous evening; she was pale, looked at him without smiling, and as soon as he entered, she pressed herself against his chest. It was as if they hadn’t seen each other for two years; their kiss was long, drawn-out.

“Well, how are you getting on there?” he asked. “Anything new?”

“Wait, I’ll tell you in a moment… I can’t.”

She couldn’t speak because she was crying. She turned away from him and pressed her handkerchief to her eyes.

“Well, let her cry, and I’ll sit down for now,” he thought, and sat down in an armchair.

Then he rang and ordered tea; and then, while he drank tea, she kept standing, turned towards the window… She cried from excitement, from the sorrowful awareness that their life had turned out so sadly; they only saw each other in secret, hiding from people like thieves! Wasn’t their life shattered?

“Come on, stop it!” he said.

It was obvious to him that their love would not end soon — when, no one knew. Anna Sergeyevna was growing more and more attached to him, adored him, and it would have been unthinkable to tell her that all this must someday end; besides, she wouldn’t have believed it.

He went up to her and took her by the shoulders to caress her, to joke, and at that moment he saw himself in the mirror.

His head was already beginning to turn gray. And it seemed strange to him that he had grown so old over the last few years, so ugly. The shoulders on which his hands rested were warm and trembling. He felt compassion for this life, still so warm and beautiful, but probably already close to beginning to fade and wither, like his own life. Why did she love him so? He had always seemed to women to be someone he was not, and they loved in him not himself, but the person created by their imagination, whom they had eagerly sought in their lives; and later, when they noticed their mistake, they still loved him. And not one of them had been happy with him. Time passed; he met people, got involved,He had parted ways, but never once loved; there had been everything under the sun, but never love.

And only now, when his hair had turned gray, he had fallen in love, properly, truly — for the first time in his life.

Anna Sergeyevna and he loved each other like very close, kindred people, like husband and wife, like tender friends; it seemed to them that fate itself had destined them for one another, and it was unclear why he was married and she was wed; and it was as if they were two migratory birds, a male and a female, who had been caught and forced to live in separate cages. They forgave each other for what they were ashamed of in their past, forgave everything in the present, and felt that this love of theirs had changed them both.

Before, in sad moments, he had comforted himself with all sorts of reasoning that came to mind, but now he had no time for reasoning; he felt deep compassion, and wanted to be sincere, tender...

"Stop, my dear," he said. "You've cried — that's enough... Now let's talk, let's think of something."

Then they counseled together for a long time, talked about how to rid themselves of the need to hide, to deceive, to live in different cities, to not see each other for long stretches. How to free themselves from these unbearable fetters?

"How? How?" he asked, clutching his head. "How?"

And it seemed that a little more — and a solution would be found, and then a new, wonderful life would begin; and it was clear to both that the end was still far, far away, and that the most complex and difficult part was only just beginning.

The Black Monk

I

Andrey Vasilyich Kovrin, a master's graduate, was exhausted and his nerves were shattered. He did not seek treatment, but somehow, casually, over a bottle of wine, he spoke with a friend who was a doctor, and the doctor advised him to spend the spring and summer in the country. Around the same time, a long letter arrived from Tanya Pesotskaya, asking him to come to Borisovka and stay. And he decided that he really ought to take a trip.

First — this was in April — he went to his own ancestral estate, Kovrinka, and lived there in seclusion for three weeks; then, after waiting for the roads to improve, he set off by horse to visit his former guardian and tutor, Pesotsky, a well-known horticulturist in Russia. From Kovrinka to Borisovka, where the Pesotskys lived, it was no more than seventy versts, and traveling along the soft spring road in a comfortable sprung carriage was a true pleasure.

Pesotsky's house was enormous, with columns and lions whose plaster was peeling, and a liveried footman at the entrance. An old park, gloomy and austere, laid out in the English style, stretched nearly a full verst from the house to the river, where it ended at a steep, sheer clay bank on which grew pines with exposed roots resembling shaggy paws; below, the water glimmered sullenly, sandpipers dashed about with plaintive cries, and the mood there was always such that you could sit down and write a ballad. But around the house itself, in the yard and in the orchard, which together with the nurseries occupied about thirty acres, it was cheerful and joyful even in bad weather. Such amazing roses, lilies, camellias, such tulips of all possible colors, ranging from bright white to black as soot, indeed such a wealth of flowers as at Pesotsky's, Kovrin had never seen anywhere else. Spring was only just beginning, and the true luxury of the flowerbeds was still hidden in the greenhouses, but even what was blooming along the avenues and here and there in the flowerbeds was enough to make one feel, while walking through the garden, as if in a kingdom of delicate colors, especially in the early hours, when dew sparkled on every petal.

What was the decorative part of the garden, and what Pesotsky himself contemptuously called trifles, had made a fairy-tale impression on Kovrin in his childhood. What whims, refined oddities, and mockeries of nature were there! There were espaliers of fruit trees, a pear tree shaped like a pyramidal poplar, spherical oaks and lindens, an umbrella of apple trees, arches, monograms, candelabras, and even the number 1862 made out of plums — a figure signifying the year when Pesotsky first took up horticulture.Here and there were beautiful, slender little trees with trunks as straight and sturdy as palm trees, and only by looking very closely could one recognize these saplings as gooseberries or currants. But what most enlivened the garden and gave it a lively look was the constant movement. From early morning until evening, near the trees, bushes, along the avenues and flowerbeds, people with wheelbarrows, hoes, and watering cans bustled about like ants.

Kovrin arrived at the Pesotskys' in the evening, around nine o'clock. He found Tanya and her father, Yegor Semyonich, in great agitation. The clear, starry sky and the thermometer foretold frost by morning, yet the gardener, Ivan Karlich, had gone to town, and there was no one to rely on. At supper, they talked only about the morning frost and it was decided that Tanya would not go to bed but would walk through the garden around midnight to check if everything was in order, while Yegor Semyonich would get up at three o'clock or even earlier.

Kovrin sat with Tanya the whole evening, and after midnight he went with her into the garden. It was cold. The yard already smelled strongly of smoke. In the large orchard, which was called the commercial garden and brought Yegor Semyonich several thousand rubles of net income annually, a thick, acrid black smoke spread along the ground and, enveloping the trees, saved those thousands from the frost. The trees stood here in a checkerboard pattern, their rows straight and regular, like ranks of soldiers, and this strict, pedantic regularity, along with the fact that all the trees were the same height and had completely identical crowns and trunks, made the picture monotonous and even boring. Kovrin and Tanya walked along the rows where fires of manure, straw, and various refuse were smoldering, and occasionally they met workers wandering in the smoke like shadows. Only the cherries, plums, and some varieties of apple trees were in bloom, but the entire garden was submerged in smoke, and only near the nursery did Kovrin breathe a full breath.

"I used to sneeze from this smoke here as a child," he said, shrugging his shoulders, "but to this day I don't understand how smoke can save from frost."

"Smoke replaces clouds when there are none..." Tanya replied.

"And what are clouds needed for?"

"In cloudy and overcast weather, there are no morning frosts."

"Is that so!"

He laughed and took her hand. Her broad, very serious, chilled face with thin black eyebrows, the raised collar of her coat which hindered her from moving her head freely, and she herself, slender, graceful, in her dress hitched up from the dew, touched him.

"Lord, she's already an adult!" he said. "When I left here last time, five years ago, you were still quite a child. You were so skinny, long-legged, bare-headed, wearing a short dress, and I used to tease you, calling you a heron... How time flies!"

"Yes, five years!" Tanya sighed. "Much water has flowed under the bridge since then. Tell me, Andryusha, honestly," she began to speak animatedly, looking him in the face, "have you grown away from us? But then, why am I asking? You are a man, you already live your own interesting life, you are a person of importance... Estrangement is so natural! But no matter what, Andryusha, I want you to consider us your own. We have the right to that."

"I consider you so, Tanya."

"Honest word?"

"Yes, honest word."

"You were surprised today that we have so many of your photographs. You know, my father adores you. Sometimes it seems to me that he loves you more than me. He is proud of you. You are a scientist, an extraordinary man, you have made a brilliant career for yourself, and he is sure that you turned out this way because he raised you. I don't stop him from thinking that. Let him."

Dawn was already breaking, and this was especially noticeable by the distinctness with which the clouds of smoke and the crowns of the trees began to stand out in the air. Nightingales were singing, and the call of quails came from the fields.

"However, it's time to sleep," said Tanya. "And it's cold." She took his arm. "Thank you, Andryusha, for coming. We have uninteresting acquaintances, and few of them at that. We have only the garden, the garden, the garden—and nothing more. Standard, half-standard," she laughed, "aport, rennet, borovinka, budding, grafting... All, all our life has gone into the garden, I never even dream of anything except apple and pear trees. Of course, it's good, useful, but sometimes one wants something else for a change."I remember when you used to come to us for the holidays or just like that, the house would somehow become fresher and brighter, as if the dust covers were being removed from the chandeliers and furniture. I was just a girl back then, yet I understood.

She spoke at length and with great feeling. For some reason, it suddenly occurred to him that over the summer he might become attached to this small, weak, talkative creature, get carried away and fall in love — in their situation, that was so possible and natural! This thought both touched and amused him; he leaned toward her dear, worried face and sang softly:

"Onegin, I will not conceal it,

I am madly in love with Tatyana..."

When they got home, Yegor Semyonich was already up. Kovrin didn't feel like sleeping, so he started talking with the old man and returned with him to the garden. Yegor Semyonich was tall, broad-shouldered, with a large belly, and suffered from shortness of breath, but he always walked so fast that it was hard to keep up with him. He had an extremely preoccupied look, always hurrying somewhere, with an expression as if everything would be lost if he were even one minute late!

"Here, brother, it's a story..." he began, stopping to catch his breath. "On the surface of the earth, as you can see, it's frosty, but raise a thermometer on a stick about two fathoms higher, and it's warm there... Why is that?"

"Honestly, I don't know," said Kovrin, and laughed.

"Hm... It's impossible to know everything, of course... No matter how vast the mind, you can't fit it all in there. You focus more on philosophy, don't you?"

"Yes. I'm reading psychology, but generally I'm occupied with philosophy."

"And it doesn't get boring?"

"On the contrary, this is what I live for."

"Well, God grant..." said Yegor Semyonich, pensively stroking his gray side-whiskers. "God grant... I'm very glad for you... glad, brother..."

But suddenly he listened, made a terrible face, ran off to the side, and soon disappeared behind the trees in clouds of smoke.

"Who tied a horse to the apple tree?" came his desperate, heart-rending cry. "What scoundrel and rascal dared to tie a horse to the apple tree? My God, my God! They've spoiled it, messed it up, defiled it, fouled it! The garden is ruined! The garden is lost! My God!"

When he returned to Kovrin, his face was exhausted and offended.

"Well, what can you do with these damned people?" he said in a tearful voice, throwing up his hands. "Styopka was hauling manure at night and tied the horse to the apple tree! The scoundrel wound the reins so tight that the bark is worn off in three places! Talk about that! I tell him, and he stands there like a dumb ox, just blinking his eyes! Hanging is too good for him!"

Having calmed down, he embraced Kovrin and kissed him on the cheek.

"Well, God grant... God grant..." he muttered. "I'm very glad you've come. Inexpressibly glad... Thank you."

Then, with the same quick gait and preoccupied face, he walked all around the garden and showed his former pupil all the greenhouses, hotbeds, cold frames, and his two apiaries, which he called the wonder of our century.

As they walked, the sun rose and brightly illuminated the garden. It became warm. Anticipating a clear, cheerful, long day, Kovrin remembered that this was only the beginning of May and that there was still a whole summer ahead, equally clear, cheerful, and long, and suddenly a joyful, youthful feeling stirred in his chest, the kind he had experienced as a child when he ran through this garden. And he himself embraced the old man and gently kissed him. Both moved, they went into the house and began to drink tea from antique porcelain cups, with cream and rich, buttered pretzels — and these little things again reminded Kovrin of his childhood and youth. The wonderful present and the awakening impressions of the past merged together; from them, his soul felt crowded, but good.

He waited until Tanya woke up, drank coffee with her, took a walk, then went to his room and sat down to work. He read attentively, took notes, and occasionally raised his eyes to look at the open windows or the fresh flowers, still wet with dew, standing in vases on the table, and then lowered his eyes to the book again, and it seemed to him that every little vein in him was trembling and playing with pleasure.

II

In the countryside, he continued to lead the same nervous and restless life as in the city.He read and wrote a lot, studied Italian, and when he went for walks, he gladly thought about how he would soon sit down to work again. He slept so little that everyone was surprised; if he accidentally dozed off for half an hour during the day, he wouldn't sleep at all that night, and after a sleepless night, as if nothing happened, he felt vigorous and cheerful.

He talked a lot, drank wine, and smoked expensive cigars. The Pesotskys were often visited, almost daily, by young ladies from the neighboring estates, who, together with Tanya, played the piano and sang; sometimes a young man, a neighbor who played the violin well, would come. Kovrin listened to the music and singing greedily and was exhausted by them, and the latter was expressed physically in his eyes growing heavy and his head drooping to the side.

One day after evening tea, he was sitting on the balcony reading. At that time, in the drawing-room, Tanya — a soprano, one of the young ladies — a contralto, and the young man on the violin were rehearsing the famous serenade by Braga. Kovrin listened to the words — they were in Russian — and could not understand their meaning. Finally, putting down his book and listening attentively, he understood: a girl, sick with imagination, heard some mysterious sounds in the garden at night, so beautiful and strange that she had to acknowledge them as a sacred harmony, incomprehensible to us mortals, and therefore it flies back to the heavens. Kovrin's eyes began to close. He stood up and, exhausted, walked through the drawing-room, then through the hall. When the singing stopped, he took Tanya by the arm and went out onto the balcony with her.

"I have been preoccupied with one legend since this morning," he said. "I don't remember whether I read it somewhere or heard it, but it's a strange legend, quite incoherent. To begin with, it is not particularly clear. A thousand years ago, a monk dressed in black was walking through a desert, somewhere in Syria or Arabia... A few miles from where he was walking, fishermen saw another black monk slowly moving across the surface of a lake. This second monk was a mirage. Now forget all the laws of optics, which the legend apparently does not acknowledge, and listen further. From the mirage came another mirage, and from that a third, so that the image of the black monk began to be transmitted endlessly from one layer of the atmosphere to another. He was seen in Africa, then in Spain, then in India, then in the Far North... Finally, he left the bounds of Earth's atmosphere and now wanders throughout the entire universe, never quite entering the conditions under which he could fade away. Perhaps he is now being seen somewhere on Mars or on some star of the Southern Cross. But, my dear, the very essence, the very crux of the legend is that exactly one thousand years after the monk walked through the desert, the mirage will again enter Earth's atmosphere and appear to people. And it is said that this thousand years is drawing to a close... According to the meaning of the legend, we should expect the black monk not today, but tomorrow."

"A strange mirage," said Tanya, who did not like the legend.

"But the most remarkable thing," Kovrin laughed, "is that I simply cannot remember where this legend came into my head. Did I read it somewhere? Hear it? Or perhaps I dreamed of the black monk? I swear to God, I don't remember. But the legend occupies my thoughts. I've been thinking about it all day today."

Releasing Tanya to her guests, he left the house and, deep in thought, walked around the flowerbeds. The sun was already setting. The flowers, because they had just been watered, gave off a damp, irritating scent. Singing started again in the house, and from a distance the violin sounded like a human voice. Kovrin, straining his mind to remember where he had heard or read the legend, headed slowly into the park and, unnoticed, reached the river.

Along a path that ran down the steep bank past exposed roots, he descended to the water, disturbing some sandpipers and scaring off two ducks. On the gloomy pines, the last rays of the setting sun were still glinting here and there, but on the surface of the river it was already true evening. Kovrin crossed to the other side over the planks. Before him now lay a wide field covered with young, not yet flowering rye. No human dwellings, no living soul in the distance, and it seemed that if one followed the path,will lead to that very same unknown, mysterious place.
