The Lady with the Dog

I

It was said that a new face had appeared on the embankment: a lady with a dog. Dmitry Dmitrich Gurov, who had been living in Yalta for two weeks and had grown accustomed to it, also began to take an interest in new faces. Sitting in Vernet's pavilion, he saw a young lady, a short blonde in a beret, walk along the embankment; a white spitz trotted behind her.

And then he met her in the city garden and in the public square several times a day. She walked alone, always in the same beret, with the white spitz; no one knew who she was, and they simply called her: the lady with the dog.

"If she is here without her husband and without acquaintances," Gurov reasoned, "then it wouldn't be amiss to get acquainted with her."

He was not yet forty, but he already had a twelve-year-old daughter and two gymnasium-student sons. He had married young, when he was still a second-year student, and now his wife seemed one and a half times older than him. She was a tall woman, with dark eyebrows, upright, serious, respectable, and, as she called herself, intellectual. She read a lot, did not write the hard sign in letters, called her husband not Dmitry but Dimitry, and he secretly considered her narrow-minded, unsophisticated, inelegant, feared her, and disliked being at home. He had started cheating on her long ago, cheated often, and probably for this reason almost always spoke ill of women, and when they were discussed in his presence, he called them:

"The inferior race!"

It seemed to him that he was sufficiently taught by bitter experience to call them whatever he pleased, but still, without the "inferior race," he could not have lived even two days. In the company of men, he was bored, ill at ease, with them he was taciturn, cold, but when he was among women, he felt free and knew what to talk about with them and how to behave; and even being silent with them was easy for him. In his appearance, in his character, in his whole nature, there was something attractive, elusive, that drew women to him, lured them; he knew about this, and some force also drew him to them.

Repeated experience, indeed bitter experience, had long taught him that every rapprochement, which at first so pleasantly diversifies life and seems like a sweet and easy adventure, among respectable people, especially among Muscovites, who are slow to act, indecisive, inevitably grows into a whole problem, extraordinarily complex, and the situation eventually becomes burdensome. But with every new encounter with an interesting woman, this experience somehow slipped from his memory, and he wanted to live, and everything seemed so simple and amusing.

And so, one evening, he was dining in the garden, and the lady in the beret approached unhurriedly to take the neighboring table. Her expression, gait, dress, hairstyle told him that she was from respectable society, married, in Yalta for the first time and alone, that she was bored here... There was much untruth in the stories about the impurity of local morals, he despised them and knew that such stories were mostly invented by people who would gladly sin themselves if they knew how, but when the lady sat down at the neighboring table three steps away from him, he remembered these stories about easy conquests, about trips to the mountains, and the tempting thought of a quick, fleeting affair, of a romance with an unknown woman whose name he did not know, suddenly took hold of him.

He gently beckoned the spitz to him and, when it approached, wagged his finger at it. The spitz growled. Gurov wagged his finger again.

The lady looked at him and immediately lowered her eyes.

"He doesn't bite," she said and blushed.

"May I give him a bone?" And when she nodded affirmatively, he asked amiably: "Have you been in Yalta long?"

"Five days."

"And I'm already finishing my second week here."

They were silent for a moment.

"Time passes quickly, and yet it's so boring here!" she said, not looking at him.

"It's only customary to say that it's boring here. An ordinary person lives somewhere in Belev or Zhizdra—and he's not bored, but he comes here: 'Oh, how boring! Oh, the dust!' You'd think he came from Granada."

She laughed.Then both continued eating in silence, like strangers; but after dinner they walked side by side—and a playful, light conversation began between free, contented people, to whom it mattered not where to go or what to talk about. They walked and talked about how strangely the sea was lit; the water was lilac-colored, so soft and warm, and a golden strip ran across it from the moon. They talked about how stuffy it was after a hot day. Gurov said that he was a Muscovite, a philologist by education, but worked in a bank; he had once prepared to sing in a private opera, but quit, and owned two houses in Moscow… And from her, he learned that she had grown up in Petersburg, but married in S., where she had lived for two years, that she would stay in Yalta for another month, and that her husband, who also wanted to rest, might come for her. She could not explain where her husband worked—in the provincial administration or in the provincial zemstvo board, and this amused her. And Gurov also learned that her name was Anna Sergeevna.

     Then, in his room, he thought about her, about how she would probably meet him tomorrow. So it must be. Going to bed, he remembered that she had been a schoolgirl so recently, studying, just like his daughter now, he remembered how much timidity, awkwardness there was in her laughter, in conversation with a stranger—it must have been the first time in her life she was alone, in such surroundings, when people followed her, and looked at her, and spoke to her with only one secret purpose, which she could not but guess. He remembered her thin, delicate neck, her beautiful, gray eyes.

     "There is something pathetic about her after all," he thought and began to fall asleep.



II



     A week passed after their acquaintance. It was a holiday. The rooms were stuffy, and dust whirled in the streets, blowing off hats. All day he wanted to drink, and Gurov often went into the pavilion and offered Anna Sergeevna either water with syrup or ice cream. There was nowhere to go.

     In the evening, when it had quieted down a bit, they went to the pier to watch the steamer arrive. There were many strollers on the pier; people had gathered to meet someone, holding bouquets. And here, two features of the elegant Yalta crowd were clearly striking: elderly ladies were dressed like young ones, and there were many generals.

     Due to the rough sea, the steamer arrived late, after the sun had already set, and turned for a long time before docking at the pier. Anna Sergeevna looked through her lorgnette at the steamer and the passengers, as if searching for acquaintances, and when she turned to Gurov, her eyes sparkled. She talked a lot, and her questions were abrupt, and she herself immediately forgot what she had asked; then she lost her lorgnette in the crowd.

     The elegant crowd dispersed, faces were no longer visible, the wind had completely died down, and Gurov and Anna Sergeevna stood, as if waiting to see if anyone else would disembark from the steamer. Anna Sergeevna was already silent and sniffing flowers, not looking at Gurov.

     "The weather got a little better towards evening," he said. "Where shall we go now? Shall we go somewhere?"

     She did not answer anything.

     Then he looked at her intently and suddenly embraced her and kissed her on the lips, and he was enveloped by the scent and moisture of flowers, and immediately he looked around timidly: had anyone seen?

     "Let's go to your place…" he said softly.

     And both walked quickly.

     In her room, it was stuffy, smelling of the perfume she had bought in a Japanese store. Gurov, looking at her now, thought: "What kind of encounters don't happen in life!" From the past, he retained memories of carefree, good-natured women, joyful from love, grateful to him for happiness, however short; and of those—like, for example, his wife—who loved without sincerity, with excessive talk, affectedly, hysterically, with an expression as if it were not love, not passion, but something more significant; and of those two or three, very beautiful, cold, in whom a predatory expression, a stubborn desire to take, to snatch more from life than it could give, suddenly flashed across their faces, and these were not in their first youth, capricious, unreasoning, domineering, not intelligent women, and when Gurov grew cold towards them,their beauty aroused hatred in him, and the lace on their lingerie then seemed to him like scales.

     But here it was all the same timidity, the awkwardness of inexperienced youth, an uncomfortable feeling; and there was an impression of bewilderment, as if someone had suddenly knocked at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, this "lady with the dog," reacted to what had happened in a particularly, very serious way, as if to her own fall—so it seemed, and this was strange and out of place. Her features drooped and withered, and long hair hung sadly by the sides of her face; she was lost in thought in a gloomy pose, like a sinner in an old painting.

     "This is not good," she said. "You yourself are the first to disrespect me now."

     There was a watermelon on the table in the room. Gurov cut himself a slice and began to eat slowly. At least half an hour passed in silence.

     Anna Sergeyevna was touching; she exuded the purity of a decent, naive woman who had lived little; the single candle burning on the table barely illuminated her face, but it was clear that her soul was troubled.

     "Why would I stop respecting you?" Gurov asked. "You don't know what you're saying."

     "May God forgive me!" she said, and her eyes filled with tears. "This is terrible."

     "You sound like you're making excuses."

     "What can I excuse myself with? I am a bad, base woman, I despise myself and don't think of excuses. I deceived not my husband, but myself. And not just now, but for a long time already. My husband may be an honest, good man, but he is a footman! I don't know what he does there, how he serves, I only know that he is a footman. When I married him, I was twenty years old, curiosity tormented me, I wanted something better; after all, there is," I told myself, "another life. I wanted to live! To live and to live... Curiosity burned me... you don't understand this, but, I swear to God, I could no longer control myself, something was happening to me, I couldn't be restrained, I told my husband I was ill and came here... And here I walked around as if in a fever, like a madwoman... and now I have become a vulgar, wretched woman whom anyone can despise."

     Gurov was already bored of listening; her naive tone, this repentance, so unexpected and out of place, irritated him; if not for the tears in her eyes, one might have thought she was joking or playing a role.

     "I don't understand," he said quietly, "what do you want?"

     She hid her face on his chest and pressed against him.

     "Believe me, believe me, I beg you..." she said. "I love an honest, pure life, and sin is hateful to me, I myself don't know what I'm doing. Simple people say: the evil one tempted me. And I can now say about myself that the evil one tempted me."

     "Enough, enough..." he mumbled.

     He looked into her still, frightened eyes, kissed her, spoke softly and tenderly, and she gradually calmed down, and cheerfulness returned to her; they both began to laugh.

     Then, when they went out, there was not a soul on the embankment, the city with its cypresses looked completely dead, but the sea was still roaring and beating against the shore; a single boat swayed on the waves, and a lantern flickered sleepily on it.

     They found a cabman and drove to Oreanda.

     "I just found out your surname downstairs in the hall: it says von Diederitz on the board," Gurov said. "Is your husband German?"

     "No, his grandfather, it seems, was German, but he himself is Orthodox."

     In Oreanda, they sat on a bench, not far from the church, looked down at the sea, and were silent. Yalta was barely visible through the morning mist, white clouds stood motionless on the mountain peaks. The foliage did not stir on the trees, cicadas chirped, and the monotonous, dull roar of the sea, coming from below, spoke of peace, of the eternal sleep that awaits us. It roared below when neither Yalta nor Oreanda was here yet, now it roars and will roar just as indifferently and dully when we are gone. And in this constancy, in the complete indifference to the life and death of each of us, lies, perhaps, the guarantee of our eternal salvation, the continuous movement of life on earth, continuous perfection. Sitting next to the young woman, who at dawn seemed so beautiful,calmed and enchanted by this fabulous setting—the sea, the mountains, the clouds, the wide sky—Gurov thought about how, in essence, if one truly considers it, everything in this world is beautiful, everything except what we ourselves think and do when we forget the higher purposes of being, our human dignity.

A man approached—probably a watchman—looked at them, and left. And this detail seemed so mysterious and beautiful too. One could see a steamboat arriving from Feodosia, illuminated by the morning dawn, already without lights.

"Dew on the grass," Anna Sergeevna said after a silence.

"Yes. Time to go home."

They returned to the town.

Then every noon they met on the embankment, had breakfast together, dined, walked, admired the sea. She complained that she slept poorly and that her heart beat anxiously, asking the same questions, agitated by jealousy or by the fear that he did not respect her enough. And often in the square or in the garden, when no one was near them, he would suddenly draw her to him and kiss her passionately. Complete idleness, these kisses in broad daylight, with glances and fear lest someone see them, the heat, the smell of the sea, and the constant flickering before their eyes of idle, well-dressed, well-fed people seemed to transform him; he told Anna Sergeevna how beautiful she was, how seductive, was impatiently passionate, did not leave her side for a step, while she often became thoughtful and kept asking him to confess that he did not respect her, did not love her at all, but only saw a vulgar woman in her. Almost every evening later they would go somewhere out of town, to Oreanda or to a waterfall; and the outing was successful, the impressions were invariably beautiful, majestic every time.

They waited for her husband to arrive. But a letter came from him, in which he informed her that his eyes were aching and begged his wife to return home as soon as possible. Anna Sergeevna hurried.

"It's good that I'm leaving," she told Gurov. "It's fate itself."

She traveled by horse, and he accompanied her. They rode all day. When she got into the express train carriage and when the second bell rang, she said:

"Let me look at you again... Look once more. Like this."

She did not cry, but was sad, as if ill, and her face trembled.

"I will think of you... remember you," she said. "God be with you, stay. Don't remember me ill. We are parting forever, it must be so, because we should not have met at all. Well, God be with you."

The train departed quickly, its lights soon disappeared, and a minute later the noise was no longer heard, as if everything had conspired on purpose to end this sweet oblivion, this madness, as quickly as possible. And, left alone on the platform and looking into the dark distance, Gurov listened to the chirping of crickets and the humming of telegraph wires with a feeling as if he had just woken up. And he thought that in his life there had been another escapade or adventure, and it too was already over, and now only a memory remained... He was touched, sad, and felt a slight remorse; for this young woman, whom he would never see again, had not been happy with him; he had been kind and cordial to her, but still, in his manner with her, in his tone and caresses, there was a faint shadow of light mockery, the somewhat coarse arrogance of a happy man who was also almost twice her age. All the time she called him kind, extraordinary, sublime; obviously, he did not seem to her what he really was, meaning he involuntarily deceived her...

Here at the station, it already smelled of autumn, the evening was cool.

"It's time for me to go north," Gurov thought, leaving the platform. "It's time!"

III

At home in Moscow, everything was already wintry, stoves were lit, and in the mornings, when the children gathered for gymnasium and drank tea, it was dark, and the nanny lit a light for a short while. Frosts had already begun. When the first snow falls, on the first day of sleigh riding, it is pleasant to see the white earth, the white roofs, one breathes softly, wonderfully, and at this time one remembers one's youth. The old lindens and birches, white with hoarfrost, have a benevolent expression, they are closer to the heart than cypresses and palms,and near them, one no longer wants to think of mountains and the sea.

Gurov was a Muscovite, he returned to Moscow on a fine, frosty day, and when he put on his fur coat and warm gloves and walked along Petrovka, and when he heard the ringing of bells on Saturday evening, the recent trip and the places he had been lost all their charm for him. Little by little, he immersed himself in Moscow life, already eagerly reading three newspapers a day and saying that he didn't read Moscow newspapers on principle. He was already drawn to restaurants, clubs, dinner parties, anniversaries, and it was already flattering to him that famous lawyers and artists visited him and that he played cards with a professor at the doctors' club. He could already eat a whole portion of solyanka from a pan…

A month or so would pass, and Anna Sergeevna, it seemed to him, would become hazy in his memory and only occasionally appear in dreams with a touching smile, as others had. But more than a month passed, deep winter set in, and everything in his memory was clear, as if he had parted with Anna Sergeevna only yesterday. And the memories grew stronger and stronger. Whether the voices of children doing their homework reached his study in the evening silence, whether he heard a romance or an organ in a restaurant, or whether a blizzard howled in the fireplace, suddenly everything would revive in his memory: what happened on the pier, and the early morning with fog on the mountains, and the steamboat from Feodosia, and the kisses. He walked around the room for a long time, remembering and smiling, and then the memories turned into dreams, and the past in his imagination mingled with what would be. Anna Sergeevna did not appear in his dreams, but followed him everywhere like a shadow, and watched him. Closing his eyes, he saw her as if alive, and she seemed more beautiful, younger, more tender than she was; and he himself seemed better than he had been then, in Yalta. In the evenings, she looked at him from the bookcase, from the fireplace, from the corner, he heard her breathing, the gentle rustle of her clothes. On the street, he followed women with his gaze, looking for someone who resembled her…

And a strong desire to share his memories with someone already tormented him. But at home, he couldn't talk about his love, and outside the home – there was no one. Not with the tenants, and not at the bank. And what was there to talk about? Did he really love her then? Was there anything beautiful, poetic, or instructive, or simply interesting in his relationship with Anna Sergeevna? And he had to speak vaguely about love, about women, and no one guessed what the matter was, and only his wife stirred her dark eyebrows and said:

"Dmitry, the role of a fop doesn't suit you at all."

One night, leaving the doctors' club with his partner, a civil servant, he couldn't resist and said:

"If only you knew what a charming woman I met in Yalta!"

The civil servant got into the sleigh and drove off, but suddenly turned around and called out:

"Dmitry Dmitrich!"

"What?"

"You were right earlier: the sturgeon smells a bit off!"

These words, so ordinary, somehow suddenly angered Gurov, seemed humiliating, unclean to him. What wild customs, what faces! What senseless nights, what uninteresting, unremarkable days! Furious card games, gluttony, drunkenness, constant talk about the same thing. Unnecessary affairs and conversations all about the same thing take up the best part of one's time, the best of one's strength, and in the end, what remains is some stunted, wingless life, some nonsense, and one cannot leave or escape, as if one is in a madhouse or in a penal company!

Gurov did not sleep all night and was indignant, and then spent the whole day with a headache. And in the following nights, he slept poorly, always sitting in bed and thinking or pacing from corner to corner. The children annoyed him, the bank annoyed him, he didn't want to go anywhere, or talk about anything.

In December, during the holidays, he prepared for a trip and told his wife that he was going to Petersburg to intercede for a young man – and left for S. Why? He himself didn't quite know. He wanted to see Anna Sergeevna and talk, to arrange a meeting, if possible.

He arrived in S. in the morning and took the best room in the hotel, where the entire floor was covered with gray soldier's cloth and there was an inkwell on the table, gray with dust, with a horseman on a horse,whose hand with a hat was raised, and whose head was broken off. The doorman gave him the necessary information: von Diederitz lives on Staro-Goncharnaya Street, in his own

     And Anna Sergeevna began to come to him in Moscow. Once every two or three months, she would leave S. and tell her husband that she was going to consult a professor about her female ailment—and her husband both believed and disbelieved her. Arriving in Moscow, she would stay at the "Slavyansky Bazar" and immediately send a man in a red cap to Gurov. Gurov would go to her, and no one in Moscow knew about it.

     Once, he was going to her in this manner on a winter morning (the messenger had been to him the previous evening and had not found him). His daughter was with him, whom he wanted to take to the gymnasium; it was on the way. Large, wet snow was falling.

     "It's three degrees above zero now, and yet it's snowing," Gurov said to his daughter. "But this warmth is only on the surface of the earth; in the upper layers of the atmosphere, the temperature is quite different."

     "Papa, why isn't there thunder in winter?"

     He explained that too. He spoke and thought about how he was going to a rendezvous and not a single living soul knew about it and, probably, never would. He had two lives: one open, which everyone who needed to saw and knew, full of conventional truth and conventional deceit, completely similar to the lives of his acquaintances and friends, and another—which flowed secretly. And by some strange coincidence, perhaps accidental, everything that was important, interesting, necessary to him, in which he was sincere and did not deceive himself, which constituted the core of his life, happened secretly from others; everything that was his lie, his shell, into which he hid to conceal the truth, such as, for example, his service in the bank, arguments in the club, his "lower race," going with his wife to anniversaries—all this was open. And by himself, he judged others, did not believe what he saw, and always assumed that every person, under the veil of secrecy, as under the cover of night, lived their true, most interesting life. Every personal existence rests on secrecy, and perhaps partly for this reason, a cultured person so nervously strives for personal secrecy to be respected.

     After taking his daughter to the gymnasium, Gurov went to the "Slavyansky Bazar." He took off his fur coat downstairs, went upstairs, and quietly knocked on the door. Anna Sergeevna, dressed in his favorite gray dress, tired from the journey and waiting, had been expecting him since yesterday evening; she was pale, looked at him without smiling, and as soon as he entered, she already clung to his chest. It was as if they hadn't seen each other for two years; their kiss was long, prolonged.

     "Well, how are you living there?" he asked. "What's new?"

     "Wait, I'll tell you now... I can't."

     She couldn't speak because she was crying. She turned away from him and pressed a handkerchief to her eyes.

     "Well, let her cry, and I'll sit for a while," he thought and sat in an armchair.

     Then he rang and asked for tea to be brought to him; and then, while he was drinking tea, she still stood, turned away to the window... She was crying from agitation, from the sorrowful realization that their life had turned out so sadly; they only met secretly, hiding from people, like thieves! Wasn't their life broken?

     "Well, stop it!" he said.

     It was obvious to him that this love of theirs would not end soon, no one knew when. Anna Sergeevna was becoming more and more attached to him, adored him, and it would be unthinkable to tell her that all this must come to an end someday; besides, she wouldn't have believed it.

     He went up to her and took her by the shoulders to caress her, to joke, and at that moment he saw himself in the mirror.

     His head was already beginning to turn gray. And it seemed strange to him that he had aged so much in recent years, had become so unattractive. The shoulders on which his hands rested were warm and trembled. He felt compassion for this life, still so warm and beautiful, but probably already close to beginning to fade and wither, like his own life. Why did she love him so much? He always seemed to women to be someone other than he was, and they loved in him not himself, but a man created by their imagination and whom they eagerly sought in their lives; and then, when they noticed their mistake, they still loved him. And none of them was happy with him. Time passed, he met people, became close,parted, but never loved; there was everything but love.

     And only now, when his hair had turned gray, did he love properly, truly—for the first time in his life.

     Anna Sergeevna and he loved each other like very close, dear people, like husband and wife, like tender friends; it seemed to them that fate itself had destined them for each other, and it was incomprehensible why he was married and she was married; and it was as if they were two migratory birds, a male and a female, who had been caught and forced to live in separate cages. They forgave each other what they were ashamed of in their past, forgave everything in the present, and felt that this love of theirs had changed them both.

     Before, in sad moments, he would comfort himself with all sorts of reasoning that came into his head, but now he had no time for reasoning, he felt deep compassion, he wanted to be sincere, tender…

     "Stop it, my dear," he said. "You've cried enough… Now let's talk, let's figure something out."

     Then they consulted for a long time, talking about how to free themselves from the need to hide, to deceive, to live in different cities, not to see each other for long periods. How to break free from these unbearable bonds?

     "How? How?" he asked, clutching his head. "How?"

     And it seemed that a little more—and a solution would be found, and then a new, beautiful life would begin; and it was clear to both of them that the end was still far, far away and that the most complex and difficult part was only just beginning.





The Black Monk



I



     Andrey Vasilich Kovrin, a master, was tired and had frayed his nerves. He wasn't undergoing treatment, but he casually mentioned it to a doctor friend over a bottle of wine, and the doctor advised him to spend the spring and summer in the countryside. Coincidentally, a long letter arrived from Tanya Pesotskaya, asking him to come to Borisovka and stay. And he decided that he really needed to take a trip.

     First—this was in April—he went to his ancestral estate, Kovrinka, and lived there in solitude for three weeks; then, waiting for good roads, he set off by horse to his former guardian and tutor, Pesotsky, a renowned gardener in Russia. From Kovrinka to Borisovka, where the Pesotskys lived, was considered no more than seventy versts, and traveling along the soft spring road in a comfortable spring carriage was a true delight.

     Pesotsky's house was enormous, with columns, with lions whose stucco was peeling, and with a footman in livery at the entrance. The ancient park, gloomy and strict, laid out in the English style, stretched for almost a whole verst from the house to the river and ended here in a steep, precipitous clay bank, on which grew pines with exposed roots resembling shaggy paws; below, the water gleamed unsociably, sandpipers darted about with plaintive squeaks, and there was always such a mood here that one could just sit down and write a ballad. But near the house itself, in the courtyard and in the orchard, which, together with the nurseries, occupied about thirty desyatins, it was cheerful and lively even in bad weather. Kovrin had never seen such amazing roses, lilies, camellias, such tulips of all possible colors, from bright white to sooty black, in general, such a wealth of flowers as at Pesotsky's. Spring was still only at its beginning, and the true luxury of the flowerbeds was still hidden in the greenhouses, but even what was blooming along the alleys and here and there in the flowerbeds was enough to feel oneself in a kingdom of delicate colors while walking through the garden, especially in the early hours, when dew sparkled on every petal.

     What was the decorative part of the garden and what Pesotsky himself contemptuously called trifles, had made a fairytale impression on Kovrin in his childhood. What whims, exquisite deformities, and mockery of nature were not there! There were espaliers of fruit trees, a pear shaped like a pyramidal poplar, spherical oaks and lindens, an apple tree umbrella, arches, monograms, candelabras, and even 1862 made of plums—a number signifying the year Pesotsky first took up gardening.There were also beautiful, slender trees with straight, strong trunks like palms, and only by looking closely could one recognize them as gooseberries or currants. But what was most amusing in the garden and gave it a lively appearance was the constant movement. From early morning until evening, around the trees, bushes, on the alleys and flowerbeds, people scurried like ants with wheelbarrows, hoes, watering cans…

     Kovrin arrived at the Pesotskys' in the evening, around ten o'clock. He found Tanya and her father, Yegor Semyonych, in great distress. The clear, starry sky and the thermometer predicted frost by morning, and meanwhile, the gardener Ivan Karlych had gone to the city, and there was no one to rely on. At dinner, they only talked about the morning frost, and it was decided that Tanya would not go to bed and would walk through the garden around one o'clock to check if everything was in order, and Yegor Semyonych would get up at three o'clock or even earlier.

     Kovrin spent the entire evening with Tanya and after midnight went with her into the garden. It was cold. In the yard, there was already a strong smell of burning. In the large fruit orchard, which was called commercial and brought Yegor Semyonych several thousand net income annually, black, thick, acrid smoke spread along the ground and, enveloping the trees, saved these thousands from the frost. The trees here stood in a checkerboard pattern, their rows were straight and regular, like ranks of soldiers, and this strict, pedantic regularity, and the fact that all the trees were of the same height and had completely identical crowns and trunks, made the picture monotonous and even boring. Kovrin and Tanya walked through the rows where bonfires of manure, straw, and all sorts of refuse smoldered, and occasionally they met workers who wandered in the smoke like shadows. Only cherries, plums, and some varieties of apple trees were blooming, but the entire garden was immersed in smoke, and only near the nurseries did Kovrin breathe deeply.

     "I used to sneeze from the smoke here even as a child," he said, shrugging his shoulders, "but I still don't understand how smoke can save from frost."

     "Smoke replaces clouds when there are none…" Tanya replied.

     "And what are clouds for?"

     "On overcast and cloudy days, there are no morning frosts."

     "Is that so!"

     He laughed and took her hand. Her broad, very serious, chilled face with thin black eyebrows, the raised collar of her coat that prevented her from moving her head freely, and her whole self, slender, graceful, in a dress gathered from the dew, touched him.

     "My God, she's already grown up!" he said. "When I left here last time, five years ago, you were still a child. You were so thin, long-legged, disheveled, wore a short dress, and I teased you as a heron… What time does!"

     "Yes, five years!" Tanya sighed. "A lot of water has flowed under the bridge since then. Tell me, Andryusha, honestly," she began animatedly, looking him in the face, "have you grown unaccustomed to us? Anyway, why am I asking? You are a man, you live your own interesting life, you are a person of importance… Alienation is so natural! But no matter what, Andryusha, I want you to consider us your own. We have the right to that."

     "I do, Tanya."

     "Honest word?"

     "Yes, honest word."

     "You were surprised today that we have so many of your photographs. You know, my father adores you. Sometimes it seems to me that he loves you more than me. He is proud of you. You are a scholar, an extraordinary person, you have made a brilliant career, and he is sure that you turned out that way because he raised you. I don't stop him from thinking that. Let him."

     Dawn was already breaking, and this was especially noticeable by the clarity with which the plumes of smoke and the crowns of the trees began to stand out in the air. Nightingales sang, and the cry of quails came from the fields.

     "Well, it's time to sleep," Tanya said. "And it's cold." She took his arm. "Thank you, Andryusha, for coming. Our acquaintances are uninteresting, and there are few of them. We only have the garden, the garden, the garden, and nothing else. Standard, semi-standard," she laughed, "aport, ranet, borovinka, budding, grafting… Our whole life, our entire life has gone into the garden, I don't even dream of anything but apple and pear trees. Of course, it's good, useful, but sometimes one wants something else for variety."I remember, when you used to come to visit us for holidays or just like that, the house would become somehow fresher and brighter, as if the covers were removed from the chandelier and furniture. I was a girl then, and yet I understood.

     She spoke for a long time and with great feeling. It suddenly occurred to him for some reason that during the summer he might become attached to this small, weak, verbose creature, become infatuated and fall in love – in their situation, it was so possible and natural! This thought touched and amused him; he leaned towards the sweet, worried face and quietly sang:

     Onegin, I will not conceal,

     I madly love Tatyana…

     When they returned home, Yegor Semyonych had already gotten up. Kovrin didn't feel like sleeping, he started talking with the old man and went back to the garden with him. Yegor Semyonych was tall, broad-shouldered, with a large belly and suffered from shortness of breath, but he always walked so fast that it was hard to keep up with him. He had an extremely worried look, always rushing somewhere and with such an expression, as if if he were even a minute late, everything would be lost!

     "Well, brother, here's a story…" he began, stopping to catch his breath. "On the surface of the earth, as you see, it's freezing, but if you raise a thermometer on a stick two sazhens above the ground, it's warm there… Why is that?"

     "Honestly, I don't know," said Kovrin and laughed.

     "Hmm… You can't know everything, of course… No matter how vast the mind, you can't fit everything in there. You're mostly into philosophy, aren't you?"

     "Yes. I read psychology, and in general, I study philosophy."

     "And it doesn't bore you?"

     "On the contrary, it's the only thing I live for."

     "Well, God grant it…" Yegor Semyonych muttered, thoughtfully stroking his gray sideburns. "God grant it… I'm very happy for you… happy, brother…"

     But suddenly he listened and, making a terrible face, ran off to the side and soon disappeared behind the trees, in clouds of smoke.

     "Who tied the horse to the apple tree?" his desperate, soul-rending cry was heard. "What scoundrel and villain dared to tie the horse to the apple tree? My God, my God! They've ruined it, frozen it, defiled it, messed it up! The garden is lost! The garden is ruined! My God!"

     When he returned to Kovrin, his face was exhausted, offended.

     "Well, what can you do with these accursed people?" he said in a weeping voice, throwing up his hands. "Styopka was hauling manure at night and tied the horse to the apple tree! The scoundrel tied the reins so tightly that the bark was rubbed off in three places. How about that! I tell him, and he's as dull as a blockhead and just blinks his eyes! Hanging is too good for him!"

     Calming down, he embraced Kovrin and kissed him on the cheek.

     "Well, God grant it… God grant it…" he mumbled. "I'm very glad you came. Inexpressibly glad… Thank you."

     Then, with the same quick gait and worried face, he walked around the entire garden and showed his former pupil all the conservatories, greenhouses, ground sheds, and his two apiaries, which he called the wonder of our century.

     While they walked, the sun rose and brightly illuminated the garden. It became warm. Anticipating a clear, cheerful, long day, Kovrin remembered that it was only the beginning of May and that a whole summer, just as clear, cheerful, and long, was still ahead, and suddenly a joyful young feeling stirred in his chest, the kind he had experienced in childhood when he ran around this garden. And he himself embraced the old man and gently kissed him. Both, moved, went into the house and began to drink tea from old porcelain cups, with cream, with hearty, rich pretzels – and these little things again reminded Kovrin of his childhood and youth. The beautiful present and the impressions of the past awakening within him merged together; his soul felt crowded, but good.

     He waited until Tanya woke up, and together with her, he drank coffee, took a walk, then went to his room and sat down to work. He read attentively, made notes, and occasionally looked up to gaze at the open windows or at the fresh flowers, still wet with dew, standing in vases on the table, and then lowered his eyes back to the book, and it seemed to him that every fiber in him trembled and played with pleasure.



II



     In the village, he continued to lead the same nervous and restless life as in the city.He read and wrote a lot, studied Italian, and when he walked, he enjoyed thinking that he would soon get back to work. He slept so little that everyone was surprised; if he accidentally fell asleep for half an hour during the day, he wouldn't sleep all night, and after a sleepless night, he would feel cheerful and lively as if nothing had happened.

     He talked a lot, drank wine, and smoked expensive cigars. Young ladies, neighbors, often came to the Pesotskys, almost every day, and played the piano and sang with Tanya; sometimes a young man, a neighbor, who played the violin well, would come. Kovrin listened to the music and singing with eagerness and was exhausted by them, and the latter manifested physically in his eyes getting heavy and his head drooping to the side.

     One day after evening tea, he was sitting on the balcony and reading. In the living room at that time, Tanya – soprano, one of the young ladies – contralto, and the young man on the violin were rehearsing a well-known Braga serenade. Kovrin listened to the words – they were Russian – and could not understand their meaning. Finally, putting down the book and listening carefully, he understood: a girl, sick with imagination, heard some mysterious sounds in the garden at night, so beautiful and strange that she had to recognize them as a sacred harmony, which is incomprehensible to us mortals and therefore flies back to heaven. Kovrin's eyes began to close. He stood up and, exhausted, walked through the living room, then through the hall. When the singing stopped, he took Tanya by the arm and went out onto the balcony with her.

     "A legend has been occupying me since this morning," he said. "I don't remember if I read it somewhere or heard it, but it's a strange legend, inconsistent with anything. To begin with, it's not clear. A thousand years ago, a monk dressed in black walked through the desert, somewhere in Syria or Arabia... A few miles from where he was walking, fishermen saw another black monk slowly moving across the surface of a lake. This second monk was a mirage. Now forget all the laws of optics, which the legend, it seems, does not recognize, and listen further. From the mirage came another mirage, then from the other a third, so that the image of the black monk began to be endlessly transmitted from one layer of the atmosphere to another. He was seen sometimes in Africa, sometimes in Spain, sometimes in India, sometimes in the Far North... Finally, he left the Earth's atmosphere and now wanders throughout the universe, never encountering the conditions under which he could fade. Perhaps he is now seen somewhere on Mars or on some star of the Southern Cross. But, my dear, the very essence, the very crux of the legend is that exactly a thousand years after the monk walked through the desert, the mirage will again enter the Earth's atmosphere and appear to people. And it seems that this thousand years is already coming to an end... According to the legend, we should expect the black monk not today – but tomorrow."

     "A strange mirage," said Tanya, who did not like the legend.

     "But the most amazing thing," Kovrin laughed, "is that I can't remember how this legend got into my head. Did I read it somewhere? Heard it? Or, perhaps, did I dream of the black monk? I swear to God, I don't remember. But the legend fascinates me. I've been thinking about it all day today."

     Dismissing Tanya to the guests, he left the house and thoughtfully walked around the flowerbeds. The sun was already setting. The flowers, having just been watered, emitted a damp, irritating smell. Singing started again in the house, and from a distance, the violin sounded like a human voice. Kovrin, straining his mind to remember where he had heard or read the legend, slowly headed to the park and imperceptibly reached the river.

     Along the path that ran along the steep bank past exposed roots, he descended to the water, disturbed the sandpipers there, and startled two ducks. On the gloomy pines, the last rays of the setting sun still reflected here and there, but on the surface of the river, it was already true evening. Kovrin crossed to the other side over the footbridges. Before him now lay a wide field covered with young, not yet blooming rye. No human dwelling, no living soul in the distance, and it seems that the path, if one were to follow it,will lead to that unknown, mysterious place,
– Well, you see... – Kovrin mumbled. – So, there's truth in the legend.

     Without trying to explain the strange phenomenon, content with merely having seen not only the black attire but even the monk's face and eyes so closely and clearly, pleasantly agitated, he returned home.

     People were calmly walking in the park and garden, music was playing in the house – so, he was the only one who had seen the monk. He strongly wanted to tell Tanya and Yegor Semyonych everything, but he realized they would surely consider his words delirium, and it would frighten them; it was better to keep silent. He laughed loudly, sang, danced the mazurka, he was cheerful, and everyone, guests and Tanya, found that today his face was somehow special, radiant, inspired, and that he was very interesting.

III

     After dinner, when the guests had left, he went to his room and lay down on the sofa: he wanted to think about the monk. But a minute later, Tanya entered.

     – Here, Andryusha, read father's articles, – she said, handing him a stack of brochures and reprints. – Excellent articles. He writes very well.

     – Oh, "very well"! – Yegor Semyonych said, entering after her and laughing unnaturally; he was embarrassed. – Don't listen, please, don't read! However, if you want to fall asleep, then, perhaps, read: it's an excellent sleeping aid.

     – In my opinion, they are magnificent articles, – Tanya said with deep conviction. – You read them, Andryusha, and persuade Papa to write more often. He could write a complete course on horticulture.

     Yegor Semyonych laughed tensely, blushed, and began to utter phrases that embarrassed authors usually say. Finally, he started to give in.

     – In that case, first read Gosha's article and these Russian articles, – he mumbled, fumbling through the brochures with trembling hands, – otherwise, it will be incomprehensible to you. Before reading my objections, you need to know what I am objecting to. However, it's nonsense... boring. And it's time to sleep, I think.

     Tanya left. Yegor Semyonych sat down next to Kovrin on the sofa and sighed deeply.

     – Yes, my dear brother... – he began after a moment of silence. – That's how it is, my dearest master. Here I am, writing articles, participating in exhibitions, and receiving medals... At Pesotsky's, they say, the apples are as big as a head, and Pesotsky, they say, made a fortune from his garden. In short, Kochubey is rich and famous. But the question is: what is all this for? The garden, indeed, is beautiful, exemplary... It's not a garden, but an entire institution of great national importance, because it is, so to speak, a step into a new era of Russian agriculture and Russian industry. But to what end? What is the goal?

     – The work speaks for itself.

     – I don't mean it in that sense. I want to ask: what will happen to the garden when I die? In the state you see it now, it won't last a month without me. The whole secret of success is not that the garden is large and there are many workers, but that I love the work – do you understand? – I love it, perhaps, more than myself. Look at me: I do everything myself. I work from morning till night. I do all the grafting myself, the pruning myself, the planting myself, everything myself. When people help me, I get jealous and irritated to the point of rudeness. The whole secret is in love, that is, in the keen eye of the owner, and in the owner's hands, and in that feeling when you go somewhere to visit for an hour, you sit there, but your heart is not at ease, you're not yourself: you're afraid something might happen in the garden. And when I die, who will look after it? Who will work? The gardener? The workers? Yes? So this is what I'll tell you, my dear friend: the first enemy in our business is not the hare, not the cockchafer, and not the frost, but a stranger.

     – And Tanya? – Kovrin asked, laughing. – She can't be more harmful than a hare. She loves and understands the business.

     – Yes, she loves and understands. If after my death she inherits the garden and becomes the owner, then, of course, one couldn't wish for better. Well, what if, God forbid, she gets married? – Yegor Semyonych whispered and looked at Kovrin in fright. – That's exactly it! She'll get married, have children, and then there'll be no time to think about the garden.

     – What I'm mainly afraid of is: she'll marry some young man, and he'll be greedy and lease the garden to traders,and everything will go to hell in the first year! In our business, women are a scourge!

     Yegor Semyonych sighed and was silent for a moment.

     – Maybe it's selfishness, but I'll be frank: I don't want Tanya to get married. I'm afraid! There's a fop with a violin who comes here and scrapes away; I know Tanya won't marry him, I know it well, but I can't stand to see him! In general, brother, I'm quite a strange fellow. I confess.

     Yegor Semyonych stood up and nervously paced the room, and it was clear that he wanted to say something very important, but hesitated.

     – I love you dearly and will speak frankly with you, – he finally decided, putting his hands in his pockets. – I approach some delicate matters simply and say directly what I think, and I can't stand so-called secret thoughts. I'll say it directly: you are the only person to whom I wouldn't be afraid to give my daughter. You are an intelligent man, with a heart, and you wouldn't let my beloved work perish. And the main reason is – I love you like a son… and I'm proud of you. If you and Tanya somehow developed a romance, then – what then? I would be very happy and even delighted. I say this directly, without affectation, as an honest man.

     Kovrin laughed. Yegor Semyonovich opened the door to leave and stopped on the threshold.

     – If you and Tanya had a son, I would make a gardener out of him, – he said after a moment's thought. – However, this is an empty dream… Good night.

     Left alone, Kovrin made himself comfortable and began to read the articles. One had the title: "On Intermediate Culture," another: "A Few Words Regarding Mr. Z.'s Note on Trenching Soil for a New Garden," and a third: "More on Budding with a Dormant Eye" – and all in that vein. But what an agitated, uneven tone, what a nervous, almost morbid fervor! Here is an article, it seems, with the most peaceful title and indifferent content: it talks about the Russian Antonovka apple tree. But Yegor Semyonych begins it with "audiatur altera pars" 1 and ends with "sapienti sat" 2, and between these sayings is a whole fountain of various venomous words addressed to "the learned ignorance of our patented gentlemen gardeners, who observe nature from the height of their pulpits," or to Mr. Goshe, "whose success is created by laymen and dilettantes," and right there, an inappropriately strained and insincere regret that the peasants who steal fruit and break trees in the process can no longer be flogged.

     "A beautiful, sweet, healthy business, but even here there are passions and war," Kovrin thought. "It must be that everywhere and in all fields, people of ideas are nervous and characterized by heightened sensitivity. Probably, that's how it should be."

     He remembered Tanya, who liked Yegor Semyonych's articles so much. Short, pale, thin, so that her collarbones were visible; eyes wide open, dark, intelligent, always peering somewhere and searching for something; her walk, like her father's, small, hurried. She talks a lot, loves to argue, and with every even insignificant phrase, she accompanies it with expressive facial expressions and gestures. She must be extremely nervous.

     Kovrin started to read further, but understood nothing and gave up. The pleasant excitement, the very same with which he had just danced the mazurka and listened to music, now tormented him and evoked many thoughts. He got up and began to walk around the room, thinking about the black monk. It occurred to him that if only he had seen this strange, supernatural monk, then he must be ill and had already reached hallucinations. This thought frightened him, but not for long.

     "But I feel good, and I'm not harming anyone; therefore, there's nothing bad in my hallucinations," he thought, and he felt good again.

     He sat on the sofa and clasped his head in his hands, restraining the incomprehensible joy that filled his whole being, then walked around again and sat down to work. But the thoughts he gleaned from the book did not satisfy him. He wanted something gigantic, immense, striking. Towards morning, he undressed and reluctantly got into bed: he had to sleep!

     When Yegor Semyonych's footsteps were heard as he left for the garden, Kovrin rang and ordered the footman to bring wine. He drank several glasses of Lafite with pleasure,then covered himself completely; his consciousness blurred, and he fell asleep.

IV

Yegor Semyonych and Tanya often quarreled and said unpleasant things to each other.

One morning, they had an argument about something. Tanya cried and went to her room. She didn't come out for lunch or tea. Yegor Semyonych at first walked around important, puffed up, as if wanting to make it clear that for him, the interests of justice and order were above everything else in the world, but soon he couldn't maintain his composure and lost heart. He sadly wandered through the park and kept sighing, "Oh, my God, my God!" and didn't eat a single crumb at lunch. Finally, feeling guilty and tormented by his conscience, he knocked on the locked door and called timidly:

"Tanya! Tanya?"

And in response, from behind the door, he heard a faint voice, exhausted from tears and at the same time resolute:

"Leave me alone, please."

The hosts' distress affected the entire house, even the people working in the garden. Kovrin was engrossed in his interesting work, but in the end, he too became bored and uncomfortable. To somehow dispel the general bad mood, he decided to intervene and knocked on Tanya's door before evening. He was let in.

"Oh, oh, how shameful!" he began playfully, looking with surprise at Tanya's tear-stained, red-spotted, sorrowful face. "Is it really that serious? Oh, oh!"

"But if you only knew how he torments me!" she said, and hot, abundant tears gushed from her large eyes. "He has tormented me!" she continued, wringing her hands. "I didn't say anything to him... nothing... I only said that there's no need to keep... extra workers, if... if one can, whenever needed, have day laborers. After all... after all, the workers haven't done anything for a whole week... I... I only said that, and he started shouting and said... many offensive, deeply insulting things to me. Why?"

"Enough, enough," Kovrin said, adjusting her hairstyle. "You've quarreled, you've cried, and that's enough. You can't stay angry for long, it's not good... especially since he loves you infinitely."

"He... he ruined my whole life," Tanya continued, sobbing. "All I hear are insults and... and grievances. He considers me superfluous in his house. So? He's right. I'll leave tomorrow, I'll become a telegraph operator... Let him..."

"Now, now, now... Don't cry, Tanya. Don't, my dear... You are both hot-tempered, irritable, and both are to blame. Come, I'll reconcile you."

Kovrin spoke gently and persuasively, and she continued to cry, her shoulders shaking and her hands clasped, as if a terrible misfortune had indeed befallen her. He felt even sorrier for her because her grief was not serious, yet she suffered deeply. What trifles were enough to make this creature unhappy for a whole day, and perhaps for her whole life! While comforting Tanya, Kovrin thought that, apart from this girl and her father, in the whole world, you wouldn't find people who loved him as their own, as a relative; if it weren't for these two people, then perhaps he, who had lost his father and mother in early childhood, would never have known until his death what sincere affection and that naive, unreasoning love, which is only felt for very close, blood relatives, truly were. And he felt that his semi-sick, frayed nerves responded to the nerves of this crying, trembling girl, like iron to a magnet. He could never have loved a healthy, strong, rosy-cheeked woman, but the pale, weak, unhappy Tanya pleased him.

And he willingly stroked her hair and shoulders, squeezed her hands, and wiped away her tears... Finally, she stopped crying. She complained for a long time about her father and her difficult, unbearable life in this house, begging Kovrin to understand her situation; then she gradually began to smile and sigh that God had given her such a bad character, and in the end, laughing loudly, called herself a fool and ran out of the room.

When Kovrin went into the garden a little later, Yegor Semyonych and Tanya were already walking side by side along the alley as if nothing had happened, and both were eating rye bread with salt, as they were both hungry.

V

Pleased that his role as peacemaker had been so successful, Kovrin went to the park. Sitting on a bench and reflecting,he heard the clatter of carriages and women's laughter – guests had arrived. When the evening shadows began to fall in the garden, the faint sounds of a violin and singing voices were heard, and this reminded him of the black monk. Somewhere, in what country or on what planet was this optical incongruity now wandering?

     No sooner had he recalled the legend and pictured in his imagination that dark apparition he had seen in the rye field, than from behind a pine tree, directly opposite, a man of medium height, with an uncovered gray head, dressed entirely in dark clothes and barefoot, resembling a beggar, emerged silently, without the slightest rustle, and on his pale, as if dead, face, black eyebrows stood out sharply. Nodding his head amiably, this beggar or wanderer noiselessly approached the bench and sat down, and Kovrin recognized in him the black monk. For a minute, both looked at each other – Kovrin with astonishment, and the monk kindly and, as before, a little slyly, with a knowing expression.

     "But you are a mirage," Kovrin said. "Why are you here and sitting in one place? That doesn't fit the legend."

     "It doesn't matter," the monk replied after a pause, in a quiet voice, turning his face towards him. "The legend, the mirage, and I – all of it is a product of your excited imagination. I am a phantom."

     "So, you don't exist?" Kovrin asked.

     "Think what you like," the monk said and smiled faintly. "I exist in your imagination, and your imagination is part of nature, which means I exist in nature too."

     "You have a very old, intelligent, and extremely expressive face, as if you truly have lived for more than a thousand years," Kovrin said. "I didn't know my imagination was capable of creating such phenomena. But why are you looking at me with such rapture? Do you like me?"

     "Yes. You are one of the few who are rightly called God's chosen. You serve eternal truth. Your thoughts, intentions, your amazing science, and your entire life bear a divine, heavenly seal, for they are dedicated to what is rational and beautiful, that is, to what is eternal."

     "You said: eternal truth... But is eternal truth accessible and necessary to people if there is no eternal life?"

     "Eternal life exists," the monk said.

     "Do you believe in the immortality of people?"

     "Yes, of course. A great, brilliant future awaits you, people. And the more people like you there are on earth, the sooner this future will come to pass. Without you, servants of the highest principle, living consciously and freely, humanity would be insignificant; developing in a natural order, it would still wait a long time for the end of its earthly history. But you will lead it into the kingdom of eternal truth several thousand years earlier – and therein lies your great merit. You embody the blessing of God that has rested upon people."

     "And what is the purpose of eternal life?" Kovrin asked.

     "Like all life – enjoyment. True enjoyment is in knowledge, and eternal life will provide countless and inexhaustible sources for knowledge, and in this sense it is said: in My Father's house are many mansions."

     "If only you knew how pleasant it is to listen to you!" Kovrin said, rubbing his hands with pleasure.

     "Very glad."

     "But I know: when you leave, the question of your essence will trouble me. You are a phantom, a hallucination. Does that mean I am mentally ill, abnormal?"

     "Even if so. Why be troubled? You are ill because you worked beyond your strength and became tired, and this means that you have sacrificed your health for an idea, and the time is near when you will give your very life to it. What could be better? This is what all gifted, noble natures strive for."

     "If I know that I am mentally ill, can I trust myself?"

     "And why do you know that brilliant people, whom the whole world trusts, also haven't seen phantoms? Scientists now say that genius is akin to madness. My friend, only ordinary, herd-like people are healthy and normal. Considerations about the nervous age, overwork, degeneration, etc., can seriously disturb only those who see the purpose of life in the present, that is, herd-like people."

     "The Romans said: mens sana in corpore sano 3.

     "Not everything is true,"what the Romans or Greeks said. An elevated mood, excitement, ecstasy – all that distinguishes prophets, poets, martyrs for an idea from ordinary people, is contrary to the animal side of man, that is, to his physical health. I repeat: if you want to be healthy and normal, join the herd.

     "Strange, you're repeating what often comes to my own mind," Kovrin said. "It's as if you've spied on and overheard my innermost thoughts. But let's not talk about me. What do you mean by eternal truth?"

     The monk did not answer. Kovrin looked at him and could not make out his face: his features blurred and dissolved. Then the monk's head and hands began to disappear; his torso merged with the bench and the evening twilight, and he vanished completely.

     "The hallucination is over!" Kovrin said and laughed. "What a pity."

     He walked back to the house cheerful and happy. The little that the black monk had told him flattered not his vanity, but his entire soul, his whole being. To be chosen, to serve eternal truth, to stand among those who, several thousand years earlier, will make humanity worthy of the kingdom of God, that is, to save people from several extra thousands of years of struggle, sin, and suffering, to give everything to an idea – youth, strength, health, to be ready to die for the common good – what a high, what a happy destiny! His past, pure, chaste, full of labor, flashed through his memory, he recalled what he had learned and what he himself had taught others, and decided that there was no exaggeration in the monk's words.

     Tanya was walking towards him through the park. She was wearing a different dress now.

     "Are you here?" she said. "We've been looking and looking for you... But what's wrong with you?" she wondered, looking at his ecstatic, radiant face and eyes full of tears. "How strange you are, Andryusha."

     "I'm content, Tanya," Kovrin said, placing his hands on her shoulders. "I'm more than content, I'm happy! Tanya, dear Tanya, you are an extraordinarily charming creature. Dear Tanya, I'm so glad, so glad!"

     He passionately kissed both her hands and continued:

     "I've just experienced bright, wonderful, unearthly moments. But I can't tell you everything, because you'll call me crazy or won't believe me. Let's talk about you. Dear, glorious Tanya! I love you and have grown accustomed to loving you. Your closeness, our meetings ten times a day, have become a necessity for my soul. I don't know how I'll manage without you when I go back home."

     "Oh!" Tanya laughed. "You'll forget about us in two days. We are small people, and you are a great man."

     "No, let's talk seriously!" he said. "I'll take you with me, Tanya. Yes? Will you come with me? Do you want to be mine?"

     "Oh!" Tanya said and tried to laugh again, but no laugh came out, and red spots appeared on her face.

     She began to breathe heavily and walked quickly, but not towards the house, but further into the park.

     "I didn't think about it... I didn't think!" she said, wringing her hands as if in despair.

     And Kovrin followed her, still with the same radiant, ecstatic face:

     "I want a love that would completely engulf me, and only you, Tanya, can give me that love. I am happy! Happy!"

     She was stunned, bent over, shrank, and seemed to age ten years at once, while he found her beautiful and loudly expressed his delight:

     "How beautiful she is!"
