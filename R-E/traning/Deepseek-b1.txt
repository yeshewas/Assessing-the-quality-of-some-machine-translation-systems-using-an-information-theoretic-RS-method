Dead Souls

A Poem

VOLUME ONE

CHAPTER ONE

A rather handsome small spring carriage, of the kind used by bachelors: retired lieutenant colonels, staff captains, landowners with about a hundred serfs – in short, all those whom they call gentlemen of the middling sort, drove through the gates of the hotel in the provincial town of NN. In the carriage sat a gentleman, not handsome, but also not of bad appearance, neither too fat nor too thin; one could not say he was old, yet neither was he too young. His arrival caused absolutely no stir in the town and was accompanied by nothing remarkable; only two Russian peasants, standing at the door of a tavern opposite the hotel, made a few remarks, relating, however, more to the carriage than to the one sitting in it. "Look here," said one to the other, "what a wheel! What do you think, would that wheel get to Moscow, if it happened, or not?" – "It would get there," answered the other. "But to Kazan, I think, it wouldn't get there?" – "To Kazan it wouldn't get there," answered the other. With that the conversation ended. And also, when the carriage pulled up to the hotel, a young man in white nankeen trousers, very tight and short, and a tailcoat with pretensions to fashion, from under which was visible a shirtfront fastened with a Tula pin shaped like a bronze pistol, met it. The young man turned around, looked at the carriage, held onto his cap, which had nearly blown off in the wind, and went on his way.

When the carriage drove into the courtyard, the gentleman was met by a tavern servant, or "floor man," as they are called in Russian taverns, so lively and fidgety that it was even impossible to make out what his face was like. He ran out briskly, with a napkin in his hand – all lanky and in a long fustian frock coat with the back nearly up to the very nape of his neck, shook his hair and quickly led the gentleman up the entire wooden gallery to show him the peaceful lodging sent by God. The lodging was of a well-known kind, for the hotel itself was also of a well-known kind – namely, exactly the sort hotels are in provincial towns, where for two rubles a day travelers get a quiet room with cockroaches peeking out, like prunes, from all corners, and a door to the next room, always blocked by a chest of drawers, where a neighbor settles in, a silent and calm person, but extremely curious, interested in knowing all the details about the traveler. The outer facade of the hotel matched its interior: it was very long, two stories high; the lower floor was not plastered and remained in dark-red brick, darkened even more by wild weather changes and already quite grimy on its own; the upper floor was painted with the eternal yellow paint; downstairs were small shops selling horse collars, ropes, and bagels. In the corner of one of these shops, or rather, in the window, sat a sbiten seller with a samovar of red copper and a face as red as the samovar, so that from a distance one might think there were two samovars in the window, if one samovar did not have a beard as black as pitch.

While the visiting gentleman inspected his room, his belongings were brought in: first of all, a suitcase of white leather, somewhat worn, showing that it was not the first time on the road. The suitcase was carried in by the coachman Selifan, a short man in a sheepskin coat, and the footman Petrushka, a fellow of about thirty, in a roomy, secondhand frock coat, evidently from his master's shoulder, a fellow with a somewhat stern look, with very large lips and nose. After the suitcase, a small casket of mahogany with fancy inlays of Karelian birch, boot lasts, and a roasted chicken wrapped in blue paper were brought in. When all this had been brought in, the coachman Selifan went off to the stable to fuss over the horses, and the footman Petrushka began to settle himself in the small front room, a very dark little kennel, where he had already managed to drag his overcoat and along with it some sort of his own personal smell, which was also imparted to the sack of various footman's toiletries brought in afterwards. In this little kennel he fixed a narrow, three-legged bed against the wall, covering it with a small semblance of a mattress, beaten flat as a pancake.... and perhaps as greasy as the pancake he had managed to wheedle out of the innkeeper.  

While the servants were busying themselves and bustling about, the gentleman went into the common room. What these common rooms are like, every traveler knows very well: the same walls painted with oil paint, darkened at the top by pipe smoke and greased at the bottom by the backs of various travelers, and even more so by the backs of local merchants, for on market days merchants came here in groups of five or six to drink their customary couple of cups of tea; the same soot-blackened ceiling; the same smoked chandelier with a multitude of hanging glass drops that jumped and jingled every time the waiter ran across the worn oilcloth, briskly waving a tray on which sat as many teacups as birds on the seashore; the same full-wall paintings done in oils — in short, everything the same as everywhere else; the only difference was that one painting depicted a nymph with such enormous breasts as the reader, surely, has never seen. This sort of trick of nature, however, occurs in various historical paintings, brought to us in Russia at some unknown time, from some unknown place and by some unknown person, and sometimes even by our own noblemen, art lovers, who bought them in Italy on the advice of the couriers who transported them. The gentleman took off his cap and unwound from his neck a woolen scarf of rainbow colors, the kind that a wife prepares with her own hands for a married man, supplying proper instructions on how to wrap it, while for bachelors — I cannot say for sure who makes them; God knows, I have never worn such scarves. Having unwound the scarf, the gentleman ordered dinner to be served. While various ordinary inn dishes were being brought to him, such as: cabbage soup with a flaky pie, deliberately saved for travelers over the course of several weeks, brains with peas, sausages with cabbage, roast hen, a pickled cucumber, and the eternal sweet flaky pie, always at the ready; while all this was being served to him, both hot and simply cold, he made the servant, or waiter, tell all sorts of nonsense — about who had previously kept the inn and who kept it now, and how much income it brought, and whether their master was a great scoundrel; to which the waiter, as usual, replied: "Oh, a great one, sir, a swindler." As in enlightened Europe, so in enlightened Russia, there are now very many respectable people who cannot eat in an inn without talking to the servant, and sometimes even making a funny joke at his expense. However, the visitor did not ask only empty questions; he inquired with extraordinary precision about who the governor in the city was, who the chairman of the chamber was, who the prosecutor was — in short, he did not miss a single significant official; but with even greater precision, if not with concern, he asked about all the significant landowners: how many serfs each had, how far they lived from the city, even what sort of character they had and how often they came to the city; he carefully inquired about the state of the region: whether there had been any diseases in their province — widespread fevers, deadly typhus, smallpox, and the like, and all this so thoroughly and with such precision that it showed more than mere simple curiosity. In his mannerisms, the gentleman had something solid about him and blew his nose extremely loudly. It is unknown how he did it, but his nose sounded like a trumpet. This, in my opinion, completely innocent merit, however, earned him much respect from the inn servant, so much so that every time he heard this sound, the servant would shake his hair, straighten up more respectfully, and, bending his head down from his height, ask if he needed anything. After dinner, the gentleman drank a cup of coffee and sat down on the sofa, placing behind his back a cushion which, in Russian inns, instead of elastic wool, is stuffed with something extremely reminiscent of brick and cobblestone. Then he began to yawn and ordered himself to be shown to his room, where, lying down, he slept for two hours. Having rested, he wrote on a scrap of paper, at the request of the inn servant, his rank, first name, and surname to be communicated to the proper authorities, the police. On the paper, the waiter, going down the stairs, read syllable by syllable the following:"Collegiate Councillor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, a landowner, traveling on his own business." While the waiter was still deciphering the note syllable by syllable, Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov himself went to look at the city, with which he seemed satisfied, for he found that it was in no way inferior to other provincial towns: the yellow paint on the stone houses struck the eye forcefully, and the gray on the wooden ones was modestly dark. The houses were of one, two, and one and a half stories, with an eternal mezzanine, very beautiful in the opinion of provincial architects. In some places these houses seemed lost amid a street as wide as a field and endless wooden fences; in other places they huddled together, and here there was more noticeable movement of people and liveliness. There were signs, almost washed out by the rain, with pretzels and boots, here and there with blue trousers painted and the signature of some Arshavsky tailor; a shop with caps and peaked caps and the inscription: "Foreigner Vasily Fedorov"; here a billiard table was painted with two players in tailcoats, such as guests entering the stage in the last act of our theaters wear. The players were depicted with cue sticks aimed, arms somewhat twisted back, and legs askew, having just performed an entr'chat in the air. Under all this was written: "And here is the establishment." Here and there, right on the street, stood tables with nuts, soap, and gingerbread resembling soap; a tavern with a painted fat fish and a fork stuck into it. Most often of all, darkened double-headed state eagles were noticeable, which have now been replaced by the laconic inscription: "Drinking House." The pavement was quite poor everywhere. He also glanced into the city garden, which consisted of thin trees, poorly rooted, with supports below in the shape of triangles, very beautifully painted with green oil paint. However, although these little trees were no taller than reeds, the newspapers, when describing the illumination, said that "our city has been adorned, thanks to the care of the civil governor, with a garden consisting of shady, broad-branched trees providing coolness on a hot day," and that at this "it was very touching to see how the hearts of the citizens trembled in an excess of gratitude and shed streams of tears as a sign of appreciation to the esteemed mayor." Having asked the watchman in detail where one could go closer, if necessary, to the cathedral, to the government offices, to the governor, he went to look at the river flowing through the middle of the city; on the way he tore off a poster nailed to a post, intending to read it thoroughly upon returning home; he looked intently at a not unattractive lady walking on the wooden sidewalk, followed by a boy in a military livery with a small bundle in his hand; and, once again glancing over everything with his eyes, as if to properly remember the layout of the place, he returned home straight to his room, slightly assisted up the stairs by the inn servant. Having drunk some tea, he sat down at the table, ordered a candle to be brought, took the poster out of his pocket, brought it to the candle, and began to read, squinting his right eye a little. However, there was nothing particularly remarkable about the poster: a drama by Mr. Kotzebue was being performed, in which Rolla was played by Mr. Poplyovin, Cora by Miss Zyablova, and the other characters were even less remarkable; nevertheless, he read them all, even got to the price of the orchestra stalls, and learned that the poster was printed at the printing house of the provincial administration; then he turned it over to the other side: to see if there was anything else there, but finding nothing, he rubbed his eyes, folded it neatly, and put it in his small chest, where he had the habit of putting everything that came his way. The day, it seems, was concluded with a portion of cold veal, a bottle of sour cabbage soup, and a sound sleep at full pump power, as they say in some parts of the vast Russian state.

The entire following day was devoted to visits; the newcomer set off to pay calls on all the city dignitaries. He respectfully visited the governor, who, as it turned out, like Chichikov, was neither fat nor thin, wore the Order of St. Anna around his neck, and it was even rumored that he had been nominated for a star; however, he was a great kind-hearted man and sometimes even embroidered on tulle himself. Then he went to the vice-governor,Then he went to see the prosecutor, the chairman of the chamber, the police chief, the tax farmer, the superintendent of state factories... a pity it's somewhat hard to remember all the powerful of this world; but enough to say that the visitor showed extraordinary activity regarding visits: he even turned up to pay his respects to the inspector of the medical board and the city architect. And afterwards he sat in his britchka for a long time, pondering whom else he might visit, but there were no more officials left in the city. In conversations with these authorities, he very skillfully managed to flatter each one. To the governor he hinted somehow in passing that entering his province was like entering paradise, the roads were velvet everywhere, and that those governments which appoint wise dignitaries were deserving of great praise. To the police chief he said something very flattering about the city constables; and in conversations with the vice-governor and the chairman of the chamber, who were only state councillors, he even mistakenly said twice: "Your Excellency," which pleased them greatly. The consequence of this was that the governor extended him an invitation to come to his house that very day for a home party, and the other officials likewise, on their part, invited him to dinner, to a game of Boston, or to a cup of tea.

     About himself, the visitor, as it seemed, avoided speaking much; if he did speak, it was in some sort of generalities, with noticeable modesty, and his conversation on such occasions took on somewhat bookish turns: that he was an insignificant worm of this world and unworthy of much concern, that he had experienced much in his life, had suffered in service for the truth, had many enemies who even attempted his life, and that now, wishing to settle down, he sought at last to choose a place to live, and having arrived in this city, he considered it his absolute duty to pay his respects to its foremost dignitaries. That is all that was learned in the city about this new person, who very soon did not fail to show himself at the governor's party. The preparations for this party took over two hours, and here the visitor displayed such attentiveness to his attire as even is not seen everywhere. After a short post-dinner nap, he ordered water for washing and rubbed both cheeks with soap for an extremely long time, propping them from the inside with his tongue; then, taking a towel from the shoulder of the inn servant, he wiped his full face from all sides with it, starting from behind his ears and snorting a couple of times right into the inn servant's face. Then he put on a shirtfront before the mirror, plucked two hairs that had come out of his nose, and immediately after that found himself in a frock coat of lingonberry color with a sparkle. Dressed thus, he rolled off in his own carriage along the endlessly wide streets, lit by the meager illumination from the occasional flickering lamps. However, the governor's house was so brightly lit, enough even for a ball; a carriage with lanterns, two gendarmes at the entrance, postilion shouts in the distance—in short, everything as it should be. Entering the hall, Chichikov had to squint his eyes for a moment because the brilliance of the candles, lamps, and ladies' dresses was terrible. Everything was flooded with light. Black frock coats flickered and darted separately and in clusters here and there, just as flies dart about on white gleaming refined sugar during the hot July summer, when the old housekeeper chops and divides it into sparkling chunks before an open window; the children all watch, gathered around, following curiously the movements of her hard hands raising the hammer, while the airy squadrons of flies, lifted by the light air, fly boldly in like full masters, and taking advantage of the old woman's dim-sightedness and the sun troubling her eyes, they shower the tasty morsels, some scattered, some in thick heaps. Sated by the rich summer, which lays out tasty dishes at every step anyway, they flew in not at all to eat, but only to show themselves, to stroll back and forth over the sugar pile, to rub their hind or front legs against each other, or to scratch themselves under their wings with them, or, stretching out both front legs, to rub them over their heads, turn around, and fly away again, and return again with new troublesome squadrons. The next day Chichikov went to dine and spend the evening at the Police Commissioner’s, where they settled down to whist at three o’clock after dinner and played until two in the morning. There, among others, he made the acquaintance of a landowner named Nozdryov, a man of about thirty, a dashing fellow who, after three or four words, began addressing him as “thou.” Nozdryov was also on “thou” terms with the Police Commissioner and the Prosecutor and treated them like friends; but when they sat down to play a high-stakes game, the Police Commissioner and the Prosecutor examined his tricks extremely carefully and watched almost every card he led.

The next day Chichikov spent the evening at the President of the Council’s house, who received his guests in a somewhat greasy dressing gown, including two ladies of some sort. Then he attended a party at the Vice-Governor’s, a large dinner at the tax-farmer’s, a small dinner at the Prosecutor’s, which, however, was worth a large one; and a luncheon after mass given by the City Mayor, which was also worth a dinner. In short, not an hour did he have to remain at home, and he returned to the inn only to sleep. The newcomer somehow managed to fit in everywhere and showed himself to be an experienced man of the world. Whatever the conversation might be, he always knew how to sustain it: if the talk turned to horse breeding, he spoke of horse breeding; if they spoke of good dogs, he made very sensible observations; if they discussed an investigation conducted by the Treasury Chamber, he showed that he was not unfamiliar with judicial tricks; if there was a discussion about billiards, he made no mistake in that either; if they spoke of virtue, he discoursed on virtue very well, even with tears in his eyes; if of making mulled wine, he knew the good of mulled wine; if of customs inspectors and officials, he judged them as if he himself had been both official and inspector. But remarkable it was that he knew how to clothe all this with a certain dignity, knew how to conduct himself well. He spoke neither loudly nor softly, but exactly as he ought. In short, no matter which way you turned, he was a very respectable man. All the officials were pleased with the arrival of the new person. The Governor said of him that he was a well-intentioned man; the Prosecutor, that he was a capable man; the Colonel of Gendarmes said he was a learned man; the President of the Council said he was a knowledgeable and honorable man; the Police Commissioner said he was an honorable and amiable man; the Police Commissioner's wife said he was the most amiable and courteous man. To which his wife replied: "Hmph!" and nudged him with her leg.

Such an opinion, very flattering to the guest, was formed of him in the city, and it held until one strange trait of the guest and an undertaking, or, as they say in the provinces, a *passage*, which the reader will soon learn about, plunged almost the entire city into complete bewilderment.

**Chapter Two**

The visiting gentleman had already lived in the city for more than a week, going to parties and dinners and thus spending, as they say, a very pleasant time. Finally he decided to extend his visits out of town and call on the landowners Manilov and Sobakevich, to whom he had given his word. Perhaps he was prompted to this by another, more substantial reason, a more serious matter, closer to his heart… But the reader will learn about all this gradually and in due time, if only he has the patience to read the proposed story, a very long one, which will later broaden and expand as it approaches the end that crowns the work.

The coachman Selifan had been ordered early in the morning to harness the horses to the familiar britzka; Petrushka was ordered to stay home, look after the room and the suitcase. It would not be superfluous for the reader to make the acquaintance of these two serfs of our hero. Although, of course, they are not such noticeable figures, what are called secondary or even tertiary, although the main moves and springs of the poem do not rest on them, and only touch and lightly engage them here and there—yet the author is extremely fond of being thorough in everything, and in this regard, despite being himself a Russian man, wishes to be as precise as a German. This will not take much time or space, because little needs to be added to what the reader already knows, namely that Petrushka wore a somewhat wide brown frock coat from his master’s shoulder, and had, as is customary with people of his station, a large nose and lips. He was of a more taciturn than talkative character; he even had a noble impulse towards enlightenment, that is, towards reading books, the content of which did not trouble him: it was all the same to him whether it was the adventures of a love-struck hero, a simple primer, or a prayer book—he read everything with equal attention; if someone had handed him a chemistry textbook, he would not have refused that either. What he liked was not what he read, but rather the act of reading itself, or, better said, the process of reading, that out of letters some word always emerges, which sometimes the devil knows what it means. This reading was mostly done lying down in the entryway, on the bed and on a mattress that had become as flat and thin as a pancake from this circumstance. Besides his passion for reading, he had two other habits that constituted two other characteristic traits: sleeping without undressing, just as he was, in the same frock coat, and always carrying with him some peculiar air of his own, a smell of his own, redolent somewhat of a lived-in room, so that it was enough for him merely to place his bed somewhere, even in a previously uninhabited room, and move his coat and belongings there, and it already seemed as if people had lived in that room for ten years. Chichikov, being a very fastidious man and even finicky in some respects, would sniff the air with his fresh nose in the morning, only wince and shake his head, saying: "You, brother, the devil knows you, you sweat or something. You should go to the bathhouse at least." To which Petrushka would say nothing and would try at once to busy himself with some task; either he would go to the hanging master’s tailcoat with a small whip, or simply tidy something up. What he was thinking at that time, when he was silent—perhaps he was saying to himself: "And you’re not so bad yourself, aren’t you tired of repeating the same thing forty times?"—God knows, it’s hard to know what a household serf is thinking at the moment his master is giving him instructions. So, that is what can be said about Petrushka for now.

The coachman Selifan was a completely different person... But the author is very ashamed to occupy the reader for so long with people of the lower class, knowing from experience how reluctant they are to make the acquaintance of the lower classes. Such is the Russian man: a strong passion to become acquainted with someone who is at least one rank higher, and a hat-tipping acquaintance with a count or a prince is better for him than any close friendly relations. The author even fears for his hero, who is only a Collegiate Councilor. Court Councilors, perhaps, might make his acquaintance, but those who have already reached general’s ranks—God knows, perhaps they will throw one of those contemptuous glances that a proud man casts on everything that grovels at his feet, or, even worse, perhaps they will pass by with a murderous lack of attention for the author. But as distressing as either of these is, one must nevertheless return to the hero.

So, having given the necessary orders the night before, having woken up very early in the morning, washed himself, wiped himself from head to toe with a wet sponge (which was done only on Sundays—and that day happened to be Sunday), having shaved in such a way that his cheeks became genuine satin in smoothness and gloss, having put on a frock coat of lingonberry color with a sparkle, and then his greatcoat with the big bearskin collar, he descended the stairs, supported under the arm now on one side, now on the other, by the inn servant, and got into the britzka.

With a rumble the britzka drove out from under the inn gates into the street. A passing priest took off his hat; several boys in dirty shirts held out their hands, saying: "Master, give to a little orphan!" The coachman, noticing that one of them was a great lover of standing on the footboard, lashed him with his whip, and the britzka started bouncing over the cobblestones. Not without joy was the striped barrier seen in the distance, giving notice that the paved road, like any other torment, would soon end; and after banging his head rather hard against the carriage body a few more times, Chichikov finally sped onto soft ground.

As soon as the city was left behind, they began to write, in our custom, nonsense and rubbish on both sides of the road: hummocks, a spruce forest, low thin bushes of young pines, charred trunks of old ones, wild heather, and similar trash. Villages stretched out along a string appeared, built like old stacked firewood, covered with gray roofs with carved wooden decorations beneath them in the form of hanging, patterned towels. Several peasants, as usual, were yawning, sitting on benches before the gates in their sheepskin coats. Women with fat faces and bound-up breasts looked out of the upper windows; from the lower ones a calf gazed out, or a pig stuck out its blind snout. In short, familiar sights.

After traveling fifteen versts, he remembered that according to Manilov, his village should be here, but the sixteenth verst also flew by, and the village was still not in sight, and had it not been for two peasants who came towards them, they would hardly have been able to hit the mark. When asked how far the village of Zamanilovka was, the peasants removed their hats, and one of them, who was smarter and wore a wedge-shaped beard, replied:

"Manilovka, perhaps, not Zamanilovka?"

"Yes, Manilovka."

"Manilovka! When you've gone another verst, then straight to the right."

"To the right?" said the coachman.

"To the right," said the peasant. "That'll be the road to Manilovka. And there is no Zamanilovka. It's called that, I mean its name is Manilovka, but there's no Zamanilovka here at all. There, straight ahead on the hill, you'll see a stone house, two stories, the manor house, where the master himself lives. That is Manilovka for you, and Zamanilovka is not here at all and never was."

They set off to find Manilovka. After driving two versts, they came upon a turn onto a dirt road, but they already seemed to have done two, three, and four versts, and the two-story stone house was still not visible. Then Chichikov remembered that if a friend invites you to his village fifteen versts away, it means that it is a good thirty versts to get there.

The village of Manilovka could lure few with its location. The manor house stood alone on a rise, that is, on an elevation open to all the winds that might feel like blowing; the slope of the hill on which it stood was covered with trimmed turf. On it were scattered, in the English style, two or three flowerbeds with bushes of lilac and yellow acacia; five or six birch trees here and there in small clumps raised their thin, small-leaved, sparse tops. Beneath two of them was visible a gazebo with a flat green dome, wooden blue columns, and the inscription: "Temple of Solitary Reflection." Below it was a pond covered with green, which, however, is not surprising in the English gardens of Russian landowners. At the foot of this elevation, and partly along the slope itself, dark gray log huts stretched crosswise, which our hero, for unknown reasons, immediately began to count and counted over two hundred; nowhere between them was there a growing tree or any greenery; only logs were visible everywhere. Two women enlivened the scene, who, having picturesquely hitched up their skirts and tucked them in all around, waded up to their knees in the pond, dragging a torn seine net between two wooden rakes, where two tangled crayfish were visible and a caught roach was gleaming; the women seemed to be quarreling and scolding each other about something. Off to one side, a pine forest darkened with a dull blueish color. Even the weather itself had very conveniently obliged: the day was neither clear nor gloomy, but of a kind of light gray color, such as only occurs on the old uniforms of garrison soldiers, this, however, of a peaceful army, but somewhat tipsy on Sundays. To complete the picture, there was no lack of a rooster, a harbinger of changeable weather, which, despite having its head pecked right down to the brain by the noses of other roosters over well-known matters of philandering, crowed very loudly and even flapped its wings, which were as ragged as old bast mats.

As he drove into the courtyard, Chichikov noticed the master himself on the porch, who was standing in a green shamrock frock coat, holding his hand like an umbrella to his forehead over his eyes, to get a better look at the approaching carriage. As the britzka drew closer to the porch, his eyes became merrier and his smile widened more and more.

"Pavel Ivanovich!" he finally exclaimed, as Chichikov climbed out of the britzka. "You have finally remembered us!"

The two friends kissed each other very heartily, and Manilov led his guest into the room. Although the time it will take them to pass through the entryway, the front hall, and the dining room is somewhat short, let us try to see if we can somehow use it to say a few things about the master of the house. But here the author must admit that such an undertaking is very difficult. It is much easier to depict characters of large scale: there you simply fling paints with your whole hand onto the canvas—black, piercing eyes, overhanging brows, a forehead cut by a wrinkle, a black or fiery-red cloak thrown over the shoulder—and the portrait is ready; but these gentlemen, who are numerous in the world, who look very similar to each other, and yet, when you look closely, you see many of the most elusive peculiarities—these gentlemen are terribly difficult for portraits. Here one must strain one's attention strongly until one forces all the fine, almost invisible features to come forward, and in general one must deeply sharpen one's gaze, already practiced in the art of detecting.

Only God, perhaps, could have said what Manilov's character was. There is a kind of people known by the name: so-so people, neither this nor that, neither Bogdan in the city nor Selifan in the village, according to the proverb. Perhaps Manilov should be joined to them. In appearance, he was a distinguished man; his facial features were not lacking in pleasantness, but in this pleasantness, it seemed, there was too much sugar; in his manners and turns of phrase there was something ingratiating and seeking favor. He smiled enticingly, was fair-haired, with blue eyes. In the first minute of conversation with him, you cannot help but say: "What a pleasant and kind man!" The next minute, you say nothing, and the third, you say: "The devil knows what!" and move away; if you don't move away, you will feel deadly boredom. You cannot expect from him any lively or even arrogant word, such as you can hear from almost anyone if you touch upon a subject that irritates them. Everyone has their own enthusiasm: one person's enthusiasm turns to greyhounds; another seems to think he is a passionate lover of music and can wonderfully feel all the deep parts of it; a third is a master at a good dinner; a fourth at playing a role at least an inch higher than the one assigned to him; a fifth, with a more limited desire, sleeps and dreams about how to take a stroll with an aide-de-camp, to show off to his friends, acquaintances, and even strangers; a sixth is already endowed with a hand that feels a supernatural desire to break the corner of some ace or deuce of diamonds, while a seventh's hand just itches to create order somewhere, to get closer to the person of the stationmaster or the coachmen—in short, everyone has their own, but Manilov had nothing. At home he spoke very little and mostly reflected and thought, but what he thought about, only God knew. It cannot be said that he occupied himself with farming; he never even went out to the fields, and the farming went on somehow by itself. When the steward said, "It would be good, master, to do this or that," "Yes, not bad," he usually replied, smoking a pipe, which he had made a habit of smoking when he was still serving in the army, where he was considered the most modest, most delicate, and most educated officer. "Yes, indeed, not bad," he repeated. When a peasant came to him and, scratching the back of his head with his hand, said, "Master, allow me to go away to work, to earn some money," "Go," he would say, smoking his pipe, and it never even occurred to him that the peasant was going off to get drunk. Sometimes, looking from the porch at the yard and the pond, he would speak of how good it would be if suddenly a tunnel could be built from the house, or a stone bridge built across the pond, which would have shops on both sides, so that merchants could sit in them and sell various small goods needed by the peasants. At this, his eyes became extremely sweet and his face took on the most satisfied expression; however, all these projects ended only in words. In his study there always lay some book, with a bookmark placed at the fourteenth page, which he had been constantly reading for two years. In his house there was always a shortage of something: in the drawing room there was beautiful furniture, covered in stylish silk fabric, which certainly cost no small amount; but there was not enough for two chairs, and the chairs stood covered simply in bast matting; however, the host for several years every time warned his guest with the words: "Don't sit on these chairs, they're not ready yet." In another room there was no furniture at all, although it had been said in the first days after the wedding: "Darling, we must start making arrangements tomorrow to put at least some furniture in this room." In the evenings, a very stylish candlestick of dark bronze with three antique graces and a stylish mother-of-pearl shield was brought to the table, and next to it was placed some simple copper invalid, lame, curled over to one side and covered in tallow, although neither the master nor the mistress nor the servants noticed this. His wife... however, they were completely content with each other. Although more than eight years of their marriage had passed, each of them still brought the other either a piece of apple, or a candy, or a nut, and spoke in a touchingly tender voice expressing perfect love: "Open your mouth, darling, I'll put this piece for you." It goes without saying that the mouth was opened very gracefully on these occasions. For birthdays, surprises were prepared: some kind of beaded toothpick case. And very often, sitting on the sofa, suddenly, for completely unknown reasons, one leaving his pipe and the other her work, if she happened to have it in her hands at that moment, they would imprint on each other such a languid and long kiss that during it one could easily have smoked a little straw cigar. In short, they were what is called happy. Of course, it could be noted that there are many other activities in the house besides long kisses and surprises, and many different questions could be asked. Why, for example, is the cooking done so stupidly and aimlessly in the kitchen? Why is the pantry rather empty? Why is the housekeeper a thief? Why are the servants unclean and drunkards? Why does the whole household sleep mercilessly and act like hooligans the rest of the time? But all these are low subjects, and Manilova was well brought up. And good upbringing, as is known, is obtained in boarding schools. And in boarding schools, as is known, three main subjects form the basis of human virtues: the French language, necessary for the happiness of family life; the piano, to provide pleasant moments for the spouse; and finally, the domestic part itself: knitting purses and other surprises. However, there are various improvements and changes in methods, especially nowadays; it all depends more on the prudence and abilities of the boarding school keepers themselves. In other boarding schools, it happens that first is the piano, then the French language, and then the domestic part. And sometimes it happens that first is the domestic part, i.e., knitting surprises, then the French language, and then the piano. Various methods exist. It would not be amiss to also remark that Manilova... but, I confess, I am very afraid to speak of ladies; besides, it is time for me to return to our heroes, who had already been standing for several minutes before the drawing-room doors, each mutually asking the other to go in first.

"Please, don't trouble yourself so for my sake, I will go after," said Chichikov.

"No, Pavel Ivanovich, no, you are the guest," said Manilov, pointing his hand towards the door.

"Don't worry, please, don't worry. Please, go in," said Chichikov.

"No, excuse me, I will not allow such a pleasant, educated guest to go behind."

"Why educated? ... Please, go in."

"Well then, please, you go."

"But why?"

"Well, because!" said Manilov with a pleasant smile.

Finally, both friends entered the door sideways and squeezed each other a little.

"Allow me to introduce my wife," said Manilov. "Darling! Pavel Ivanovich!"

Chichikov indeed saw a lady whom he had completely not noticed while bowing with Manilov in the doorway. She was not bad-looking, dressed becomingly. A pale-colored, fabric silk dress-coat fitted her well; her thin, small hand hastily threw something onto the table and grasped a batiste handkerchief with embroidered corners. She rose from the sofa where she was sitting; Chichikov approached her hand not without pleasure. Manilova spoke, even with a slight lisp, saying that he had greatly gladdened them with his arrival and that her husband had not let a day pass without mentioning him.

"Yes," added Manilov, "she would always ask me: 'Why doesn't your friend come?' 'Wait, darling, he'll come.' And now you have finally honored us with your visit. Truly, you have given us such pleasure... a May day... the name day of the heart..."

Chichikov, hearing that it had come to the name day of the heart, was somewhat embarrassed and replied modestly that he had neither a loud name nor even a notable rank.

"You have everything," interrupted Manilov with the same pleasant smile, "you have everything, even more."

"How did you find our city?" added Manilova. "Did you spend a pleasant time there?"

"A very good city, a beautiful city," replied Chichikov, "and I spent a very pleasant time: the society is most courteous."

"And how did you find our Governor?" said Manilova.

"Isn't it true that he is a most honorable and most amiable man?" added Manilov.

"Absolutely true," said Chichikov, "a most honorable man. And how he has entered upon his duties, how he understands them! One could wish for more such people."

"How he can, you know, receive everyone so well, observe delicacy in his actions," added Manilov with a smile and almost completely closed his eyes with pleasure, like a cat whose ear has been lightly tickled with a finger.

"A very courteous and pleasant man," continued Chichikov, "and what an artisan! I never would have suspected it. How well he embroiders various household patterns! He showed me a purse of his own work: a rare lady could embroider so skillfully."

"And the Vice-Governor, isn't he a nice man?" said Manilov, again somewhat narrowing his eyes.

"Very, very worthy man," replied Chichikov.

"Well, allow me, how did you find the Police Commissioner? Isn't it true that he is a very pleasant man?"

"Exceedingly pleasant, and what a smart, what a well-read man! We played whist at his house together with the Prosecutor and the President of the Council until the very late cocks crowed; a very, very worthy man."

"Well, and what is your opinion of the Police Commissioner's wife?" added Manilova. "Isn't it true she is a most amiable woman?"

"Oh, she is one of the most worthy women I know," replied Chichikov.

After that, they did not miss the President of the Council, the Postmaster, and thus went through almost all the officials of the city, all of whom turned out to be the most worthy people.

"Do you always spend your time in the countryside?" finally asked Chichikov in his turn.

"Mostly in the countryside," replied Manilov. "However, we come to town only to see educated people. You become wild, you know, if you live shut up all the time."

"True, true," said Chichikov.

"Of course," continued Manilov, "it would be another matter if the neighborhood were good, if, for example, there were a person with whom one could in some way talk about civility, about good treatment, follow some such science, so that it might stir the soul, give, so to speak, a kind of soaring..." Here he wanted to express something else, but, noticing that he had strayed a bit, he just waved his hand in the air and continued: "Then, of course, the countryside and solitude would have many pleasures. But there is absolutely no one... Only sometimes I read *The Son of the Fatherland*."

Chichikov agreed with this completely, adding that nothing could be more pleasant than to live in solitude, enjoy the spectacle of nature, and occasionally read some book...

"But you know," added Manilov, "it's still not the same if you don't have a friend with whom you could share..."

"Oh, that is fair, that is absolutely fair!" interrupted Chichikov. "What are all the treasures in the world then! 'Have no money, but have good people for company,' said one sage."

"And you know, Pavel Ivanovich!" said Manilov, showing in his face an expression not only sweet but even cloying, similar to that mixture which a skillful, worldly doctor has sweetened mercilessly, imagining by it to please his patient. "Then you feel a kind of, in some way, spiritual pleasure... Just like now, when chance has given me the happiness, one might say exemplary, to talk with you and enjoy your pleasant conversation..."

"Good heavens, what kind of pleasant conversation? ... An insignificant person, and nothing more," replied Chichikov.

"Oh! Pavel Ivanovich, allow me to be frank: I would gladly give half of all my possessions to have a part of the virtues that you have!..."

"On the contrary, I for my part would consider it the greatest..."

It is unknown how far the mutual outpouring of feelings of the two friends might have gone, if a servant who entered had not announced that the meal was ready.

"I humbly ask you," said Manilov. "Please excuse us if we do not have such a dinner as on parquet floors and in capitals; we have simply, in the Russian custom, cabbage soup, but from a pure heart. I humbly ask."

Here they argued for some time about who should enter first, and finally Chichikov entered the dining room sideways.

In the dining room already stood two boys, Manilov's sons, who were of those ages when children are already seated at the table, but still on high chairs. With them stood a teacher, who bowed politely and with a smile. The hostess sat down to her soup bowl; the guest was seated between the host and hostess; the servant tied napkins around the children's necks.

"What lovely children," said Chichikov, looking at them. "And how old are they?"

"The elder is eight, and the younger just turned six yesterday," said Manilova.

"Themistocleus!" said Manilov, turning to the elder, who was trying to free his chin, tied in a napkin by the footman.

Chichikov raised an eyebrow slightly upon hearing this somewhat Greek name, to which, for some unknown reason, Manilov had given the ending "-eus," but he tried at once to bring his face back to a normal expression.

"Themistocleus, tell me, what is the best city in France?"

Here the teacher turned all his attention to Themistocleus and seemed to want to jump into his eyes, but finally he completely calmed down and nodded his head when Themistocleus said: "Paris."

"And which is the best city in our country?" Manilov asked again.

The teacher again focused his attention.

"Petersburg," answered Themistocleus.

"And what else?"

"Moscow," answered Themistocleus.

"Clever boy, darling!" said Chichikov at this. "But tell me, though..." he continued, turning with a look of some astonishment to the Manilovs, "at such an age and already such knowledge! I must tell you that this child will have great abilities."

"Oh, you don't know him yet," replied Manilov, "he has an extraordinary amount of wit. Now the younger one, Alcides, is not as quick, but this one, if he sees something, a little bug, a gnat, his little eyes suddenly start darting around; he'll run after it and immediately pay attention. I intend for him to go into the diplomatic corps. Themistocleus," he continued, turning again to him, "do you want to be an ambassador?"

"Yes," answered Themistocleus, chewing bread and swinging his head right and left.

At that moment, the footman standing behind wiped the ambassador's nose, and did very well, otherwise a sizeable extraneous drop would have fallen into the soup.

At the table, conversation began about the pleasure of a quiet life, interrupted by the hostess's remarks about the city theater and the actors. The teacher watched the speakers very attentively, and as soon as he noticed that they were about to smile, he immediately opened his mouth and laughed with zeal. He was probably a grateful man and wanted to repay the host for his good treatment. Once, however, his face took on a stern expression, and he rapped strictly on the table, staring at the children sitting opposite him. This was appropriate, because Themistocleus had bitten Alcides on the ear, and Alcides, having squeezed his eyes shut and opened his mouth, was ready to sob in the most pathetic way, but, feeling that he could easily lose his dish for this, he brought his mouth back to its previous position and began with tears to gnaw on a mutton bone, from which both his cheeks shone with fat.

The hostess often turned to Chichikov with the words: "You're not eating anything, you took very little." To which Chichikov replied each time: "I humbly thank you, I am full; pleasant conversation is better than any dish."

They had already risen from the table. Manilov was extremely pleased and, supporting the back of his guest with his hand, was thus preparing to escort him into the drawing room, when suddenly the guest announced with a very significant air that he intended to talk with him about a very necessary matter.

"In that case, allow me to ask you into my study," said Manilov, and led him into a small room facing the blue-tinged forest. "Here is my corner," said Manilov.

"A pleasant little room," said Chichikov, glancing around it.

The room was, indeed, not without pleasantness: the walls were painted with some bluish paint, sort of grayish; four chairs, one armchair, a table on which lay a book with a bookmark, which we have already had occasion to mention; several sheets of paper covered in writing, but most of all there was tobacco. It was in various forms: in caps, in a tobacco pouch, and finally simply poured in a heap on the table. On the windowsills, too, were placed piles of ash knocked out of the pipe, arranged not without effort into very beautiful rows. It was noticeable that this sometimes provided the host with a pastime.

"Allow me to ask you to sit in these armchairs," said Manilov. "Here you will be more comfortable."

"Allow me to sit on a chair."

"Allow me not to allow you that," said Manilov with a smile. "This armchair is already assigned by me for a guest: whether you want to or not, you must sit down."

Chichikov sat down.

"Allow me to offer you a pipe."

"No, I don't smoke," replied Chichikov affectionately and as if with a look of regret.

"Why not?" said Manilov also affectionately and with a look of regret.

"I never acquired the habit, I'm afraid; they say a pipe dries one out."

"Allow me to remark to you that this is a prejudice. I even believe that smoking a pipe is much healthier than taking snuff. In our regiment there was a lieutenant, a most excellent and educated man, who did not take the pipe out of his mouth, not only at the table, but even, if you'll excuse me, in all other places. And now he is over forty, but thank God, he is still as healthy as could be."

Chichikov remarked that this does indeed happen, and that there are many things in nature that are inexplicable even to a broad mind.

"But allow me first one request..." he said in a voice that conveyed some strange, or almost strange, expression, and then, for some unknown reason, glanced back. "How long ago did you submit your revision list?"

"Oh, it's been a long time; or rather, I don't remember."

"How many of your peasants have died since that time?"

"I don't know; I suppose I need to ask the steward. Hey there! Send for the steward; he must be here today."

The steward appeared. He was a man about forty, who shaved his beard, wore a frock coat, and apparently led a very comfortable life, because his face looked somewhat puffy and full, and the yellowish color of his skin and small eyes showed that he knew too well what featherbeds and mattresses were. One could see at once that he had run his course just as all master's stewards do: he was first a simply literate little boy in the house, then he married some Agashka, the mistress's favorite housekeeper, became a butler himself, and then a steward. And having become a steward, he acted, of course, like all stewards: he associated and made cronies of those in the village who were richer, added to the tax burden of the poorer, woke up at nine o'clock in the morning, waited for the samovar, and drank tea.

"Listen, my good man! How many of our peasants have died since the last revision?"

"Well, how many? Many have died since then," said the steward, and hiccupped, covering his mouth slightly with his hand like a shield.

"Yes, I confess, I thought so myself," added Manilov. "Indeed, very many have died!" Here he turned to Chichikov and added: "Exactly, very many."

"And how many, for example?" asked Chichikov.

"Yes, how many?" added Manilov.

"How can one say a number? After all, it's unknown how many died; no one ever counted them."
Here is the English translation of the continuation:

"Yes, exactly," said Manilov, turning to Chichikov. "I also assumed there was a high mortality; it's completely unknown how many died."

"Please count them," said Chichikov, "and make a detailed list of all their names."

"Yes, all by name," said Manilov.

The steward said, "Very well," and left.

"And what reasons do you have for needing this?" Manilov asked after the steward had left.

This question seemed to perplex the guest; his face showed a kind of strained expression, which even made him blush—a strain to express something not quite obedient to words. And indeed, Manilov finally heard such strange and extraordinary things as human ears had never heard before.

"You ask for what reasons? Here are the reasons: I would like to buy peasants…" said Chichikov, stammered, and did not finish his speech.

"But allow me to ask you," said Manilov, "how do you wish to buy the peasants: with the land or simply for removal, that is, without land?"

"No, it's not exactly peasants that I mean," said Chichikov, "I wish to have dead ones…"

"How, sir? Excuse me… I am somewhat hard of hearing; I thought I heard a very strange word…"

"I intend to acquire dead ones, which, however, would be listed in the revision as living," said Chichikov.

Manilov immediately dropped his pipe stem with the pipe on the floor, and as he opened his mouth, he remained with his mouth open for several minutes. The two friends, who had been discussing the pleasantness of a friendly life, remained motionless, staring into each other's eyes like those portraits that used to be hung in olden times facing each other on both sides of a mirror. Finally, Manilov picked up his pipe with the stem and looked up at his face, trying to see if there was any smirk on his lips, if he was joking; but nothing of the sort was visible; on the contrary, his face seemed even more composed than usual. Then he thought whether his guest might have somehow inadvertently gone mad, and stared at him intently with fear; but his guest's eyes were perfectly clear; there was no wild, restless fire in them, like that which runs in the eyes of a madman; everything was proper and in order. No matter how Manilov tried to figure out how to behave and what to do, he could think of nothing else but to let the remaining smoke out of his mouth in a very thin stream.

"So, I would like to know whether you can transfer, cede to me such souls—not alive in reality, but alive relative to the legal form—or as you see fit?"

But Manilov was so embarrassed and confused that he just stared at him.

"I think you are perplexed?…" observed Chichikov.

"I?… no, it's not that," said Manilov, "but I cannot comprehend… excuse me… I, of course, could not receive such a brilliant education as is, so to speak, evident in every movement of yours; I do not possess the high art of expressing myself… Perhaps here… in this explanation you have just expressed… something else is hidden… Perhaps you were pleased to express yourself that way for the beauty of style?"

"No," chimed in Chichikov, "no, I mean the subject as it is, that is, those souls which have indeed already died."

Manilov was completely flustered. He felt that he needed to do something, to ask a question—but what question, the devil only knew. He ended finally by letting out smoke again, but only not through his mouth, but through his nostrils.

"So, if there are no obstacles, then with God's blessing we could proceed to drawing up the deed of purchase," said Chichikov.

"What, a deed of purchase for dead souls?"

"Oh, no!" said Chichikov. "We will write that they are alive, just as it actually stands in the revision list. I am accustomed not to deviate from the civil laws in anything, although I suffered for this in the service, but excuse me: duty is a sacred thing for me; the law—I am mute before the law."

The last words pleased Manilov, but he still could not grasp the essence of the matter at all, and instead of answering, began to suck on his pipe stem so strongly that it finally began to wheeze like a bassoon. It seemed as if he wanted to draw out of it an opinion regarding such an unheard-of circumstance; but the pipe stem wheezed and nothing more.

"Perhaps you have some doubts?"

"Oh! Mercy, not at all. I do not speak in that sense, that I have any, that is, critical prejudice against you. But allow me to suggest, will not this undertaking—or, to express it even more, so to speak, this *negotiation*—will not this negotiation be incompatible with the civil regulations and the further prospects of Russia?"

Here Manilov, making a slight movement of his head, looked very significantly into Chichikov's face, showing in all the features of his face and his compressed lips such a deep expression as, perhaps, has never been seen on a human face, except perhaps on some overly wise minister, and even then at the moment of the most perplexing affair.

But Chichikov said simply that such an undertaking, or negotiation, would in no way be incompatible with the civil regulations and the further prospects of Russia, and a minute later added that the treasury would even derive benefits, for it would receive the lawful fees.

"So you think?…"

"I think it will be good."

"Ah, if it is good, that is a different matter; I have nothing against that," said Manilov, and became completely calm.

"Now it remains to agree on a price."

"How, price?" said Manilov again and stopped. "Do you really suppose that I will take money for souls which have in some way ended their existence? If you have come up with such a, so to speak, fantastic desire, then on my part I turn them over to you without interest and I will take the deed of purchase upon myself."

A great reproach would be due to the historian of the events being proposed if he failed to say that pleasure overwhelmed the guest after such words spoken by Manilov. As composed and judicious as he was, he almost performed a leap after the manner of a goat, which, as is known, is performed only in the most powerful bursts of joy. He turned around so strongly in his armchair that the woolen fabric covering the cushion burst; Manilov himself looked at him in some bewilderment. Moved by gratitude, he immediately uttered so many thanks that Manilov became confused, blushed all over, made a negative gesture with his head, and finally expressed that it was truly nothing, that he, indeed, would like to prove by something his heartfelt attraction, the magnetism of his soul, and that dead souls were in some way complete rubbish.

"Very far from rubbish," said Chichikov, shaking his hand. A very deep sigh was released here. It seemed he was disposed to heartfelt outpourings; not without feeling and expression he finally uttered the following words: "If only you knew what a service you have rendered with this, seemingly rubbish, to a man without tribe or family! And indeed, what have I not suffered? like some kind of barge amidst ferocious waves… What persecutions, what harassments have I not experienced, what grief have I not tasted, and for what? for upholding the truth, for being pure in my conscience, for giving a hand to the helpless widow and the wretched orphan!…" Here he even wiped away a rolling tear with his handkerchief.

Manilov was completely moved. The two friends long pressed each other's hands and long looked silently into each other's eyes, in which tears were seen welling up. Manilov would not let go of our hero's hand and continued to press it so warmly that he no longer knew how to free it. Finally, pulling it away gently, he said that it would not be a bad idea to complete the deed of purchase as soon as possible, and it would be good if he himself would stop by the city. Then he took his hat and began to take his leave.

"What? Are you already wanting to leave?" said Manilov, suddenly coming to his senses and almost frightened.

At this moment, Manilova entered the study.

"Lizanka," said Manilov with a somewhat pitiful look, "Pavel Ivanovich is leaving us!"

"Because we have bored Pavel Ivanovich," replied Manilova.

"Madam! here," said Chichikov, "here, right here"—here he placed his hand on his heart—"yes, here shall remain the pleasantness of the time spent with you! And believe me, there would be no greater bliss for me than to live with you, if not in the same house, then at least in the very closest neighborhood."

"And you know, Pavel Ivanovich," said Manilov, who very much liked this thought, "how truly good it would be to live like that together, under the same roof, or beneath the shade of some elm tree to philosophize about something, to delve deep!…"

"Oh, that would be a heavenly life!" said Chichikov, sighing. "Farewell, madam!" he continued, approaching Manilova's hand. "Farewell, most honorable friend! Do not forget my request!"

"Oh, be assured!" replied Manilov. "I shall part with you for no more than two days."

Everyone went out into the dining room.

"Farewell, dear little ones!" said Chichikov, seeing Alcides and Themistocleus, who were occupied with some kind of wooden hussar that had neither an arm nor a nose. "Farewell, my little ones. You will excuse me for not bringing you a gift, because, I confess, I did not even know whether you lived in the world, but now, when I come back, I will certainly bring you one. I'll bring you a saber; do you want a saber?"

"Yes," answered Themistocleus.

"And for you a drum; isn't that right, you'd like a drum?" he continued, bending down to Alcides.

"Pah-rum," answered Alcides in a whisper, lowering his head.

"Good, I'll bring you a drum. Such a glorious drum, it will go like: turrr… ru… tra-ta-ta, ta-ta-ta… Farewell, dear one! farewell!" Thereupon he kissed him on the head and turned to Manilov and his wife with a slight laugh, such as is usually addressed to parents, letting them know about the innocence of their children's wishes.

"Really, stay, Pavel Ivanovich!" said Manilov, when they were all already out on the porch. "Look at those clouds."

"Those are little clouds," replied Chichikov.

"Do you know the way to Sobakevich's?"

"That is what I wanted to ask you about."

"Allow me, I will immediately tell your coachman."

Here Manilov with the same courtesy explained the matter to the coachman and even addressed him once as "you."

The coachman, hearing that he needed to pass two turnings and take the third, said: "We'll manage, your honor," and Chichikov drove off, accompanied by long bows and waves of the handkerchief from the hosts, who had risen up on their tiptoes.

Manilov stood on the porch for a long time, following the departing britzka with his eyes, and when it was completely out of sight, he still stood there, smoking his pipe. Finally, he went into the room, sat down on a chair, and gave himself over to reflection, rejoicing in his heart that he had given his guest a small pleasure. Then his thoughts imperceptibly transferred themselves to other subjects and finally carried themselves God knows where. He thought about the bliss of a friendly life, about how good it would be to live with a friend on the bank of some river, then a bridge began to be built across this river in his mind, then a huge house with such a high belvedere that one could see even Moscow from there, and there to drink tea outdoors in the evening and discuss some pleasant subjects. Then, that they would go together with Chichikov to some society in fine carriages, where they would charm everyone with the pleasantness of their manners, and that the sovereign, hearing of such their friendship, would make them generals, and further, finally, God knows what, which he himself could no longer make out.

Chichikov's strange request suddenly interrupted all his daydreaming. The thought of it somehow particularly refused to brew in his head: no matter how he turned it over, he could not explain it to himself, and he sat all the time and smoked his pipe, which continued right up until supper.
