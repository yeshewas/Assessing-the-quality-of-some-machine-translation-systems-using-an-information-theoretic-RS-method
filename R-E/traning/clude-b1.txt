Nikolai Vasilievich Gogol

Dead Souls

Dead Souls

A Poem

VOLUME ONE

CHAPTER ONE

A rather handsome small carriage with springs, the kind in which bachelors travel: retired lieutenant colonels, staff captains, landowners with about a hundred peasant souls, – in short, all those whom they call gentlemen of the middle rank, – drove into the gates of the inn of the provincial town of NN. In the carriage sat a gentleman, not handsome, but not of bad appearance either, neither too fat nor too thin; one could not say he was old, but neither was he particularly young. His arrival produced absolutely no stir in the town and was not accompanied by anything special; only two Russian peasants standing at the door of a tavern opposite the inn made some remarks, which, however, referred more to the carriage than to the man sitting in it. "Look there," said one to the other, "what a wheel! do you think that wheel would make it to Moscow if it happened to, or wouldn't it?" – "It would make it," answered the other. "But to Kazan, I don't think it would make it?" – "To Kazan it wouldn't make it," answered the other. The conversation ended there. And also, when the carriage drove up to the inn, a young man in white canvas trousers, very narrow and short, in a frock coat with pretensions to fashion, from under which a shirt front was visible, fastened with a Tula pin with a bronze pistol, met them. The young man turned around, looked at the carriage, held his hat with his hand, which was almost blown away by the wind, and went on his way.

When the carriage drove into the courtyard, the gentleman was met by a tavern servant, or waiter, as they are called in Russian taverns, so lively and fidgety that it was impossible to make out what his face looked like. He ran out nimbly, with a napkin in his hand, – all tall and in a long cotton coat with the back almost at the very nape of his neck, shook his hair and quickly led the gentleman up the entire wooden gallery to show him the room granted to him by God. The room was of a known kind, for the inn was also of a known kind, that is, precisely such as are the inns in provincial towns, where for two rubles a day travelers receive a comfortable room with cockroaches peering out like prunes from all corners, and a door to the neighboring room, always blocked by a chest of drawers, where a neighbor settles, a silent and calm man, but extraordinarily curious, interested in knowing all the details about the traveler. The outer facade of the inn corresponded to its interior: it was very long, two stories; the lower one was not plastered and remained in dark red bricks, darkened even more by harsh weather changes and dirty in themselves; the upper one was painted with eternal yellow paint; below were shops with horse collars, ropes and bagels. In the corner of one of these shops, or rather, in the window, stood a sbiten seller with a samovar of red copper and a face as red as the samovar, so that from a distance one might think there were two samovars in the window, if one of the samovars did not have a beard as black as pitch.

While the arriving gentleman was examining his room, his belongings were brought in: first of all a white leather suitcase, somewhat worn, showing that it had traveled before. The suitcase was brought in by the coachman Selifan, a short man in a short sheepskin coat, and the lackey Petrushka, a man of about thirty, in a spacious secondhand frock coat, apparently from his master's shoulder, a man somewhat stern in appearance, with very large lips and nose. After the suitcase, a small chest of mahogany with inlays of Karelian birch was brought in, shoe lasts and a fried chicken wrapped in blue paper. When all this had been brought in, the coachman Selifan went off to the stable to fuss about the horses, and the lackey Petrushka began to settle in a small front room, a very dark cubicle, where he had already managed to drag his overcoat and along with it some sort of his own smell, which was communicated to and brought in after that a bag with various lackey toiletries. In this cubicle he fitted to the wall a narrow three-legged bed, covering it with a small semblance of a mattress, beaten and flat as a pancake,and, perhaps, just as greasy as a pancake, which he had managed to extract from the innkeeper.

     While the servants were bustling about and fussing, the gentleman went into the common room. What these common rooms are like – every traveler knows very well: the same walls, painted with oil paint, darkened at the top from pipe smoke and greasy at the bottom from the backs of various travelers, and even more so from local merchants, for merchants on trading days would come here six at a time and seven at a time to drink their customary pair of teas; the same soot-covered ceiling; the same smoky chandelier with a multitude of hanging glass pieces, which jumped and jingled every time the waiter ran across the worn oilcloth, waving his tray briskly, on which sat such a multitude of teacups as there are birds on a seashore; the same paintings covering entire walls, painted in oils – in short, everything the same as everywhere else; the only difference being that in one painting was depicted a nymph with such enormous breasts as the reader has surely never seen. Such a freak of nature, however, occurs in various historical paintings, brought to us in Russia at some unknown time, from some unknown place, and by whom, sometimes even by our nobles, lovers of the arts, who purchased them in Italy on the advice of the couriers who brought them. The gentleman removed his cap and unwound from his neck a woolen scarf of rainbow colors, the kind that wives prepare with their own hands for married men, providing proper instructions on how to wrap it up, and for bachelors – I certainly cannot say who makes them, God knows, I have never worn such scarves. Having unwound the scarf, the gentleman ordered dinner to be brought. While various dishes usual in taverns were being served to him, such as: cabbage soup with a puff pastry pie, deliberately kept for travelers for several weeks, brains with peas, sausages with cabbage, roasted pullet, salted cucumber and the eternal sweet puff pastry pie, always ready to serve; while all this was being served to him, both heated and simply cold, he made the servant, or waiter, tell him all sorts of nonsense – about who had kept the tavern before and who keeps it now, and whether it brings in much income, and what a scoundrel their master is; to which the waiter, as usual, replied: "Oh, a great rogue, sir." Just as in enlightened Europe, so in enlightened Russia there are now very many respectable people who cannot eat in a tavern without talking to the servant, and sometimes even amusing themselves by joking with him. However, the visitor did not ask only empty questions; he inquired with extraordinary precision about who was the governor in the town, who was the chairman of the chamber, who was the prosecutor – in short, he did not skip a single important official; but with even greater precision, if not with concern, he inquired about all the significant landowners: how many serfs each one owned, how far they lived from the town, what sort of character they had and how often they came to town; he inquired carefully about the state of the region: whether there had been any illnesses in their province – epidemic fevers, deadly fevers of some kind, smallpox and the like, and all so thoroughly and with such precision as to show more than mere curiosity. In his manner the gentleman had something solid about him and he blew his nose with extraordinary loudness. It is unknown how he did it, but his nose sounded like a trumpet. This, in my opinion, a perfectly innocent quality, nevertheless acquired for him much respect from the tavern waiter, so that every time he heard this sound, he would shake his hair, straighten up more respectfully and, bending his head down from above, would ask: is there anything you need? After dinner the gentleman drank a cup of coffee and sat down on a sofa, placing behind his back a pillow, which in Russian taverns, instead of elastic wool, is stuffed with something extremely resembling brick and cobblestone. Then he began to yawn and ordered to be shown to his room, where, having lain down, he fell asleep for two hours. Having rested, he wrote on a scrap of paper, at the request of the tavern waiter, his rank, name and surname for reporting to the proper place, to the police. On the paper the waiter, descending the stairs, read syllable by syllable the following:"Collegiate Councillor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, landowner, on his own business". While the waiter was still spelling out the note syllable by syllable, Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov himself set off to look at the city, which he seemed to be satisfied with, for he found that the city in no way yielded to other provincial cities: the yellow paint on the stone houses struck the eye forcefully and the gray on the wooden ones darkened modestly. The houses were one, two, and one and a half stories, with an eternal mezzanine, very beautiful, in the opinion of provincial architects. In places these houses seemed lost amid a street as wide as a field and endless wooden fences; in places they huddled together, and here there was noticeably more movement of people and liveliness. There were almost rain-washed signs with pretzels and boots, here and there with drawn blue trousers and the signature of some Arshavsky tailor; where a shop with caps and forage caps and the inscription: "Foreigner Vasily Fedorov"; where there was drawn a billiard room with two players in tailcoats, the kind worn at our theaters by guests entering the stage in the last act. The players were depicted with aimed cues, arms somewhat turned back and slanting legs, just having made an entrechat in the air. Under all this was written: "And here's the establishment". Here and there simple tables stood right on the street with nuts, soap and gingerbreads resembling soap; where a tavern with a drawn fat fish and a fork stuck in it. Most often, however, one noticed darkened double-headed state eagles, which were now already replaced by a laconic inscription: "Drinking house". The pavement everywhere was rather poor. He peeped into the city garden too, which consisted of thin trees, poorly taken, with supports at the bottom in the form of triangles, very beautifully painted with green oil paint. However, although these little trees were no taller than reeds, they were mentioned in the newspapers in the description of the illumination, that "our city was adorned, thanks to the care of the civil governor, with a garden consisting of shady, wide-branched trees, giving coolness on a hot day", and that "it was very touching to see how the hearts of citizens trembled in excess of gratitude and poured forth streams of tears as a sign of recognition to the lord governor". Having inquired in detail of the sentry where one could go closer, if needed, to the cathedral, to the government offices, to the governor, he set off to look at the river flowing through the middle of the city, on the way tore off a poster nailed to a post, so that, coming home, he could read it properly, looked intently at a woman of not bad appearance passing along the wooden sidewalk, followed by a boy in military livery with a bundle in his hand, and, having cast his eyes over everything once more, as if to remember well the position of the place, went home straight to his room, supported slightly on the stairs by the tavern servant. Having drunk his tea, he sat down at the table, ordered a candle to be brought, took the poster from his pocket, held it to the candle and began to read, squinting his right eye slightly. However, there was little remarkable in the poster: a drama by Mr. Kotzebue was being given, in which Rolla was played by Mr. Poplvine, Kora by Miss Zyablova, the other characters were even less remarkable; nevertheless he read them all, even got to the price of the pit and learned that the poster was printed in the printing house of the provincial administration, then turned it over to the other side: to see if there was anything there, but, finding nothing, rubbed his eyes, folded it neatly and put it in his casket, where he was accustomed to putting everything that came his way. The day, it seems, was concluded with a portion of cold veal, a bottle of sour cabbage soup and sound sleep in the full manner of a pump wrap, as they say in some places of the vast Russian state.

The whole next day was devoted to visits; the newcomer set off to pay visits to all the city dignitaries. He was respectfully with the governor, who, as it turned out, like Chichikov was neither fat nor thin, had an Anna on his neck, and they even said he had been presented for a star; however, he was a great good-natured fellow and even sometimes embroidered on tulle himself. Then he went to the vice-governor,Then he visited the prosecutor, the chairman of the chamber, the police chief, the tax farmer, the head of the state factories… it is a pity that it is somewhat difficult to mention all the mighty of this world; but it is enough to say that the visitor displayed extraordinary diligence with regard to visits: he even appeared to pay his respects to the inspector of the medical administration and the city architect. And then for a long time he sat in his carriage, thinking of whom else to visit, but there were no more officials left in the city. In conversations with these rulers he was very skillfully able to flatter each one. To the governor he hinted somehow in passing that entering his province was like entering paradise, the roads everywhere were like velvet, and that those governments which appoint wise officials are worthy of great praise. To the police chief he said something very flattering about the city watchmen; and in conversations with the vice-governor and the chairman of the chamber, who were still only state councillors, he even made a mistake twice by saying "your excellency," which pleased them greatly. As a result of this, the governor invited him to come to his house that very day for an evening party, and the other officials, for their part, some invited him to dinner, some to boston, some for a cup of tea.

About himself the visitor, as it seemed, avoided speaking much; if he did speak, it was in some general phrases, with noticeable modesty, and his conversation in such cases took on somewhat bookish turns: that he was an insignificant worm of this world and not worthy of much concern, that he had experienced much in his lifetime, suffered in service for the sake of truth, had many enemies who even attempted his life, and that now, wishing to find peace, he seeks finally to choose a place of residence, and that, having arrived in this city, he considered it his absolute duty to pay his respects to its first officials. That is all that was learned in the city about this new person, who very soon did not fail to show himself at the governor's evening party. Preparation for this party took more than two hours, and here the visitor displayed such attention to his toilet as is not seen everywhere. After a short afternoon nap he ordered water brought and rubbed his cheeks with soap for an extraordinarily long time, supporting them from within with his tongue; then, taking a towel from the shoulder of the inn servant, he wiped his full face on all sides, beginning from behind his ears and snorting twice first into the face of the inn servant. Then he put on a shirt front before the mirror, plucked out two hairs that had grown from his nose and immediately after that found himself in a frock coat of lingonberry color with a sparkle. Thus dressed, he rolled along in his own carriage through the endlessly wide streets, illuminated by meager lighting from the occasional glimmering lamps. However, the governor's house was lit as if for a ball; a carriage with lanterns, two gendarmes before the entrance, coachmen's shouts in the distance – in short, everything as it should be. Upon entering the hall, Chichikov had to squint for a moment, because the glitter from the candles, lamps and ladies' dresses was terrible. Everything was flooded with light. Black frock coats flashed and moved about separately and in clusters here and there, as flies move on white shining sugar loaf during the hot July summer, when an old housekeeper chops and divides it into sparkling pieces before an open window; children all look, gathered around, watching curiously the movements of her stiff hands raising the hammer, and aerial squadrons of flies, lifted by the light air, fly in boldly, like full masters, and, taking advantage of the old woman's poor eyesight and the sun troubling her eyes, cover the delicious pieces some scattered, some in thick clusters. Satiated by the rich summer, which in any case at every step sets out delicious dishes, they flew in not at all to eat, but only to show themselves, to walk back and forth over the sugar heap, to rub one against another their hind or front legs, or scratch themselves under their wings, or, stretching out both front paws, rub them over their heads, turn around and fly away again, and fly in again with new troublesome squadrons. Chichikov had scarcely had time to look around,as he was already seized by the arm by the governor, who introduced him right there to the governor's wife. The visiting guest did not demean himself here either: he said some compliment, quite proper for a man of middle years, holding a rank neither too high nor too low. When the formed pairs of dancers pressed everyone against the wall, he, clasping his hands behind his back, looked at them for about two minutes very attentively. Many ladies were well dressed and fashionable, others dressed in whatever God sent to the provincial city. The men here, as everywhere, were of two kinds: some thin ones, who were all fawning around the ladies; some of them were of such a kind that it was difficult to distinguish them from Petersburg men, had equally well-considered and tastefully combed sideburns or simply respectable, very smoothly shaven oval faces, equally carelessly approached the ladies, equally spoke French and amused the ladies just as in Petersburg. The other kind of men consisted of fat ones or such as Chichikov, that is, not exactly too fat, but not thin either. These, on the contrary, squinted and backed away from the ladies and only looked around to see if the governor's servant had not set up a green table for whist somewhere. Their faces were full and round, some even had warts, some were pockmarked, they did not wear their hair in tufts, nor in curls, nor in the manner of "devil take me," as the French say, – their hair was either cut short or slicked down, and their facial features were more rounded and strong. These were the honorable officials in the city. Alas! the fat ones know better how to manage their affairs in this world than the thin ones. The thin ones serve more on special assignments or only exist on paper and waver back and forth; their existence is somehow too light, airy, and altogether unreliable. The fat ones never occupy indirect positions, but all direct ones, and if they sit down somewhere, they sit down reliably and firmly, so that sooner would the place crack and bend under them, but they will not fly off. They do not love outward splendor; their frock coat is not as skillfully tailored as that of the thin ones, but in their coffers there is divine grace. A thin one in three years does not have a single soul not pawned to a pawnbroker; a fat one calmly, look – and suddenly a house appeared somewhere at the end of the city, bought in his wife's name, then at the other end another house, then near the city a small village, then a village with all its lands. Finally the fat one, having served God and the state, having earned universal respect, leaves service, moves, and becomes a landowner, a glorious Russian gentleman, a hospitable man, and lives, and lives well. And after him again the thin heirs squander, according to Russian custom, all their father's property by courier. One cannot conceal that almost this kind of reflection occupied Chichikov at the time when he was observing society, and the consequence of this was that he finally joined the fat ones, where he met almost all familiar faces: the prosecutor with very black thick eyebrows and a left eye somewhat winking, as if to say: "Come, brother, to another room, I'll tell you something there," – a man, however, serious and taciturn; the postmaster, a short man, but a wit and a philosopher; the chairman of the chamber, a very sensible and amiable man, – who all greeted him as an old acquaintance, to which Chichikov bowed somewhat sideways, but not without pleasantness. There he also became acquainted with a very courteous and polite landowner Manilov and somewhat awkward-looking Sobakevich, who stepped on his foot the first time, saying: "I beg your pardon." Then they thrust a card for whist into his hands, which he accepted with an equally polite bow. They sat down at the green table and did not get up until supper. All conversation completely ceased, as always happens when one finally devotes oneself to a serious occupation. Although the postmaster was very talkative, but he too, taking cards in his hands, immediately assumed a thoughtful expression on his face, covered his upper lip with his lower one and maintained this position throughout the game. Coming out of a hand, he would strike the table firmly with his hand, saying, if it was a lady: "Go,""old popadia!", if the king: "Go, Tambov peasant!"
DEAD SOULS
By Nikolai Gogol
PART ONE
CHAPTER TWO

The visiting gentleman had been living in the town for more than a week, attending evening parties and dinners, and thus spending, as they say, a very pleasant time. Finally, he resolved to transfer his visits to the countryside and visit the landowners Manilov and Sobakevich, to whom he had given his word. Perhaps some other, more substantial reason, a more serious matter, closer to his heart, prompted him to do so... But the reader will learn about all this gradually and in due course, if only he has the patience to read the proposed tale, which is very long and will, as it were, expand wider and more spaciously as it approaches the conclusion that crowns the work. The coachman Selifan was ordered early in the morning to harness the horses to the familiar britchka; Petruska was ordered to remain at home, look after the room and the trunk. It will be useful for the reader to become acquainted with these two serfs of our hero. Although, of course, they are not particularly notable figures, and what one might call secondary or even tertiary characters, and although the main mechanisms and springs of the poem do not rest upon them and scarcely touch and only lightly catch on them—but the author loves exceedingly to be thorough in all things and, though a Russian himself, wishes to be as meticulous as a German. This will not, however, take much time and space, because not much need be added to what the reader already knows—that is, that Petruska wore a rather loose brown coat from his master's shoulder and had, according to the custom of men of his station, a large nose and lips. His character was more silent than talkative; he even had a noble inclination toward enlightenment—that is, reading books—and troubled himself not at all about their contents: it made no difference to him whether he read about the adventures of a lover, a simple primer, or a prayer book—he read everything with equal attention; if chemistry had come his way, he would not have refused it either. What pleased him was not what he read, but the very act of reading, or rather, the process of reading itself—that words somehow constantly emerged from letters, which sometimes the devil knew what meant. This reading was conducted mostly in a reclining position in the anteroom, on a bed and mattress, which had become worn and thin as a pancake from this arrangement. Besides his passion for reading, he had two other habits that constituted his other characteristic traits: he slept without undressing, just as he was, in his coat, and he always carried about with him a certain peculiar air, his own particular smell, reminiscent somewhat of a lived-in room, so that it was enough for him to set up his bed somewhere, even in a previously uninhabited room, and drag his overcoat and belongings there, and it would already seem that people had been living in that room for ten years. Chichikov, being a very fastidious man and even in some cases rather particular, having drawn a breath of fresh air to his nose in the morning, would only frown and shake his head, saying: "You, brother, the devil knows what, are you sweating? You should at least go to a bathhouse." To which Petruska would say nothing and would try to busy himself with something; or would approach with a whip the master's coat hanging there, or would simply tidy something up. What he thought during those moments of silence—perhaps he said to himself: "And you, however, are fine, aren't you tired of repeating the same thing forty times"—God knows; it is difficult to know what a house serf thinks when his master is giving him instruction. Thus, what can be said for the first time about Petruska. The coachman Selifan was a completely different man... But the author is quite ashamed to occupy the readers' attention so long with people of the lower classes, knowing from experience how reluctant they are to become acquainted with lower stations. Such is the Russian: a strong passion to become acquainted with someone who is at least one rank above him, and a hat-tip acquaintance with a count or prince is better for him than close friendly relations. The author even fears for his hero, who is only a collegiate councillor. Titular councillors, perhaps, will become acquainted with him, but those who have already risen to general ranks, heaven knows, may perhaps cast even one of those contemptuous glances cast proudly by a man upon all who crawl at his feet, or, what is worse, may pass by with an indifference fatal to the author. But however distressing one or the other may be, still, one must return to the hero. Thus, having given the necessary orders the evening before, having awakened very early in the morning, having washed and dried himself from head to foot with a damp sponge—which was done only on Sundays, and that day happened to be a Sunday—having shaved so that his cheeks became real satin in respect to smoothness and shine, having put on a frock coat of a cranberry color with a spark, and then an overcoat with large beaver fur, he descended the stairs, supported under the arm now from one side, then from the other by the innkeeper's servant, and sat in the britchka. With a roar, the britchka drove out from under the gates of the inn onto the street. A passing priest removed his hat; several boys in soiled shirts stretched out their hands, saying: "Master, give something to the orphans!" The coachman, noticing that one of them was particularly eager to leap onto the back of the carriage, struck him with his whip, and the britchka began to bounce over the cobblestones. Not without pleasure did he see in the distance the striped barrier, which indicated that the pavement, like any other such inconvenience, would soon end; and after several more quite forceful bumps of his head against the carriage frame, Chichikov was finally carried along on soft earth. Scarcely had the town receded behind when, according to our custom, nonsense and rubbish began to appear on both sides of the road: hummocks, small spruce groves, low sparse bushes of young pine trees, burnt trunks of old ones, wild heather, and such other rubbish. Stretched out in a line were villages, in their construction resembling old stacked firewood, covered with gray roofs with carved wooden decorations beneath them in the form of hanging embroidered towel-holders. Several peasants, as usual, yawned, sitting on benches before gates in their sheepskin coats. Women with thick faces and bound breasts looked out from upper windows; from lower ones a calf or a pig thrust out its blind snout. In short, familiar views. Having driven the fifteenth verst, he recalled that according to Manilov's words, his village should be here, but the sixteenth verst flew past, and still the village was not to be seen, and had it not been for two peasants he happened to meet, they would scarcely have managed to find the right road. When asked how far the village of Zamanilova was, the peasants removed their hats, and one of them, who was somewhat cleverer and wore his beard in a wedge shape, answered:

"Perhaps it's Manilovka, not Zamanilova?"

"Well yes, Manilovka."

"Manilovka! So when you drive another verst, then, that is to say, turn straight to the right."

"To the right?" the coachman answered.

"To the right," said the peasant. "That will be the road to Manilovka; and there is no Zamanilova at all. Its name, that is, it's called Manilovka, and Zamanilova is not here at all. You'll see a stone house, two stories, a manor house, where the master himself lives, that is. That's your Manilovka, and there's no Zamanilova here at all and never was."

They went off in search of Manilovka. After driving two versts, they encountered a turn to a country road, but it seemed they had already traveled two, three, and four versts, and still the two-story stone house was not to be seen. Then Chichikov recalled that when a friend invites you to his village at a distance of fifteen versts, it really means there are thirty versts there truly. The village of Manilovka could not have attracted many by its location. The manor house stood alone on a hill, that is, on an elevation open to every wind that cared to blow; the slope of the hill on which it stood was clothed in mown grass. On it were scattered in the English fashion two or three flowerbeds with lilac bushes and yellow acacias; five or six small birches with thin, small-leaved tops rose here and there in little clumps. Under two of them stood a small arbor with a flat green cupola, wooden blue columns, and an inscription: "Temple of Solitary Meditation"; below was a pond covered with green, which, however, is nothing unusual in the English gardens of Russian landowners. At the foot of this elevation, and partially along its slope, stretched gray log huts, which our hero, for reasons unknown to him, suddenly began to count and counted more than two hundred; nowhere among them grew a single tree or any vegetation; everywhere only logs met the eye. Two women, having arranged their clothes picturesquely and tucked themselves up on all sides, waded knee-deep into the pond, dragging a torn fishing net along after them by means of two wooden poles, where two entangled crabs were visible and a bream glimmered; the women seemed to be in some dispute with each other and were quarreling over something. In the distance, a pine forest loomed with a dull blue-gray color. Even the weather proved most opportune: the day was neither clear nor gloomy, but of some light-gray color, such as is found only on old uniforms of garrison soldiers—that peaceful force, though somewhat intoxicated on Sundays. To complete the picture, there was no lack of a rooster, that harbinger of changeable weather, who, despite his head being pecked through to the very brain by other roosters in the known affairs of roguery, cried out very loudly and even flapped his wings, bedraggled like old burlap. Approaching the courtyard, Chichikov noticed the master himself on the porch, standing in a green cloth coat, with his hand raised to his forehead like a sunshade over his eyes, to better examine the approaching carriage. As the britchka drew closer to the porch, his eyes became merrier and his smile grew wider and wider.

"Pavel Ivanovich!" he finally cried out when Chichikov was getting out of the carriage. "At last you have remembered us!"

Both friends kissed each other firmly, and Manilov led his guest into a room. Although the time during which they would pass through the halls, the anteroom, and the dining room was rather short, let us try to see if we cannot make use of it somehow and say something about the master of the house. But here the author must confess that such an undertaking is very difficult. It is far easier to depict characters of great magnitude: there you simply fling colors with all your hand upon canvas—black burning eyes overhanging brows, a forehead cut by wrinkles, a black or scarlet cloak, like fire, thrown over the shoulder—and the portrait is done; but those gentlemen, of whom there are many in the world, who at first glance resemble one another very much, and yet when you look more closely, you see many most elusive peculiarities—those gentlemen are terribly difficult to portray. Here you must strain your attention considerably before you make all the subtle, almost invisible features appear before you, and in general you must look much more deeply with an eye already schooled in the science of investigation.

Only God could say what sort of character Manilov had. There is a class of people known by the name: people so-so, neither one thing nor another, neither Bogdan in the town nor Selifan in the village, according to the saying. Perhaps Manilov should be classed with them. To the eye, he was a presentable man; his facial features were not devoid of pleasantness, but into that pleasantness it seemed too much sugar had been introduced; in his gestures and turns of phrase was something ingratiating, courting favor and acquaintance. He smiled enticingly, was fair-haired, with blue eyes. In the first minute of conversation with him you cannot help but say: "What a pleasant and kind man!" In the next minute you will say nothing, and in the third you will say: "The devil only knows what!" and walk away; if you do not walk away, you will feel deadly boredom. From him you will never hear any lively word, or even an insolent one such as you can hear from almost anyone if you touch upon a subject that excites them. Everyone has his own pet subject: one's is hunting dogs; another imagines he is a great lover of music and marvelously understands all the deep passages in it; a third is a master at dining well; a fourth wants to play a role even slightly higher than the one assigned him; a fifth, with more limited desires, dreams of strolling in public with a flügel-adjutant, to show off to his friends, acquaintances, and even strangers; a sixth is already endowed with such hands as feel an almost supernatural desire to bend the corner of a diamond ace or deuce, while the hands of a seventh positively itch to bring order somewhere, to get closer to the personality of a station master or a postillion—in short, everyone has his own something, but Manilov had nothing. At home he spoke very little and mostly meditated and thought, but what he thought about, only God knew. One could not say that he occupied himself with his estate; he did not even ride out to the fields; the estate ran itself somehow. When the steward said: "It would be good, sir, if we did this or that," he would usually answer: "Yes, that's not bad," smoking his pipe, a habit he had acquired during his military service, where he was considered the most modest, the most delicate, and the most educated of officers. "Yes, exactly not bad," he would repeat. When a peasant came to him and, scratching the back of his head, said: "Sir, permit me to go work, to earn myself some money," he would say: "Go," smoking his pipe, and it never occurred to him that the peasant was going to get drunk. Sometimes, looking from the porch at the courtyard and the pond, he would speak of how good it would be if they could suddenly run an underground passage from the house or build a stone bridge over the pond, with shops on both sides, where merchants could sit and sell various small goods needed by the peasants. At these moments his eyes would become extremely tender and his face would take on the most contented expression; however, all these projects ended only in words. In his study there always lay some book, with a bookmark placed on the fourteenth page, which he had been constantly reading for two years. In his house something was always lacking: in the drawing room stood beautiful furniture, upholstered with elegant silk material, which certainly cost a considerable sum; but there was not enough material for two armchairs, and these stood upholstered simply with burlap; however, for several years the master had invariably warned his guest: "Do not sit on these armchairs, they are not yet ready." In one room there was no furniture at all, although it had been said in the first days after marriage: "My dear, we must try tomorrow to put at least some temporary furniture in this room." In the evening, a very elegant candlestick of dark bronze with three ancient graces and a pearl-decorated shield would be placed on the table, and next to it would be set some simple copper invalid, lame, bent to the side and covered all over with tallow, though neither the master, nor the mistress, nor the servants noticed this. His wife... however, they were perfectly satisfied with each other. Although more than eight years had passed since their marriage, each of them still brought the other either a piece of apple, or a sweet, or a nut, and said in a touchingly tender voice expressing complete love: "Open your mouth wide, my dear, I shall put this morsel in it." It goes without saying that the mouth opened on such occasions very gracefully. For her birthday, surprises were prepared: some little beaded case for toothpicks. And very often, sitting on the sofa, suddenly, for no apparent reason, one, leaving his pipe, and the other her work, if she happened to be holding anything at that moment, they would press upon each other such a languid and lengthy kiss that during it one could easily smoke a small straw cigar. In short, they were, what one might call, happy. Of course, one might remark that in the house there are many other occupations besides prolonged kisses and surprises, and many different needs could be attended to. Why, for instance, is there silly and thoughtless cooking in the kitchen? Why is there rather little in the storeroom? Why is the housekeeper a thief? Why are the servants so slovenly and drunkards? Why do all the household servants sleep mercilessly and idle the rest of the time? But all these are base matters, and Manilov had been well educated. And good education, as is well known, is obtained in boarding schools. And in boarding schools, as is well known, three main subjects form the foundation of human virtues: French, necessary for family happiness; the piano, for providing pleasant moments to one's spouse; and, finally, the properly domestic part: the knitting of purses and other surprises. However, there are various refinements and changes in methods, especially nowadays; all this depends more on the wisdom and abilities of the proprietresses of the boarding schools themselves. In some boarding schools it happens thus: first the piano, then French, then the domestic part. And sometimes it is this way: first the domestic part, that is, the knitting of surprises, then French, then the piano. There are various methods. It is also worth noting that Manilov... but, I confess, I am very afraid of speaking about ladies, and besides, it is time for me to return to our heroes, who had been standing for several minutes before the drawing-room door, mutually urging each other to enter first.

"Please, do not trouble yourself for me; I shall go after," said Chichikov.

"No, Pavel Ivanovich, no, you are the guest," said Manilov, pointing to the door.

"Do not trouble yourself, please, do not trouble yourself. Please, go ahead," said Chichikov.

"No, excuse me, I shall not allow such a pleasant, educated guest to go after me."

"Why educated?... Please, go ahead."

"Well then, excuse me, but do go ahead."

"But why should I?"

"Well, exactly for that reason!" said Manilov with a pleasant smile.

Finally both friends entered the door sideways and somewhat pressed against each other.

"Permit me to introduce my wife," said Manilov. "My dear! Pavel Ivanovich!"

Chichikov indeed saw a lady whom he had completely failed to notice while bowing to Manilov at the door. She was not unattractive, dressed tastefully. On her sat well a thin silk capote of a pale color; a thin small hand had hastily thrown something on the table and clasped a batiste handkerchief with embroidered corners. She rose from the sofa on which she had been sitting; Chichikov approached her hand with not without pleasure. Manilov's wife, speaking somewhat with a lisp, said that he had gladdened them greatly by his arrival and that her husband not a day passed without him speaking of him.

"Yes," added Manilov, "she was always asking me: 'But why doesn't your friend come?' 'Wait, my dear, he will come.' And so you have finally deigned to visit us. What a joy it is... a May day... the birthday of the heart..."

Chichikov, hearing that it had reached the point of the birthday of the heart, was somewhat embarrassed and answered modestly that he had neither a loud name nor even a notable rank.

"You have everything," interrupted Manilov with the same pleasant smile, "you have everything, even more than that."

"What did you think of our town?" asked Manilov's wife. "Did you spend time there pleasantly?"

"A very good town, an excellent town," answered Chichikov, "and I spent the time very pleasantly: the society is most courteous."

"And how did you find our governor?" asked Manilov's wife.

"A very respectable and charming man, is he not?" added Manilov.

"Absolutely," said Chichikov, "a most respectable man. And how he has understood his position, how he comprehends it! One must wish for more such men."

"How he can, you know, receive anyone, maintain delicacy in his conduct," added Manilov with a smile and from pleasure he almost completely closed his eyes like a cat when you tickle its ears slightly with your finger.

"A very courteous and pleasant man," continued Chichikov, "and what an artist! I could never have supposed it. How beautifully he embroiders various household patterns! He showed me a purse of his work: few ladies could embroider so skillfully."

"And the vice-governor, is he not a charming man?" said Manilov, again squinting his eyes slightly.

"Very, very worthy," answered Chichikov.

"Well, permit me, but what did you think of the police master? Is he not a very pleasant man?"

"Extraordinarily pleasant, and what an intelligent, what a well-read man! We played cards with him and the prosecutor and the chairman of the chamber until the very late roosters; very, very worthy man."

"And what is your opinion of the police master's wife?" added Manilov's wife. "Is she not a most charming woman?"

"Oh, she is one of the most worthy women I know," answered Chichikov.

They did not skip over the chairman of the chamber, the postmaster, and thus went over almost all the officials of the town, who all turned out to be the most worthy of men.

"Do you always spend your time in the country?" Chichikov finally asked in his turn.

"Mostly in the country," answered Manilov, "though we come to town just to see educated people. You become uncivilized, you know, if you live all the time shut up."

"True, very true," said Chichikov.

"Of course," continued Manilov, "it would be different if the neighborhood were good, if, for instance, there were such a person with whom one could, in a sense, speak of courtesy, of good manners, follow some such science, so to speak, to stir up the soul, give it, as it were, some uplift... Here he wanted to express something more, but, noticing that he had talked rather too much, merely waved his hand in the air and continued: "Then, of course, the country and solitude would have very many pleasures. But decidedly there is no one... Only occasionally one reads the 'Son of the Fatherland.'"

Chichikov entirely agreed with this, adding that nothing could be more pleasant than to live in solitude, enjoy the spectacle of nature and sometimes read a book...

"But you know," added Manilov, "it is all if there is no friend with whom to share..."

"Oh, that is true, that is absolutely true!" interrupted Chichikov. "What are all the treasures of the world then! 'Have not money, have good people for company,' said a sage."

"And you know, Pavel Ivanovich!" said Manilov, showing in his face an expression not only sweet but even sickly-sweet, like that mixture which a clever society doctor has oversweetened, imagining thereby to please the patient. "Then you feel a certain, in a sense, spiritual pleasure... Just as, for example, now, when chance has given me the happiness, one might say the exemplary one, to converse with you and enjoy your pleasant conversation..."

"How can I! What kind of pleasant conversation... I am a man of no consequence, nothing more," answered Chichikov.

"Oh! Pavel Ivanovich, permit me to be frank: I would gladly give half of my entire fortune to possess even a part of those excellencies which you possess!..."

"On the contrary, I would consider it for myself the greatest..."

It is unknown to what extent the mutual effusion of feelings of both friends might have extended, had not a servant who entered announced that dinner was ready.

"I beg you earnestly," said Manilov. "You will excuse us if we do not have such a dinner as you would find in palaces and in the capital; we have simply, according to Russian custom, cabbage soup, but from a pure heart. I beg you earnestly."

Here they again disputed for some time as to who should enter first, and finally Chichikov entered sideways into the dining room.

In the dining room there were already two boys, Manilov's sons, who were at the age when children are already seated at table, but still on high chairs. With them stood a teacher, who bowed politely and with a smile. The hostess seated herself at her soup bowl; the guest was placed between the master and the mistress; a servant tied napkins around the children's necks.

"What lovely children," said Chichikov, looking at them. "And how old are they?"

"The elder is eight years old, and the younger only turned six yesterday," said Manilov's wife.

"Themistocles!" said Manilov, turning to the elder, who was trying to free his chin, which had been tied by the lackey in a napkin.

Chichikov raised his eyebrows somewhat upon hearing such a partly Greek name, which Manilov had, for some reason unknown, given an ending in "us," but tried that same moment to bring his face to its ordinary position.

"Themistocles, tell me, what is the best city in France?"

Here the teacher turned all his attention to Themistocles and seemed as though he wanted to jump into his eyes, but finally completely calmed down and nodded his head when Themistocles said: "Paris."

"And what is the best city with us?" asked Manilov again.

The teacher again prepared his attention.

"Petersburg," answered Themistocles.

"And what other one?"

"Moscow," answered Themistocles.

"A clever lad, my dear!" said Chichikov to this. "Tell me, however..." he continued, turning with a certain air of surprise to the Manilovs, "at such a tender age and already such knowledge! I must tell you, this child will have great abilities."

"Oh, you do not yet know him," answered Manilov. "He has remarkably much wit. As for the younger one, Alcides, he is not so quick, but this one, if he meets anything, an insect, a tiny creature, his little eyes at once begin to run; he will run after it and immediately turn his attention. I intend him for the diplomatic service. Themistocles," he continued, turning to him again, "do you want to be an ambassador?"

"I do," answered Themistocles, chewing bread and shaking his head from side to side.

At this moment the lackey standing behind wiped the ambassador's nose, and did it very well; otherwise a considerable drop would have fallen into the soup. Conversation at the table began about the pleasure of a peaceful life, interrupted by remarks from the hostess about the city theater and the actors. The teacher watched the conversation very attentively and, as soon as he noticed that they were about to smile, he would at that instant open his mouth and laugh with zeal. Probably he was a grateful man and wanted in this way to repay his master for his good treatment. Once, however, his face took on a stern look, and he knocked sharply on the table, fixing his gaze on the children sitting opposite. This was appropriate, because Themistocles had bitten Alcides on the ear, and Alcides, squeezing his eyes shut and opening his mouth, was ready to weep in the most pitiful manner, but, feeling that for this he might easily lose his dish, brought his mouth back to its former position and began, with tears, to gnaw on a mutton bone, from which both his cheeks shone with grease. The hostess very often addressed Chichikov with the words: "You are not eating anything, you have taken very little." To which Chichikov answered each time: "I am most grateful; I am full; pleasant conversation is better than any dish."

They had already risen from the table. Manilov was extremely pleased and, supporting his guest's back with his hand, was about to conduct him in such a manner into the drawing room, when suddenly the guest announced with a very significant air that he intended to speak with him about a most necessary matter.

"In that case, permit me to ask you into my study," said Manilov and led him into a small room with windows facing the bluish forest. "Here is my corner," said Manilov.

"A pleasant room," said Chichikov, glancing around it.

The room was indeed not without charm: the walls were painted with some bluish paint like a grayish shade, four chairs, one armchair, a table on which lay a book with the bookmark we already had occasion to mention, several written papers, but above all there was tobacco in various forms: in pouches and in tobacco boxes, and finally it was simply heaped in a pile on the table. On his windows too were placed heaps of ashes knocked from pipes, arranged without care but in very neat rows. It was evident that this sometimes afforded the master a way to pass the time.

"Permit me to ask you to sit in this armchair," said Manilov. "You will be more comfortable here."

"Permit me to sit on a chair."

"Permit me not to permit you to do so," said Manilov with a smile. "This armchair is, so to speak, designated for a guest: for better or worse, but you must sit."

Chichikov sat down.

"Permit me to treat you to a pipe."

"No, I don't smoke," answered Chichikov kindly and as though with an air of regret.

"Why not?" said Manilov also kindly and with an air of regret.

"I have not formed the habit; I am afraid; they say a pipe dries one out."

"Permit me to remark that this is a prejudice. I even think that smoking a pipe is much healthier than snuffing tobacco. In our regiment there was a sublieutenant, a most excellent and educated man, who never took a pipe out of his mouth not only at table but even, with your permission, in all other places. And now he is over forty, but, thank God, he is so healthy as one could not be better."

Chichikov remarked that this, indeed, happens, and that nature finds many things inexplicable even to a broad mind.

"But permit me first one request..." said Chichikov in a voice in which sounded some strange or almost strange expression, and then, it is unknown why, he looked back. "How long have you not submitted a census report?"

"Oh, quite a long time; or rather, I do not recall."

"How many of your peasants have died since that time?"

"And I cannot know; I think for that you must ask the steward. Hey, man! Call the steward; he should be here today."

The steward appeared. He was a man of about forty, shaven-bearded, dressed in a frock coat, and apparently living a very comfortable life, for his face looked swollen and full, and a yellowish complexion of his skin and small eyes showed that he knew very well what feather beds and mattresses were. One could immediately see that he had made his career as all manor stewards do: he was first simply a literate boy in the house, then married some Agasha-housekeeper, a favorite of the mistress, became a housekeeper himself, and then a steward. And having become a steward, he acted, of course, as all stewards do: he associated with those who were richer in the village, increased the levies on the poorer ones, rose at nine o'clock in the morning, waited for his samovar, and drank tea.

"Listen, my friend! how many peasants of ours have died since the census report was submitted?"

"How many? Many have died since then," said the steward and hiccupped, slightly covering his mouth with his hand, like a shield.

"Yes, I agree, I myself think so," picked up Manilov. "Yes, exactly, very many have died!" Then he turned to Chichikov and added: "Really, very many."

"But how many, for instance, in number?" asked Chichikov.

"Yes, how many in number?" picked up Manilov.

"How can one say in number? After all, it is unknown how many died; no one counted them."

"Yes, exactly," said Manilov, turning to Chichikov, "I also supposed there was great mortality; it is completely unknown how many died."

"Please," said Chichikov, "count them and make a detailed list of all of them by name."

"Yes, all by name," said Manilov.

The steward said, "I obey!" and left.

"But for what reason do you need this?" asked Manilov after the steward had gone.

This question seemed to trouble the guest; his face showed some strained expression, which even caused him to blush—a strain to express something not entirely submissive to words. And indeed, Manilov finally heard such strange and extraordinary things as human ears had never heard before.

"You ask for what reason?" said Chichikov. "The reason is this: I would like to purchase peasants..." he said, stammering and not finishing his speech.

"But permit me to ask you," said Manilov, "how do you wish to purchase peasants: with land or simply for export, that is, without land?"

"No, it is not that I want to purchase peasants exactly," said Chichikov, "I wish to have dead ones..."

"How's that, sir? Excuse me... I am somewhat hard of hearing; I heard some very strange word..."

"I propose to acquire the dead, who, however, would be listed according to the census as living," said Chichikov.

Manilov dropped his pipe stem with the pipe bowl to the floor and remained with his mouth open for several minutes. Both friends, who had been discussing the pleasures of friendly life, remained motionless, staring at each other like those portraits that used to hang in old times, one opposite the other on both sides of a mirror. Finally Manilov picked up the pipe with its stem and looked him in the face from below, trying to see if there was any smile on his lips, if he had made a joke; but there was nothing of the sort; on the contrary, his face seemed even more dignified than usual; then he wondered if the guest might have somehow gone mad, and looked at him with fear intently; but the guest's eyes were perfectly clear, there was no wild, restless fire in them such as runs in the eyes of a madman, everything was proper and in order. Try as Manilov might to think what to do and what to make of it, he could think of nothing else but to blow the remaining smoke from his mouth in a very thin stream.

"Thus, I would like to know whether you can transfer to me, cede to me, such ones—not alive in reality but alive according to legal form—or as you think best?"

But Manilov was so confused and flustered that he could only stare at him.

"It seems to me you are troubled?" remarked Chichikov.

"I?... no, that is not it," said Manilov, "but I cannot comprehend... forgive me... I certainly could not have received such brilliant education as is visible, so to speak, in all your movements; I do not have the high art of expression... Perhaps here... in this explanation just expressed by you... something else is concealed... Perhaps you were pleased to express yourself so for the beauty of the style?"

"No," said Chichikov, "no, I mean the subject as it is, that is, those souls who have truly already died."

Manilov was completely bewildered. He felt that he should do something, ask a question, but what question—the devil knows. He ended by blowing smoke again, but this time not from his mouth but through his nostrils.

"Thus, if there are no obstacles, then with God's blessing we could proceed to complete the deed of purchase," said Chichikov.

"How, a deed of purchase for dead souls?"

"Ah, no!" said Chichikov. "We shall write that they are alive, just as they actually stand in the census report. I am accustomed not to depart from civil laws in anything, though I have suffered for it in service; but excuse me: duty is a sacred matter for me, the law—I am silent before the law."

The last words pleased Manilov, but he still could not understand the substance of the matter, and instead of answering, he began to suck on his pipe stem so hard that it began to wheeze like a bassoon. It seemed as though he wanted to extract from it an opinion regarding such an unheard-of circumstance; but the pipe stem wheezed and nothing more.

"Perhaps you have some doubts?"

"Oh, forgive me, not at all. I do not speak as though I have any, that is, critical prejudice against you. But permit me to observe, will not this enterprise, or, so to speak, will not this negociation be unsuitable to civil statutes and the further prospects of Russia?"

Here Manilov, having made a certain movement of his head, looked very significantly at Chichikov's face, showing in all the features of his face and compressed lips such a deep expression as perhaps had never been seen on a human face, except perhaps on that of some overly intelligent minister, and then only in a moment of the most perplexing business.

But Chichikov spoke simply, saying that such an enterprise, or negociation, would in no way be unsuitable to civil statutes and the further prospects of Russia, and a minute later added that the treasury would even gain benefit, for it would receive the lawful fees.

"So you think?..."

"I think it will be good."

"Ah, if it is good, that is another matter: I have nothing against it," said Manilov and became completely calm.

"Now it remains to agree on the price."

"How on the price?" said Manilov again and stopped. "Do you really think I will take money for souls who have, in a certain sense, ended their existence? If such a fantastic wish has come to you, then for my part I transfer them to you without self-interest, and I will take the deed of purchase upon myself."

It would be a great reproach to the historian of the events presented if he omitted to say that joy overcame the guest after such words spoken by Manilov. However proper and judicious he was, he almost made a leap in the manner of a goat, which, as is known, is produced only in the strongest bursts of joy. He turned so forcefully in his chair that the woolen material upholstering the pillow burst; Manilov himself looked at him in some bewilderment. Prompted by gratitude, he poured forth so many thanks right then that Manilov became embarrassed, turned completely red, made a negative gesture with his head, and finally expressed that it was nothing at all, that he truly wished to show something of the heartfelt inclination, the magnetism of the soul, and that dead souls were, in a certain sense, perfect rubbish.

"Not rubbish at all," said Chichikov, pressing his hand. Here a very deep sigh was released. It seemed he was disposed to heartfelt outpourings; not without feeling and expression, he finally pronounced the following words: "If you only knew what a service you have rendered to a man without tribe or family with this, apparently, rubbish! And indeed, what have I not suffered? like a barque on savage waves... What persecutions have I not endured, what torments have I not experienced, and for what? For having preserved truth, for being pure in conscience, for extending a hand to a helpless widow and to an unfortunate orphan!..." Here he even wiped away a tear that had rolled down with his handkerchief.

Manilov was completely moved. Both friends pressed each other's hands for a long time and looked silently into each other's eyes, in which tears were visible. Manilov did not want to release the hand of our hero and continued to press it so ardently that he no longer knew how to free it. Finally, pulling it away gently, he said that it would be good to complete the deed of purchase as soon as possible and that it would be well if Manilov himself came to town. Then he took his hat and began to take his leave.

"What? You already want to leave?" said Manilov, suddenly coming to himself and becoming almost frightened.

At that moment Manilov's wife entered the study.

"Lizanka," said Manilov with a somewhat pitiful expression, "Pavel Ivanovich is leaving us!"

"Because we have tired Pavel Ivanovich," answered Manilov's wife.

"Madam!" said Chichikov, "here—here—" and he placed his hand on his heart—"yes, here will remain the pleasant memory of the time spent with you! and believe me, there would be no greater bliss for me than to live with you, if not in one house, then at least in the nearest neighborhood."

"And you know, Pavel Ivanovich," said Manilov, to whom such an idea very much pleased, "how it would really be good if we were to live together thus, under one roof, or in the shade of some elm tree, to philosophize about something, to delve into it..."

"Oh, that would be paradise!" said Chichikov, sighing. "Farewell, madam!" he continued, approaching Manilov's wife's hand. "Farewell, most respectable friend! Do not forget my request!"

"Oh, be assured!" answered Manilov. "I part with you for no more than two days."

Everyone came out into the dining room.

"Farewell, sweet little ones!" said Chichikov, seeing Alcides and Themistocles, who were occupied with some wooden hussar that had neither an arm nor a nose. "Farewell, my darlings. You will forgive me for not bringing you a gift, because, I confess, I did not even know if you lived in this world, but now, when I return, I shall certainly bring gifts. I'll bring you a sword; would you like a sword?"

"I would," answered Themistocles.

"And for you a drum; wouldn't you like a drum?" he continued, bending to Alcides.

"Dapped," answered Alcides softly and looking down.

"Very well, and I'll bring you a drum. Such a fine drum, and it will go like this: turrr... ru... tra-ta-ta, ta-ta-ta... Farewell, sweetheart! Farewell!" Then he kissed him on the head and turned to Manilov and his wife with a slight smile, with which people usually turn to parents, letting them know of the innocence of their children's wishes.

"Really, stay, Pavel Ivanovich!" said Manilov when they all came out on the porch. "Look what clouds."

"Those are just small clouds," answered Chichikov.

"But do you know the way to Sobakevich's?"

"I wanted to ask you about that."

"Permit me, I shall tell your coachman at once."

Then Manilov with the same courtesy explained the matter to the coachman and even said "you" to him once.

The coachman, hearing that he must pass two turns and turn at the third, said: "We'll find the way, your honor," and Chichikov drove off, accompanied for a long time by the bows and waving of a handkerchief of the hosts who were rising on tiptoe.

Manilov stood long on the porch, following with his eyes the departing carriage, and when it had completely disappeared from view, he still stood there, smoking his pipe. Finally he entered the room, sat in a chair, and gave himself over to meditation, feeling joy in his soul that he had rendered his guest some small pleasure. Then his thoughts imperceptibly shifted to other matters and finally flew God knows where. He thought about the happiness of friendly life, about how good it would be to live with a friend on the bank of some river, then through this river in his imagination a bridge began to be constructed, then an enormous house with such a high belvedere that one could see Moscow from there and drink tea in the open air in the evening and discuss some pleasant subjects. Then that he and Chichikov had arrived in some social circle in fine carriages, where they enchanted everyone with the pleasantness of their manners, and that the sovereign, having learned of their friendship, had presented them with the rank of general, and further, finally, God knows what, which he himself could not even unravel. Chichikov's strange request suddenly interrupted all his reveries. The thought of it somehow would not be digested in his head: however much he turned it over, he could not explain it to himself, and all the time he sat and smoked his pipe, which continued until supper itself.
