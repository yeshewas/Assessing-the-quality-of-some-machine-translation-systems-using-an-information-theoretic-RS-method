Anton Pavlovich Chekhov

The Lady with the Dog



I



     It was said that a new face had appeared on the promenade: a lady with a dog. Dmitry Dmitrich Gurov, who had already lived in Yalta for two weeks and was used to it there, also began to take an interest in new faces. Sitting in the pavilion at Verne’s, he saw a young lady walk along the promenade, a short blonde woman in a beret; a white Pomeranian ran after her.

     And then he met her in the city garden and in the park several times a day. She walked alone, still in that same beret, with the white Pomeranian; no one knew who she was, and she was simply called that: the lady with the dog.



     “If she’s here without her husband and without acquaintances,” Gurov reasoned, “then it wouldn’t hurt to get acquainted with her.”



     He was not yet forty, but he already had a twelve-year-old daughter and two sons in gymnasium. He had been married early, when he was still a second-year student, and now his wife seemed one and a half times older than him. She was a tall woman with dark eyebrows, upright, important, solid, and, as she called herself, a thinking person. She read a lot, did not write the hard sign in letters, called her husband not Dmitry, but Dimitry, and he secretly considered her shallow, narrow-minded, inelegant, feared her, and didn’t like being at home. He had started cheating on her long ago, did so often, and probably because of that, he almost always spoke ill of women, and whenever they were talked about in his presence, he called them that:



     — The lowest race!



     He thought he was sufficiently educated by bitter experience to call them whatever he wanted, but still, without the “lowest race” he could not live even two days. In the company of men, he was bored, uncomfortable, silent, cold; but when among women, he felt free and knew what to talk about with them and how to behave; even being silent with them was easy for him. There was something attractive, elusive in his appearance, in his character, in his entire nature that inclined women to him, lured them; he knew it. And some force in him also drew him to them.



     Repeated experience, indeed bitter experience, had long taught him that every rapprochement, which at first pleasantly diversifies life and seems like a sweet and easy adventure, for decent people, especially Muscovites, heavy to get moving, indecisive, inevitably grows into a whole task, extremely complicated, and the situation eventually becomes burdensome. But at every new meeting with an interesting woman, that experience somehow slipped from his memory, and he wanted to live, and everything seemed so simple and amusing.



     And so one evening he was having dinner in the garden while the lady in the beret walked slowly up to take the neighboring table. Her expression, her walk, her dress, her hairstyle told him that she was from respectable society, married, in Yalta for the first time and alone, that she was bored here… In the stories about the local immorality there was a lot of falsehood, he despised them and knew that such stories were mostly invented by people who would gladly sin themselves if they could, but when the lady sat at the neighboring table just three steps from him, he remembered those stories of easy conquests, trips to the mountains, and the tempting thought of a quick, fleeting connection, a romance with an unknown woman whose name and surname you do not know, suddenly possessed him.



     He softly called the Pomeranian to him and when it came up, wagged his finger at it. The Pomeranian growled. Gurov wagged again.



     The lady looked at him then immediately dropped her eyes.



     — He doesn’t bite, — she said, blushing.



     — May I give him a bone? — And when she nodded affirmatively, he asked kindly: — How long have you been in Yalta?



     — About five days.



     — And I’m already finishing my second week here.



     They were silent for a moment.



     — Time goes fast, but meanwhile it’s so boring here! — she said, not looking at him.



     — That’s just the usual thing to say, that it’s boring here. A commoner lives somewhere in Belev or Zhizdra — and he’s not bored, but he comes here and says: “Ah, boring! Ah, dust!” As if he came from Granada.



     She laughed.Then both continued to eat silently, like strangers; but after lunch, they walked side by side—and a playful, light conversation began between free, content people who did not care where to go or what to talk about. They walked and talked about how strangely the sea was lit; the water was a soft and warm lilac color, and a golden stripe from the moon ran across it. They spoke about how stuffy it was after the hot day. Gurov said he was a Muscovite, educated as a philologist but working in a bank; he had once prepared to sing in a private opera but gave it up and owned two houses in Moscow… From her, he learned that she had grown up in Petersburg but married in S., where she had lived for two years, that she would stay in Yalta for about another month, and perhaps her husband would come for her, as he also wanted to rest. She could not explain exactly where her husband worked—whether in the provincial administration or the provincial zemstvo board—and found that funny herself. Gurov also learned that her name was Anna Sergeyevna.

Then in his room, he thought about her, about how tomorrow she would probably meet with him. It should be that way. Going to bed, he remembered that she had recently been a student, studying, just like his daughter now, and recalled how much shyness and awkwardness there was in her laughter, in her talk with a stranger—it must have been the first time in her life that she was alone in such circumstances, when people followed her, looked at her, and only spoke to her with one secret purpose, which she could not help but guess. He remembered her delicate, weak neck and her beautiful gray eyes.

"There is something pathetic about her, after all," he thought and began to fall asleep.

II

A week passed after their meeting. It was a holiday. The rooms were stuffy, and dust whirled in the streets, hats were blown off. All day long, one felt thirsty, and Gurov often went into the pavilion and offered Anna Sergeyevna either water with syrup or ice cream. There was nowhere to go.

In the evening, when it calmed down a bit, they went to the pier to watch the steamer arrive. There were many people strolling on the pier; they had gathered to meet someone and held bouquets. Two features of the festive Yalta crowd stood out clearly: elderly ladies were dressed like young women, and there were many generals.

Due to the sea disturbance, the steamer arrived late, when the sun had already set, and before docking at the pier, it circled for a long time. Anna Sergeyevna looked through a lorgnette at the steamer and its passengers, as if searching for acquaintances, and when she turned to Gurov, her eyes shone. She talked a lot, and her questions were clipped, and she immediately forgot what she had asked; then she lost the lorgnette in the crowd.

The festive crowd dispersed; faces could no longer be seen, the wind died down completely, and Gurov and Anna Sergeyevna stood, as if waiting to see if anyone else would disembark from the steamer. Anna Sergeyevna was silent and sniffed the flowers without looking at Gurov.

"The weather has gotten better toward evening," he said. "Where shall we go now? Shall we go somewhere?"

She did not answer.

Then he looked at her closely and suddenly hugged her and kissed her on the lips, and he was enveloped by the scent and moisture of the flowers, and immediately he looked around timidly: had anyone seen?

"Let’s go to your place…" he said quietly.

And both walked quickly.

Her room was stuffy, smelling of perfume she had bought in a Japanese shop. Looking at her now, Gurov thought: "What encounters life has!" From the past, he retained memories of carefree, kind women, cheerful from love, grateful to him for happiness, even if very brief; and of such women—like, for example, his wife—who loved without sincerity, with excessive talking, mannerisms, hysteria, with an expression as if it were not love, not passion, but something more significant; and of two or three very beautiful, cold women whose faces suddenly flashed with a predatory look, a stubborn desire to take, to snatch from life more than it could give, and these were not young women, capricious, unreflective, controlling, not smart women, and when Gurov cooled toward them,their beauty stirred hatred in him, and the lace on their underwear then seemed to him like scales.

    But here was the same shyness, the awkwardness of inexperienced youth, an uneasy feeling; and there was an impression of confusion, as if someone had suddenly knocked on the door. Anna Sergeyevna, this "lady with the dog," took what had happened somewhat differently, very seriously, as if it were a fall of hers — so it seemed, and it was strange and inappropriate. Her features sank, withered, and long hair hung sadly around the sides of her face; she sat lost in thought in a melancholy pose, like a sinner in an old painting.

    "That’s not right," she said. "You don’t respect me anymore."

    On the table in the room was a watermelon. Gurov cut himself a slice and began to eat slowly. At least half an hour passed in silence.

    Anna Sergeyevna was touching; she radiated the purity of a decent, naive woman who had lived little; the lone candle burning on the table barely illuminated her face, but it was clear she was troubled at heart.

    "Why would I stop respecting you?" Gurov asked. "You don’t even know what you’re saying."

    "May God forgive me!" she said, and her eyes filled with tears. "It’s terrible."

    "You sound like you’re justifying yourself."

    "How can I justify myself? I’m a foolish, low woman; I despise myself and don’t think about justification. I haven’t deceived my husband, only myself. And not just now, but for a long time. My husband may be an honest, good person, but he’s a lackey! I don’t know what he does there, how he serves, I only know he’s a lackey. When I married him, I was twenty; I was restless with curiosity, I wanted something better; after all, there is — I told myself — another life. I wanted to live! To live and live... Curiosity burned me... you don’t understand this, but, I swear to God, I couldn’t control myself anymore, something was happening to me, I couldn’t be held back, I told my husband I was ill and came here... And here I wandered around like in a haze, like a madwoman... and so I became a cheap, lousy woman whom anyone can despise."

    Gurov was already bored listening; her naive tone irritated him, this repentance so unexpected and out of place; if it weren’t for the tears in her eyes, one might have thought she was joking or playing a role.

    "I don’t understand," he said quietly, "what do you want?"

    She buried her face against his chest and pressed close to him.

    "Believe me, believe me, I beg you..." she said. "I love an honest, pure life, and sin disgusts me; I don’t even know what I’m doing. Ordinary people say: the unclean has confused me. And now I can say this to myself — I have been confused by the unclean."

    "Enough, enough..." he mumbled.

    He looked into her still, frightened eyes, kissed her, spoke quietly and tenderly, and she gradually calmed down, and cheerfulness returned to her; they both began to laugh.

    Then, when they went out, there was not a soul on the embankment; the city with its cypresses looked completely dead, but the sea still roared and crashed against the shore; one boat rocked on the waves, and a sleepy lantern twinkled on it.

    They found a cabman and went to Oreanda.

    "I just saw your surname downstairs in the hall: the board says von Diederich," Gurov said. "Is your husband German?"

    "No, his grandfather was probably German, but he himself is Orthodox."

    In Oreanda, they sat on a bench not far from the church, looking down at the sea and remained silent. Yalta was barely visible through the morning mist; white clouds stood motionless on the mountain peaks. The leaves did not move on the trees, cicadas screamed, and the monotonous, muffled sound of the sea coming from below spoke of peace, of the eternal sleep that awaits us. The sea made that noise below when there was neither Yalta nor Oreanda; now it still makes that same indifferent, muffled noise when we are no longer here. And in this constancy, in complete indifference to the life and death of each of us, perhaps lies the guarantee of our eternal salvation, the continuous movement of life on earth, continuous perfection. Sitting next to the young woman who at dawn seemed so beautiful,Calm and enchanted by the fairy-tale setting—the sea, mountains, clouds, wide sky—Gurov thought about how, essentially, if you really think about it, everything in this world is beautiful, except for what we ourselves think and do when we forget the higher purposes of existence, about our human dignity.

Some man came up—he must have been a guard—looked at them and left. And this detail seemed so mysterious and also beautiful. You could see a steamboat arriving from Feodosia, lit by the morning dawn, already without lights.

"There's dew on the grass," Anna Sergeyevna said after a silent pause.

"Yes. Time to go home."

They returned to the city.

Then every noon they met on the embankment, had breakfast together, lunch, walked, admired the sea. She complained that she slept poorly and that her heart beat anxiously, asked the same questions over and over, troubled by jealousy or the fear that he didn’t respect her enough. And often in the park or garden, when no one was nearby, he would suddenly pull her close and kiss her passionately. Perfect idleness, these kisses in broad daylight, looking around nervously, afraid someone might see, the heat, the smell of the sea, and the constant flicker before their eyes of idle, well-dressed, well-fed people seemed to renew him; he told Anna Sergeyevna how beautiful she was, how seductive, was impatiently passionate, never left her side, and she often pondered and repeatedly asked him to confess that he didn’t respect her, didn’t love her at all, and only saw her as a vulgar woman. Almost every evening later they went somewhere outside the city, to Oreanda or to the waterfall; and the outings were a success, the impressions were always wonderful, majestic.

They were waiting for her husband to arrive. But a letter came from him informing that his eyes had started to hurt, and he begged his wife to return home quickly. Anna Sergeyevna hurried.

"This is good that I am leaving," she told Gurov. "This is fate itself."

She rode by horse, and he saw her off. They traveled all day. When she sat down in the courier train carriage and the second bell rang, she said:

"Let me look at you one more time… Just one more time. Like this."

She didn’t cry but was sad, as if ill, and her face trembled.

"I will think of you… remember you," she said. "God be with you, stay well. Don’t speak ill of me. We say goodbye forever, it must be so because we shouldn’t have met at all. Well, God be with you."

The train left quickly, its lights soon disappeared, and after a minute there was no sound, as if everything had conspired on purpose to end this sweet oblivion as soon as possible, this madness. And left alone on the platform, looking into the dark distance, Gurov listened to the cry of crickets and the hum of telegraph wires with a feeling as if he had just woken up. And he thought that here in his life there had been another escapade or adventure, and it too was over, and only a memory remained… He was moved, sad, and felt a slight remorse; after all, this young woman, whom he would never see again, had not been happy with him; he was kind and warm toward her, but still in his manner, in his tone and caresses, there was a faint shadow of mockery, a rough arrogance of a happy man, who was almost twice her age. She always called him kind, extraordinary, exalted; obviously, he seemed to her not what he really was, so he had unwittingly deceived her…

It already smelled like autumn at the station, the evening was cool.

“It’s time for me to go north too,” Gurov thought as he left the platform. "Time!"

III

At home in Moscow everything was already winter-like, they were heating the stoves, and in the mornings, when the children gathered for gymnasium and drank tea, it was dark, and the nanny briefly lit a fire. Frosts had already begun. When the first snow falls, on the first day of sleigh riding, it’s pleasant to see white ground, white rooftops, the air breathes softly, nicely, and at that time one remembers their youth. Near the old lindens and birches, white with frost, there is a kind expression; they are closer to the heart than cypresses and palms.and near them he no longer wanted to think about mountains and the sea.

Gurov was a Muscovite, he returned to Moscow on a fine, frosty day, and when he put on his fur coat and warm gloves and walked along Petrovka, and when on Saturday evening he heard the ringing of bells, his recent trip and the places he had been lost all their charm for him. Little by little he immersed himself in Moscow life, greedily reading three newspapers a day and saying that he did not read Moscow newspapers on principle. He was already drawn to restaurants, clubs, to invited dinners, anniversaries, and he was flattered that he sometimes had famous lawyers and actors and that at the doctors’ club he played cards with a professor. He could already eat a whole portion of peasant stew on a skillet…

A month would pass, and Anna Sergeyevna, it seemed to him, would become shrouded in memory by a mist and only occasionally appear in dreams with a touching smile, as others did. But more than a month passed, deep winter came, and the memory was still clear, just as if he had parted with Anna Sergeyevna only yesterday. And the memories grew stronger and stronger. Whether in the evening silence voices of children preparing their lessons reached his office, or he heard a romance or an organ in a restaurant, or the storm howled in the fireplace, suddenly everything came back to memory: what happened on the pier, the early morning fog on the mountains, the steamboat from Feodosia, and kisses. He paced the room for a long time, remembered, smiled, and then the memories turned into dreams, and the past in imagination mixed with what was to come. Anna Sergeyevna did not appear in his dreams, but followed him everywhere like a shadow and watched over him. Closing his eyes, he saw her as if alive, and she seemed more beautiful, younger, more tender than she had been; and he himself seemed better than he was then, in Yalta. In the evenings she looked at him from the bookcase, from the fireplace, from the corner, he heard her breathing, the gentle rustle of her clothes. Outside on the street he watched women, looking to see if any resembled her…

And already a strong desire tormented him to share his memories with someone. But at home he could not speak of his love, and outside home—there was no one. Not the tenants and not at the bank. And what to talk about? Did he love then? Was there anything beautiful, poetic, or instructive, or simply interesting in his relationship with Anna Sergeyevna? And he had to speak vaguely about love, about women, and no one suspected what it was about, only his wife moved her dark eyebrows and said:

— You, Dmitry, the role of the fool does not suit you at all.

One night, leaving the doctors’ club with his partner, an official, he could not resist and said:

— If only you knew what a charming woman I met in Yalta!

The official got into the sled and drove off, but suddenly turned and called out:

— Dmitry Dmitrich!

— What?

— And recently you were right: the sturgeon does have an odor!

These words, so ordinary, suddenly outraged Gurov, seemed humiliating, impure. What wild morals, what faces! What senseless nights, what uninteresting, unnoticed days! The frantic card games, overeating, drunkenness, constant talks all about the same thing. Useless affairs and talks all about the same thing take the best part of time, the best energy, and in the end, what remains is some scanty, wingless life, some nonsense, and to leave and run away is impossible, as if you are sitting in a madhouse or prison companies!

Gurov did not sleep all night and was outraged and then spent the whole day with a headache. And in the following nights he slept poorly, kept sitting in bed and thinking or walking from corner to corner. Children bored him, the bank bored him, he did not want to go anywhere or talk about anything.

In December during the holidays, he prepared for a journey and told his wife he was leaving for Petersburg to take care of a young man—and left for S. Why? He himself did not know well. He wanted to see Anna Sergeyevna and talk, arrange a meeting, if possible.

He arrived in S. in the morning and took the best room in the hotel, where the entire floor was covered with gray soldier’s cloth and there was an inkstand on the table, gray with dust, with a rider on a horse,who had a raised hand with a hat, and a bruised head.      And Anna Sergeyevna began coming to him in Moscow. Once every two or three months, she would leave S. and tell her husband that she was going to consult a professor about her woman’s illness—and the husband both believed and didn’t believe it. Arriving in Moscow, she stayed at the "Slavyansky Bazaar" and immediately sent a man in a red hat to Gurov. Gurov visited her, and no one in Moscow knew about it.

     One day he was going to her this way on a winter morning (the messenger had been with him the previous evening and had missed it). His daughter was with him, whom he wanted to see off to the gymnasium, as it was on the way. Large wet snow was falling.

     “It’s three degrees above zero now, and yet it’s snowing,” Gurov said to his daughter. “But that warmth is only at the surface of the earth; in the upper layers of the atmosphere, the temperature is completely different.”

     “Papa, why is there no thunder in winter?”

     He explained that too. He spoke and thought about how he was going to a meeting and not a single living soul knew about it and probably never would. He had two lives: one obvious, seen and known by everyone who needed to know, full of conditional truth and conditional deceit, completely resembling the life of his acquaintances and friends, and another—that flowed secretly. And by some strange coincidence, perhaps accidental, everything that was important, interesting, necessary to him, in which he was sincere and did not deceive himself, which made up the kernel of his life, happened secretly from others; everything that was his lie, his shell, in which he hid to conceal the truth, such as his job at the bank, debates at the club, his “lower race,” attending anniversaries with his wife—all this was obvious. And he judged others by himself, not believing what he saw, always assuming that everyone’s true, most interesting life passed under the cover of secrecy, like under the cover of night. Every personal existence rests on secrecy, and perhaps for that reason a cultured person so nervously cares about respecting personal secrets.

     Having seen his daughter off to the gymnasium, Gurov went to the "Slavyansky Bazaar." He took off his coat downstairs, went upstairs, and quietly knocked on the door. Anna Sergeyevna, dressed in his favorite gray dress, tired from the trip and the wait, had been waiting for him since the previous evening; she was pale, looked at him without smiling, and as soon as he entered, she clung to his chest. It seemed they hadn’t seen each other for two years; their kiss was long, lingering.

     “So, how do you live there?” he asked. “What’s new?”

     “Wait, I’ll tell you now… I can’t.”

     She couldn’t speak because she was crying. She turned away from him and pressed a handkerchief to her eyes.

     “Well, let her cry, and I’ll sit for a while,” he thought and sat down in a chair.

     Then he called and told them to bring him some tea; and later, while drinking tea, she kept standing, turned to the window… She was crying from excitement, from the sorrowful realization that their life had turned out so sadly; they met only secretly, hiding from people like thieves! Wasn’t their life shattered?

     “Well, stop it!” he said.

     It was obvious to him that their love would not end anytime soon, unknown when exactly. Anna Sergeyevna grew more attached to him, adored him, and it would have been unthinkable to tell her that all this must have an end someday; indeed, she wouldn’t believe it.

     He approached her and took her by the shoulders to caress her, to joke, and at that moment saw himself in the mirror.

     His hair was already starting to gray. And it seemed strange to him how much he had aged in recent years, how much uglier he had become. The shoulders on which his hands rested were warm and trembling. He felt compassion for this life, still so warm and beautiful, but probably already close to beginning to fade and wither, like his life. Why did she love him so? He always seemed to women to be different from what he really was, and they loved not him, but the person created by their imagination, whom they eagerly sought in their lives; and then, when they noticed their mistake, nevertheless loved him. And none of them had been happy with him. Time passed, he met, connected,he broke up, but never loved even once; there was everything but love.

And only now, when his hair had turned gray, did he love properly, truly — for the first time in his life.

Anna Sergeyevna and he loved each other like very close, dear people, like husband and wife, like tender friends; it seemed to them that fate itself had destined them for each other, and it was unclear why he was married and she was married; and they were exactly like two migratory birds, male and female, caught and forced to live in separate cages. They forgave each other for what they were ashamed of in their past, forgave everything in the present, and felt that this love had changed both of them.

Before, in sad moments, he calmed himself with all sorts of reasoning that came to mind, but now he was not up for reasoning, he felt deep compassion, wanted to be sincere, tender...

— Stop, my dear, — he said. — You cried — and it will be... Now let's talk, let's figure something out.

Then they consulted for a long time, talking about how to free themselves from the need to hide, deceive, live in different cities, and not see each other for a long time. How to break free from these unbearable chains?

— How? How? — he asked, grabbing his head. — How?

And it seemed that a little more — and the solution would be found, and then a new, wonderful life would begin; and both clearly understood that the end was still very far away and that the hardest and most difficult part was only just beginning.



Black Monk



I



Andrey Vasilyich Kovrin, a master’s degree holder, was tired and had disturbed his nerves. He did not seek treatment but somehow casually, over a bottle of wine, talked with a doctor friend, who advised him to spend spring and summer in the countryside. Along with that, a long letter arrived from Tanya Pesotskaya, who asked him to come to Borisovka and visit. And he decided that he really needed to make the trip.

At first — it was in April — he went to his ancestral Kovrinka, and lived there in seclusion for three weeks; then, after waiting for good roads, he set off on horseback to his former guardian and tutor Pesotsky, a well-known gardener in Russia. From Kovrinka to Borisovka, where the Pesotskys lived, it was considered no more than seventy versts, and traveling along soft spring roads in a quiet spring-suspension carriage was a true pleasure.

Pesotsky’s house was huge, with columns, with lions whose plaster was peeling off, and a footman in a tailcoat at the entrance. The old park, gloomy and strict, laid out in the English manner, stretched almost a full verst from the house to the river and ended there at a steep, clay bank where pines grew with exposed roots that looked like shaggy paws; below, water glistened desertedly, sandpipers scurried with plaintive cries, and there was always such a mood there that you could sit and write a ballad. But near the house, in the yard and the orchard, which together with the nurseries occupied about thirty desyatins, it was cheerful and lively even in bad weather. Such amazing roses, lilies, camellias, such tulips of all colors, from bright white to black as soot, in general such a richness of flowers as at Pesotsky’s, Kovrin had never seen anywhere else. Spring had only just begun, and the true luxury of flowerbeds was still hidden in the greenhouses, but even what was blooming along the alleys and here and there on the flowerbeds was enough for one to feel like being in the realm of delicate colors while walking in the garden, especially in the early hours when dew sparkled on every petal.

What was the decorative part of the garden and what Pesotsky himself disdainfully called trifles once produced a fairy-tale impression on Kovrin in his childhood. There were all sorts of whims, exquisite deformities, and mockeries of nature! There were trellises made from fruit trees, a pear tree shaped like a pyramidal poplar, spherical oaks and lindens, an umbrella of apple trees, arches, monograms, candelabra, and even 1862 made from plums — the number indicating the year Pesotsky first took up gardening.Here and there were beautiful slender trees with straight and sturdy trunks like palms, and only on close inspection could you recognize these trees as gooseberries or currants. But what amused the most in the garden and gave it a lively appearance was the constant movement. From early morning until evening, around the trees, bushes, on the alleys and flowerbeds, people with wheelbarrows, hoes, and watering cans were bustling like ants…

Kovrin arrived at the Pesotskys’ in the evening, around ten o’clock. He found Tanya and her father, Yegor Semenych, deeply worried. The clear, starry sky and the thermometer foretold frost by morning, yet the gardener Ivan Karlych had gone to the city, and there was no one to rely on. During dinner, they spoke only about the morning event and decided that Tanya would not go to bed but would walk through the garden at one o’clock and check if everything was alright, and Yegor Semenych would get up at three or even earlier.

Kovrin spent the entire evening with Tanya and after midnight went with her into the garden. It was cold. The yard already strongly smelled of burning. In the large fruit garden, known as the commercial one and bringing Yegor Semenych several thousand clean income annually, black, thick, pungent smoke spread over the ground and, enveloping the trees, protected these thousands from frost. The trees stood in a checkerboard pattern, their rows straight and orderly, like lines of soldiers, and this strict pedantic precision and the fact that all the trees were of the same height and had completely identical crowns and trunks made the scene monotonous and even boring. Kovrin and Tanya walked along rows where fires of manure, straw, and various scraps were smoldering, and occasionally met workers wandering in the smoke like shadows. Only cherries, plums, and some varieties of apples were blooming, but the entire garden was immersed in smoke, and only near the nurseries did Kovrin take a deep breath.

“I used to sneeze here from the smoke as a child,” he said, shrugging, “but I still don’t understand how this smoke can protect against frost.”

“Smoke replaces clouds when there are none…” Tanya replied.

“And what are clouds for?”

“There are no morning events on overcast and cloudy days.”

“Is that so!”

He laughed and took her hand. Her broad, very serious, chilled face with thin black eyebrows, the raised collar of her coat that hindered her from moving her head freely, and her whole slender, thin figure, dressed in a dress gathered from the dew, charmed him.

“My God, she’s already an adult!” he said. “When I left here last time, five years ago, you were still a child. You were so skinny, long-legged, with simple hair, wore a short dress, and I teased you as a heron… What time does!”

“Yes, five years!” Tanya sighed. “A lot has happened since then. Tell me, Andryusha, honestly,” she began lively, looking him in the face, “have you gotten out of the habit of us? But why do I ask? You are a man, already living your own, interesting life, you are a big deal… Alienation is so natural! But no matter what, Andryusha, I want you to consider us yours. We have the right.”

“I do consider you, Tanya.”

“Really?”

“Yes, really.”

“You were surprised today that we have so many of your photographs. You know, my father adores you. Sometimes I think he loves you more than me. He is proud of you. You are a scientist, an extraordinary person, you’ve made a brilliant career for yourself, and he is sure that you turned out this way because he raised you. I don’t interfere with his thinking so. Let him.”

Dawn was already beginning, and this was especially noticeable by the distinctness with which smoke plumes and tree crowns stood out in the air. Nightingales were singing, and from the fields came the call of quails.

“Still, it’s time to sleep,” Tanya said. “And it’s cold.” She took his arm. “Thank you, Andryusha, for coming. We have no interesting acquaintances, and those few very little. We only have garden, garden, garden—and nothing else. Trunk, half-trunk,” she laughed, “apport, ranet, borovinka, budding, copulation… Our whole life has gone into the garden, I can’t even dream of anything but apple and pear trees. Of course, it’s good, useful, but sometimes you want something else for variety.”I remember when you used to come to us for the holidays or just like that, the house somehow became fresher and brighter, as if the covers were removed from the chandelier and the furniture. I was a girl then and still understood.

She spoke for a long time and with great feeling. For some reason, it suddenly occurred to him that during the summer he might get attached to this small, weak, talkative creature, become fascinated and fall in love – in their situation, this was so possible and natural! This thought amused and amused him; he bent over the sweet, worried face and sang softly:

Onegin, I won’t hide,

I madly love Tatyana…

When they came home, Yegor Semenych was already up. Kovrin didn't feel like sleeping, he started talking with the old man and went back with him to the garden. Yegor Semenych was tall, broad-shouldered, with a big belly and suffered from shortness of breath, but always walked so fast that it was hard to keep up with him. He looked extremely worried, always in a hurry somewhere and with such an expression as if missing even one minute meant everything was lost!

– Here, brother, is the story… – he began, stopping to catch his breath. – On the surface of the earth, as you see, there is frost, but if you raise a thermometer on a stick about two fathoms above the ground, it’s warm there… Why is that?

– Honestly, I don’t know, – said Kovrin and laughed.

– Hmm… You can’t know everything, of course… No matter how vast the mind is, it can’t hold everything. You are more into philosophy, right?

– Yes. I read psychology, but I’m generally into philosophy.

– And it doesn’t bore you?

– On the contrary, it’s the only thing I live by.

– Well, God bless… – said Yegor Semenych, thoughtfully stroking his gray sideburns. – God bless… I’m very glad for you… glad, brother…

But suddenly he listened carefully, made a terrible face, ran off in a direction, and soon disappeared behind the trees, in a cloud of smoke.

– Who tied the horse to the apple tree? – came his desperate, soul-rending cry. – What kind of scoundrel and rascal dared to tie a horse to the apple tree? My God, my God! Spoiled, froze, defiled, messed up! The garden is ruined! The garden is lost! My God!

When he returned to Kovrin, his face was exhausted, offended.

– What can you do with this anathema people? – he said in a crying voice, spreading his hands. – Stepka carried manure at night and tied the horse to the apple tree! The scoundrel wound the reins so tightly that the bark was rubbed off in three places. Imagine that! I told him, but he just shrugged and only blinked! Hanging is too little!

Having calmed down, he hugged Kovrin and kissed him on the cheek.

– Well, God bless… God bless… – he mumbled. – I’m very glad you came. Inexpressibly glad… Thank you.

Then he walked quickly all around the garden again with that worried look, showing his former pupil all the greenhouses, hothouses, ground sheds, and his two apiaries, which he called the wonder of our century.

While they were walking, the sun rose and brightly illuminated the garden. It became warm. Sensing a clear, cheerful, long day ahead, Kovrin remembered that it was still only early May and that the whole summer, just as clear, cheerful, and long, was still ahead, and suddenly a joyful young feeling stirred in his chest, the kind he felt as a child running around this garden. And he himself hugged the old man and gently kissed him. Both, moved, went into the house and began drinking tea from antique porcelain cups, with cream, with filling, sweet pretzels – and these little things again reminded Kovrin of his childhood and youth. The beautiful present and the awakening impressions of the past merged together; his soul felt crowded but good.

He waited until Tanya woke up and drank coffee with her, went for a walk, then went to his room and sat down to work. He read attentively, took notes, and occasionally raised his eyes to look at the open windows or at the fresh, still dew-wet flowers standing in vases on the table, then lowered his eyes back to the book, and it seemed to him that every vein in him trembled and played with pleasure.

II

In the village, he continued to lead the same nervous and restless life as in the city.He read and wrote a lot, studied Italian, and when he walked, he gladly thought about the fact that he would soon sit down to work again. He slept so little that everyone was amazed; if he accidentally fell asleep for half an hour during the day, he would not sleep at all that night and, after a sleepless night, as if nothing had happened, he felt alert and cheerful.

He talked a lot, drank wine, and smoked expensive cigars. The Pesotskys often had young ladies from neighboring apartments visit, almost every day, who played piano and sang with Tanya; sometimes a young man, a neighbor, who played the violin well, would come. Kovrin listened to the music and singing eagerly and was exhausted by them, which physically manifested in his eyes closing and his head tilting to one side.

One evening after tea, he was sitting on the balcony reading. At that time, Tanya—the soprano—one of the young ladies—the contralto—and the young man with the violin were rehearsing Braga’s famous serenade in the living room. Kovrin listened to the words—they were Russian—and could not understand their meaning at all. Finally, putting down the book and listening attentively, he understood: the girl, sick with imagination, had heard some mysterious sounds in the garden at night, so beautiful and strange that she had to recognize them as sacred harmony, which to us mortals is incomprehensible and therefore returns back to the heavens. Kovrin’s eyes began to close. He stood up and, exhausted, walked around the living room, then the hall. When the singing stopped, he took Tanya by the arm and went out to the balcony with her.

“I’ve been occupied with one legend since this morning,” he said. “I don’t remember if I read it somewhere or heard it, but it’s a strange, incongruous legend. To start with, it’s not clear. A thousand years ago, some monk dressed in black was walking through the desert somewhere in Syria or Arabia… A few miles from where he was walking, fishermen saw another black monk slowly moving across the surface of a lake. This second monk was a mirage. Now forget all the laws of optics, which the legend, it seems, does not acknowledge, and listen further. From the mirage came another mirage, then from the second a third, so the image of the black monk endlessly transferred from one atmospheric layer to another. He was seen in Africa, Spain, India, the Far North… Finally, he left the bounds of the earth’s atmosphere and now wanders through the entire universe, never finding conditions under which he could fade. Maybe he is now seen somewhere on Mars or on some star of the Southern Cross. But, my dear, the very essence, the main point of the legend is that exactly a thousand years after the monk walked through the desert, the mirage will again enter the earth’s atmosphere and appear to people. And supposedly this thousand years is coming to an end… According to the legend’s meaning, we should expect the black monk not today—but tomorrow.”

“A strange mirage,” said Tanya, who did not like the legend.

“But the most amazing thing,” Kovrin laughed, “is that I can’t remember where this legend came into my head. Did I read it somewhere? Hear it? Or maybe the black monk dreamed of me? I swear to God, I don’t remember. But the legend preoccupies me. I’ve been thinking about it all day.”

Having sent Tanya back to the guests, he left the house and walked thoughtfully near the flower beds. The sun was already setting. The flowers, freshly watered, gave off a damp, irritating smell. The singing resumed inside, and from afar the violin resembled a human voice. Kovrin, straining his mind to recall where he had heard or read the legend, headed slowly to the park and quietly reached the river.

Along the path running along the steep bank past exposed roots, he descended down to the water, disturbing sandpipers and scaring two ducks away. On the gloomy pines, here and there the last rays of the setting sun still glimmered, but on the river’s surface evening had already fully settled. Kovrin crossed over the logs to the other side. Before him now lay a wide field covered with young, still not blooming rye. No human dwelling, no living soul in the distance, and it seemed that if he followed the path,will lead to that very unknown mysterious place,
– Well, you see… – Kovrin muttered. – So, there’s some truth to the legend.

Not trying to explain to himself the strange phenomenon, content just to have been able to see not only the black clothing but even the face and eyes of the monk so clearly and up close, pleasantly excited, he returned home.

People were walking calmly in the park and garden, and there was music in the house – which meant he was the only one who saw the monk. He really wanted to tell everything to Tanya and Yegor Semenych, but he realized they would probably think he was crazy, and that would scare them; it was better to stay silent. He laughed loudly, sang, danced the mazurka, he was happy, and everyone, guests and Tanya included, thought that today his face was somehow special, radiant, inspired, and that he was very interesting.

III

After dinner, when the guests left, he went to his room and lay down on the couch: he wanted to think about the monk. But after a minute, Tanya came in.

– Here, Andryusha, read these articles by Father, – she said, handing him a stack of brochures and prints. – Wonderful articles. He writes excellently.

– Well, excellently indeed! – said Yegor Semenych, following her in and forcing a laugh; he felt ashamed. – Please don’t listen, don’t read! However, if you want to fall asleep, then maybe read them: a perfect sleeping aid.

– In my opinion, they’re magnificent articles, – Tanya said with deep conviction. – You should read them, Andryusha, and convince Dad to write more often. He could write a complete course on gardening.

Yegor Semenych laughed tensely, blushed, and started saying phrases that embarrassed authors usually say. Finally, he began to give in.

– In that case, read Gosha’s article first and these Russian pieces, – he mumbled, sorting through the brochures with trembling hands, – or else you won’t understand. Before reading my objections, you need to know what I’m objecting to. Though, it’s nonsense… boring stuff. And it’s time to sleep, it seems.

Tanya left. Yegor Semenych sat down next to Kovrin on the couch and sighed deeply.

– Yes, my brother… – he began after a moment of silence. – Well, my dear magister. I write articles, participate in exhibitions, and get medals… They say Pesotsky’s apples are huge, and Pesotsky, they say, made a fortune with his garden. In short, Kochubey is rich and famous. But the question is: what’s the point of it all? The garden is indeed beautiful, exemplary… It’s not just a garden, it’s a whole institution of great state importance because it’s, so to speak, a step into a new era of Russian agriculture and Russian industry. But to what end? What’s the goal?

– The matter speaks for itself.

– I don’t mean that. I want to ask: what will happen to the garden when I die? In the condition you see it now, it won’t last a month without me. The whole secret of success isn’t that the garden is large and has many workers, but that I love the work – you understand? – love it perhaps more than myself. Look at me: I do everything myself. I work from morning till night. All the grafting I do myself, the pruning – myself, the planting – myself, everything – myself. When someone helps me, I get jealous and irritated to the point of rudeness. The whole secret is love, meaning an attentive owner’s eye, and the owner’s hands, and that feeling when you go somewhere for a visit for an hour, sit down, but your heart isn’t at ease, you’re not yourself: you worry that something might happen in the garden. And when I die, who will watch over it? Who will work? The gardener? The workers? Yes? So here’s what I’ll tell you, my dear friend: the greatest enemy in our work isn’t the hare, not the chafer beetle, nor frost, but a stranger.

– And Tanya? – Kovrin asked, laughing. – It can’t be that she’s more harmful than a hare. She loves and understands the work.

– Yes, she loves and understands. If after my death she inherits the garden and becomes the owner, of course, you couldn’t wish for better. But if, God forbid, she gets married? – Yegor Semenych whispered and looked at Kovrin fearfully. – That’s the thing! She’ll get married, there will be children, and then there’s no time to think about the garden.

– What I fear most is that she’ll marry some young man, and he will be stingy and rent the garden out to merchants,and everything will go to hell in the very first year! In our business, women are a scourge of God!

Yegor Semenych sighed and was silent for a moment.

– Maybe it's selfishness, but frankly, I don't want Tanya to get married. I'm afraid! There's a certain firt who comes to us with a violin and fiddles; I know Tanya won't go for him, I know well, but I can't stand seeing him! Honestly, brother, I'm still a bit of an oddball. I confess.

Yegor Semenych stood up and walked nervously around the room, and it was clear he wanted to say something very important but didn’t dare.

– I love you warmly and will speak openly with you, – he finally decided, putting his hands in his pockets. – I approach some delicate issues simply and say straight what I think, and I can’t stand so-called secret thoughts. To put it bluntly: you’re the only person I wouldn’t be afraid to give my daughter to. You’re a smart person, with a heart, and you wouldn’t let my beloved business perish. And the main reason – I love you like a son…and I’m proud of you. If some kind of romance were to develop between you and Tanya, well? I’d be very glad and even happy. I say this plainly, without affectation, as an honest person.

Kovrin laughed. Yegor Semenovich opened the door to leave and stopped at the threshold.

– If you and Tanya had a son, I’d make him a gardener, – he said thoughtfully. – Although, that’s just an empty dream… Good night.

Left alone, Kovrin lay down comfortably and began reading the articles. One had the title: “On Intermediate Culture,” another: “A Few Words Regarding Mr. Z’s Note on Replanting Soil for a New Orchard,” a third: “More on Budding with a Dormant Eye” – all the same kind. But what a restless, uneven tone, what a nervous, almost sickly enthusiasm! Here’s an article, it seems, with the calmest title and indifferent content: it talks about the Russian Antonovka apple tree. But Yegor Semenych begins it with “audiatur altera pars”1 and ends with “sapienti sat”2, and between these sayings there’s a whole fountain of different poisonous words against “the scholarly ignorance of our certified gardeners who observe nature from the heights of their chairs,” or Mr. Goshe, “whose success was made by amateurs and dilettantes,” and then, oddly, there is a forced and insincere regret that the peasant thieves who steal fruit and break trees can no longer be whipped with rods.

“A beautiful, lovely, healthy business, but even here there is passion and war,” thought Kovrin. “It must be that everywhere and in all fields ideological people are nervous and have heightened sensitivity. Probably, that’s how it needs to be.”

He remembered Tanya, who liked Yegor Semenych’s articles so much. Short in stature, pale, thin, so that the collarbones are visible; eyes wide open, dark, smart, always gazing somewhere and searching for something; walk like her father’s, small and hurried. She talks a lot, likes to argue, and even the slightest phrase is accompanied by expressive facial expressions and gestures. She must be extremely nervous.

Kovrin kept reading but understood nothing and gave up. A pleasant excitement, the very one with which he had recently danced the mazurka and listened to music, now tormented him and stirred many thoughts inside him. He got up and paced the room, thinking about the black monk. It occurred to him that if only he had seen this strange, supernatural monk, then he must be ill and have reached hallucinations. This thought frightened him, but only briefly.

“But I feel good, and I don’t harm anyone; so there is nothing bad in my hallucinations,” he thought, and he felt good again.

He sat on the sofa and hugged his head with his hands, restraining the incomprehensible joy filling his entire being, then paced again and sat down to work. But the thoughts he read from the book did not satisfy him. He wanted something gigantic, boundless, striking. Toward morning he undressed and reluctantly went to bed: after all, he had to sleep!

When he heard Yegor Semenych’s footsteps leaving for the garden, Kovrin rang and ordered the lackey to bring some wine. He drank several glasses of Lafite with pleasure,then buried his head; his consciousness grew hazy, and he fell asleep.



IV



     Yegor Semenych and Tanya often argued and said unpleasant things to each other.

     One morning they quarreled about something. Tanya cried and went to her room. She didn’t come out for lunch or tea. At first, Yegor Semenych walked around proudly, sulking, as if wanting to show that for him the interests of justice and order are above all else, but soon he couldn’t handle his temper and grew despondent. He wandered sadly through the park, sighing, “oh my God, my God!” and didn’t eat a single crumb at dinner. Finally, feeling guilty and tormented by conscience, he knocked on the locked door and timidly called:

     – Tanya! Tanya?

     And in response, from behind the door, came a weak voice, exhausted from tears but at the same time resolute:

     – Leave me alone, I beg you.

     The hosts’ distress affected the whole house, even the people working in the garden. Kovrin was immersed in his interesting work, but by the end, even he got bored and uneasy. To somehow dispel the general bad mood, he decided to intervene and before evening, knocked on Tanya’s door. She let him in.

     – Oh dear, how shameful! – he began jokingly, surprised as he looked at Tanya’s tear-stained, blotchy, sorrowful face. – Is it really that serious? Oh dear!

     – But if you only knew how he torments me! – she said, and tears, bitter, abundant tears burst from her big eyes. – He’s worn me out! – she continued, wringing her hands. – I didn’t say anything to him… nothing… I only said that there’s no need to keep… extra workers, if… if you can have day laborers whenever you want. After all… the workers have done nothing for a whole week… I… I just said that, and he started shouting and said many hurtful, deeply insulting things to me. Why?

     – Enough, enough, – said Kovrin, fixing her hair. – You argued, cried, and that’s that. You can’t stay angry for long, it’s not good… especially since he loves you endlessly.

     – He… he ruined my whole life, – Tanya continued, sobbing. – All I hear are insults and… and offenses. He thinks I’m unnecessary in his house. Well? He’s right. I’ll leave here tomorrow, enroll as a telegraph operator… Let it be…

     – Well, well, well… Don’t cry, Tanya. Don’t, dear… You’re both hot-tempered, irritable, and both to blame. Come on, I’ll make peace between you.

     Kovrin spoke gently and convincingly, but she kept crying, trembling in her shoulders and clenching her hands as if a terrible misfortune had truly befallen her. He felt sorry for her all the more because her grief was not serious, yet she suffered deeply. Such trifles were enough to make this creature unhappy for a whole day, and maybe for life! While comforting Tanya, Kovrin thought that besides this girl and her father, there were hardly any people in the whole world, day or night, who loved him as their own, as kin; if it weren’t for these two, he, having lost his father and mother in early childhood, would probably never have known what sincere affection and that naïve, unreasoning love are, the kind that only very close, blood relatives can feel. And he felt that his half-sick, frayed nerves responded like iron to a magnet to the nerves of this crying, trembling girl. He could never have loved a healthy, strong, rosy-cheeked woman, but pale, weak, miserable Tanya pleased him.

     And he gladly stroked her hair and shoulders, shook her hands, and wiped her tears… Finally, she stopped crying. She long complained about her father and her heavy, unbearable life in this house, begging Kovrin to understand her situation; then she gradually began to smile and sigh that God had sent her such a bad temper, and finally, laughing loudly, called herself a fool and ran out of the room.

     When a little while later Kovrin went out into the garden, Yegor Semenych and Tanya were already walking side by side along the alley as if nothing had happened, both eating rye bread with salt, as they were both hungry.



V



     Pleased that his role as peacemaker had gone so well, Kovrin went to the park. Sitting on a bench, reflecting,He heard the knocking of carriages and women’s laughter – the guests had arrived. When the evening shadows began to fall in the garden, faint sounds of a violin and singing voices could be heard, reminding him of the black monk. Somewhere, in some country or on some planet, what is this optical absurdity that now roams?

As soon as he recalled the legend and pictured in his mind the dark ghost he had seen in the rye field, a man of average height, with an uncovered gray head, dressed all in dark and barefoot, resembling a beggar, came out silently, without the slightest rustle, from behind the pine, directly opposite. On his pale, almost dead face, sharp black eyebrows stood out. Nodding his head kindly, this beggar or stranger silently approached the bench and sat down, and Kovrin recognized the black monk in him. For a moment, both looked at each other – Kovrin with amazement, and the monk gently and, as before, a little slyly, with a knowing expression.

“But you’re a mirage,” Kovrin said. “Why are you here and sitting in one place? That doesn’t match the legend.”

“That doesn’t matter,” the monk answered quietly after a moment, facing him. “Legend, mirage, and I – all are products of your excited imagination. I am a ghost.”

“So you don’t exist?” Kovrin asked.

“Think as you wish,” said the monk, smiling faintly. “I exist in your imagination, and your imagination is part of nature, so I exist in nature as well.”

“You have a very old, wise, and highly expressive face, as if you really have lived more than a thousand years,” Kovrin said. “I didn’t know my imagination could create such phenomena. But why do you look at me with such admiration? Do you like me?”

“Yes. You are one of the few rightly called God’s chosen. You serve eternal truth. Your thoughts, intentions, your amazing science, and your whole life bear the divine, heavenly seal because they are devoted to the rational and beautiful, that is, to what is eternal.”

“You said: eternal truth… But is eternal truth accessible and necessary to people if there is no eternal life?”

“There is eternal life,” said the monk.

“Do you believe in human immortality?”

“Yes, of course. A great, brilliant future awaits you humans. And the more people like you there are on earth, the sooner this future will come true. Without you, servants of the highest principle, living consciously and freely, humanity would be nothing; developing by natural order, it would still wait a long time for the end of its earthly history. But you will bring it into the kingdom of eternal truth thousands of years earlier – and that is your great merit. You embody the blessing of God that rested on humans.”

“And what is the purpose of eternal life?” Kovrin asked.

“As with all life – enjoyment. True enjoyment lies in knowledge, and eternal life will provide countless and inexhaustible sources for knowledge, and in this sense it is said: in my Father’s house are many mansions.”

“If only you knew how pleasant it is to listen to you!” Kovrin said, rubbing his hands with pleasure.

“I’m very glad.”

“But I know: when you leave, I will be troubled by the question of your essence. You are a ghost, a hallucination. So, am I mentally ill, abnormal?”

“Even if so. Why be embarrassed? You are ill because you have worked beyond your strength and are tired, which means you have sacrificed your health to an idea, and the time is near when you will sacrifice your very life to it. What could be better? This is what all gifted noble natures strive for.”

“If I know I am mentally ill, can I trust myself?”

“And why do you know that the genius people, whom the whole world trusts, never saw ghosts? Scientists now say that genius is akin to madness. My friend, only ordinary, herd people are healthy and normal. Concerns about the nervous age, overwork, degeneration, etc., can seriously trouble only those who see the purpose of life in the present, that is, herd people.”

“The Romans said: mens sana in corpore sano.”

“Not everything is true,”What the Romans or Greeks said. Elevated mood, excitement, ecstasy – all that distinguishes prophets, poets, martyrs for an idea from ordinary people is repugnant to the animal side of a person, that is, to their physical health. I repeat: if you want to be healthy and normal, go to the herd.

– Strange, you keep repeating what often comes to my own mind, – Kovrin said. – It’s as if you’ve peeked and overheard my innermost thoughts. But let’s not talk about me. What do you mean by eternal truth?

The monk did not answer. Kovrin looked at him but could not make out his face: his features blurred and melted away. Then the monk’s head and hands began to disappear; his torso merged with the bench and the evening twilight, and he vanished completely.

– The hallucination is over! – Kovrin said and laughed. – What a pity.

He walked back to the house cheerful and happy. The little that the black monk told him flattered not his pride but his whole soul, his entire being. To be chosen, to serve the eternal truth, to stand among those who would make humanity worthy of the kingdom of God several thousand years earlier – that is, save people from several unnecessary thousand years of struggle, sin, and suffering; to give everything to the idea – youth, strength, health, to be ready to die for the common good – what a lofty, what a happy fate! His past flashed through his memory, pure, chaste, full of labor, he recalled what he had learned and what he himself had taught others, and decided that the monk’s words were not an exaggeration.

Tanya was walking toward him through the park. She was already wearing a different dress.

– You’re here? – she said. – We’ve been looking for you, looking… But what’s wrong with you? – she was surprised, looking at his enthusiastic, radiant face and eyes full of tears. – How strange you are, Andryusha.

– I’m content, Tanya, – Kovrin said, placing his hands on her shoulders. – I’m more than content, I’m happy! Tanya, dear Tanya, you are an extremely charming being. Dear Tanya, I’m so glad, so glad!

He kissed both her hands warmly and continued:

– I just experienced bright, wonderful, unearthly moments. But I can’t tell you everything because you’ll call me crazy or won’t believe me. Let’s talk about you. Dear, lovely Tanya! I love you and have already gotten used to loving. Your closeness, our meetings ten times a day have become a need of my soul. I don’t know how I’ll manage without you when I go back home.

– Well! – Tanya laughed. – You’ll forget about us in two days. We are small people, and you are a great man.

– No, let’s be serious! – he said. – I’ll take you with me, Tanya. Yes? Will you go with me? Do you want to be mine?

– Well! – Tanya said and wanted to laugh again, but no laughter came, and red spots appeared on her face.

She began breathing heavily and walked quickly quickly, but not home, further into the park.

– I haven’t thought about this… I haven’t! – she said, clutching her hands as if in despair.

And Kovrin followed her, still with the same radiant, enthusiastic face:

– I want a love that would capture me completely, and only you, Tanya, can give me this love. I’m happy! Happy!

She was stunned, bent over, shrank, and seemed to age ten years all at once, while he found her beautiful and loudly expressed his admiration:

– How beautiful she is!
