Dead Souls

Dead Souls

A Poem

VOLUME ONE

CHAPTER ONE

Into the gates of the inn of the provincial town of NN drove a rather handsome, spring-mounted, small britzka, of the kind bachelors ride in: retired lieutenant colonels, staff captains, landowners with about a hundred serfs—in short, all those whom they call gentlemen of the middle rank. In the britzka sat a gentleman, not handsome, but not ill-favored either, neither too stout nor too thin; one could not say he was old, yet neither was he too young. His arrival produced absolutely no stir in the town and was accompanied by nothing special; only two Russian peasants, standing at the doors of a tavern opposite the inn, made some remarks, which, however, related more to the carriage than to the person sitting in it. "Look," said one to the other, "what a wheel! What do you think, will that wheel make it to Moscow, if it happened, or not?" "It will," answered the other. "But to Kazan, I think, it won't make it?" "To Kazan it won't make it," answered the other. With this, the conversation ended. And also, when the britzka drove up to the inn, a young man in white canvas trousers, very narrow and short, in a frock coat with pretensions to fashion, from under which a shirtfront was visible, fastened with a Tula pin with a bronze pistol, met it. The young man turned back, looked at the carriage, held his cap, which almost flew off in the wind, and went on his way.

When the carriage entered the yard, the gentleman was met by the inn servant, or "polovoy," as they are called in Russian taverns, so lively and fidgety that it was impossible even to make out what his face looked like. He ran out briskly, with a napkin in his hand—all long and in a long dimity frock coat with the back almost at the very nape of his neck, shook his hair, and briskly led the gentleman up the entire wooden gallery to show him the room sent to him by God. The room was of a known kind, for the inn was also of a known kind, that is, exactly like inns in provincial towns, where for two rubles a day travelers receive a comfortable room with cockroaches peeking out, like prunes, from all corners, and a door to an adjoining room, always blocked by a chest of drawers, where a neighbor settles in, a silent and calm person, but extremely curious, interested in knowing all the details of the traveler. The exterior facade of the inn matched its interior: it was very long, two stories; the lower one was not plastered and remained in dark red bricks, further darkened by harsh weather changes and already somewhat dirty in themselves; the upper one was painted with the eternal yellow paint; downstairs were shops with harnesses, ropes, and bagels. In the corner of one of these shops, or rather, in the window, sat a sbiten vendor with a samovar of red copper and a face as red as the samovar, so that from a distance one might think that there were two samovars in the window, if one samovar did not have a beard as black as pitch.

While the arriving gentleman was inspecting his room, his belongings were brought in: first of all, a suitcase of white leather, somewhat worn, showing that it was not its first time on the road. The suitcase was carried in by the coachman Selifan, a short man in a sheepskin coat, and the valet Petrushka, a fellow of about thirty, in a spacious, secondhand frock coat, evidently from his master's shoulder, a fellow somewhat stern in appearance, with very large lips and nose. Following the suitcase, a small mahogany casket with inlaid work of Karelian birch, shoe lasts, and a roasted chicken wrapped in blue paper were brought in. When all this had been brought in, the coachman Selifan went to the stable to attend to the horses, and the valet Petrushka began to settle into a small, very dark cubbyhole of an anteroom, where he had already managed to drag his overcoat and, along with it, some peculiar smell of his own, which was also imparted to the bag of various valet's toiletries brought in afterward. In this cubbyhole, he fitted a narrow, three-legged bed against the wall, covering it with a small semblance of a mattress, beaten and flat as a pancake,and perhaps as greasy as the pancake he managed to coax from the innkeeper.

While the servants were busy, the gentleman went to the common room. Every traveler knows very well what these common rooms are like: the same walls, painted with oil paint, darkened at the top from pipe smoke and polished at the bottom by the backs of various travelers, and even more so by local merchants, for on market days merchants would come here in groups of six or seven to drink their customary two cups of tea; the same smoke-blackened ceiling; the same smoky chandelier with many hanging glass pieces that jumped and jingled every time the waiter ran across the worn oilcloths, briskly waving a tray on which sat as many teacups as birds on a seashore; the same full-wall paintings, done in oil paints – in short, everything was the same as everywhere else; the only difference was that one painting depicted a nymph with such enormous breasts as the reader has surely never seen. Such a quirk of nature, however, occurs in various historical paintings, brought to us in Russia at an unknown time, from an unknown place, and by unknown artists, sometimes even by our noblemen, art lovers who bought them in Italy on the advice of the couriers who brought them. The gentleman took off his cap and unwound a woolen, rainbow-colored scarf from his neck, the kind a wife prepares with her own hands for her married husband, providing suitable instructions on how to wrap it, and for bachelors – I certainly cannot say who makes them, God knows, I have never worn such scarves. Having unwound the scarf, the gentleman ordered dinner. While he was served various usual tavern dishes, such as: shchi with a puff pastry pie, specially saved for travelers for several weeks, brains with peas, sausages with cabbage, roasted pullet, pickled cucumber, and the eternal sweet puff pastry pie, always ready to serve; while all this was being served to him, both warmed up and simply cold, he made the servant, or waiter, tell all sorts of nonsense – about who used to run the tavern and who runs it now, and how much income it brings, and what a big scoundrel their landlord is; to which the waiter, as usual, replied: "Oh, a big scoundrel, sir." Both in enlightened Europe and in enlightened Russia, there are now many respectable people who cannot eat in a tavern without talking to the servant, and sometimes even making amusing jokes at their expense. However, the newcomer did not ask all empty questions; he inquired with extreme precision about who was the governor in the city, who was the chairman of the chamber, who was the prosecutor – in short, he did not miss a single significant official; but with even greater precision, if not even with interest, he inquired about all the significant landowners: how many serfs each had, how far they lived from the city, even their character and how often they came to the city; he carefully inquired about the state of the region: whether there had been any diseases in their province – widespread fevers, deadly fevers of any kind, smallpox, and the like, and all so thoroughly and with such precision that it showed more than mere simple curiosity. In his manners, the gentleman had something solid and blew his nose extremely loudly. It is unknown how he did it, but his nose sounded like a trumpet. This, in my opinion, completely innocent quality, however, earned him much respect from the tavern servant, so much so that every time he heard this sound, he shook his hair, straightened up more respectfully, and, bending his head from above, asked: "Do you need anything?" After dinner, the gentleman drank a cup of coffee and sat on the sofa, placing a pillow behind his back, which in Russian taverns, instead of elastic wool, is stuffed with something extremely similar to brick and cobblestone. Here he began to yawn and ordered to be taken to his room, where, lying down, he slept for two hours. After resting, at the request of the tavern servant, he wrote his rank, name, and surname on a scrap of paper to be reported to the police, where it was due. On the paper, the waiter, descending the stairs, read syllable by syllable the following:"Collegiate Councillor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, landowner, for his own needs." While the waiter was still deciphering the note syllable by syllable, Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov himself went to see the town, with which he seemed satisfied, for he found that the town was in no way inferior to other provincial towns: the yellow paint on the stone houses was striking, and the grey on the wooden ones modestly darkened. The houses were one, two, and one and a half stories high, with an eternal mezzanine, very beautiful, in the opinion of the provincial architects. In places, these houses seemed lost amidst streets as wide as a field and endless wooden fences; in other places, they clustered together, and here there was noticeably more movement of people and liveliness. There were signs almost washed away by rain, with pretzels and boots, here and there with painted blue trousers and the signature of some tailor Arshavsky; a shop with caps, peaked caps, and the inscription: "Foreigner Vasily Fyodorov"; a billiard table was painted with two players in tailcoats, such as guests wear in our theaters when entering the stage in the last act. The players were depicted with cues aimed, hands somewhat turned back, and crooked legs, having just performed an entrechat in the air. Below all this was written: "And here is the establishment." Here and there, simply on the street, stood tables with nuts, soap, and gingerbread resembling soap; a tavern with a painted fat fish and a fork stuck into it. Most often, however, darkened double-headed state eagles were visible, which are now replaced by the laconic inscription: "Drinking House." The pavement was poor everywhere. He also looked into the city garden, which consisted of thin, poorly rooted trees, with supports below, in the form of triangles, very beautifully painted with green oil paint. However, although these saplings were no taller than reeds, it was stated in the newspapers, when describing the illumination, that "our city has been adorned, thanks to the care of the civil governor, with a garden consisting of shady, wide-branched trees, providing coolness on a hot day," and that "it was very touching to see how the hearts of the citizens trembled in an excess of gratitude and shed streams of tears as a sign of appreciation for the mayor." After asking the watchman in detail where one could go closer, if necessary, to the cathedral, to the government offices, to the governor, he went to look at the river flowing through the middle of the city, on the way tearing off a poster nailed to a post, so that, upon returning home, he could read it properly, stared intently at a lady of not unpleasant appearance walking on the wooden sidewalk, followed by a boy in military livery, with a small bundle in his hand, and, once again surveying everything with his eyes, as if to remember the location well, he went straight home to his room, slightly supported on the stairs by the inn servant. After drinking tea, he sat down at the table, ordered a candle, took the poster from his pocket, held it to the candle, and began to read, squinting his right eye slightly. However, there was little remarkable in the poster: a drama by Mr. Kotzebue was being performed, in which Rolla was played by Mr. Poplvin, Cora by Miss Zyablova, and the other characters were even less remarkable; nevertheless, he read them all, even got to the price of the parterre and learned that the poster was printed in the printing house of the provincial government, then turned it over to the other side: to see if there was anything there, but, finding nothing, he rubbed his eyes, folded it neatly, and put it in his casket, where he was accustomed to putting everything he came across. The day, it seems, concluded with a portion of cold veal, a bottle of sour shchi, and a sound sleep "to the full pump-bolt," as they say in some parts of the vast Russian state.

The entire next day was devoted to visits; the newcomer went to pay visits to all the city dignitaries. He respectfully called on the governor, who, it turned out, like Chichikov, was neither fat nor thin, wore an Anna around his neck, and it was even rumored that he had been presented with a star; however, he was a very kind man and even sometimes embroidered on tulle himself. Then he went to the vice-governor,then he visited the prosecutor, the chamber president, the police chief, the tax farmer, the head of the state factories… it's a pity it's somewhat difficult to remember all these powerful people; but suffice it to say, the visitor showed extraordinary activity in making visits: he even appeared to pay his respects to the inspector of the medical board and the city architect. And then he sat in his carriage for a long time, thinking of whom else to visit, but there were no more officials left in the city. In conversations with these authorities, he very skillfully managed to flatter each one. He casually hinted to the governor that entering his province was like entering paradise, the roads everywhere were like velvet, and that governments appointing wise dignitaries deserved great praise. He said something very flattering to the police chief about the city watchmen; and in conversations with the vice-governor and the chamber president, who were still only collegiate assessors, he even mistakenly said "Your Excellency" twice, which pleased them greatly. The result of this was that the governor invited him to his home party that same day, and the other officials, for their part, also invited him, some for dinner, some for a game of boston, some for a cup of tea.

The visitor, it seemed, avoided talking much about himself; if he did speak, it was in general terms, with noticeable modesty, and his conversation in such cases took on a somewhat bookish turn: that he was an insignificant worm of this world and not worthy of much concern, that he had experienced much in his life, suffered for truth in service, had many enemies who even attempted his life, and that now, wishing to settle down, he sought to finally choose a place of residence, and that, upon arriving in this city, he considered it his indispensable duty to pay his respects to its foremost dignitaries. This was all that was learned in the city about this new person, who very soon did not fail to show himself at the governor's party. Preparation for this party took more than two hours, and here the visitor showed such attention to his toilet as was not even seen everywhere. After a short afternoon nap, he ordered water to wash and rubbed both cheeks with soap for an extraordinarily long time, propping them from within with his tongue; then, taking a towel from the inn servant's shoulder, he wiped his full face with it from all sides, starting from behind his ears and snorting twice directly into the inn servant's face. Then he put on a dickey in front of the mirror, plucked two hairs that had grown out of his nose, and immediately thereafter appeared in a cranberry-colored tailcoat with a sparkle. Dressed in this manner, he rolled in his own carriage along the endlessly wide streets, illuminated by the meager light from the occasional flickering oceans. However, the governor's house was so brightly lit, as if for a ball; a carriage with lanterns, two gendarmes at the entrance, postilion shouts in the distance – in short, everything as it should be. Upon entering the hall, Chichikov had to close his eyes for a moment, because the brilliance from the candles, lamps, and ladies' dresses was tremendous. Everything was flooded with light. Black tailcoats flickered and darted about, singly and in clusters here and there, like flies buzzing on white, shining refined sugar during the hot July summer, when the old housekeeper chops and divides it into sparkling fragments before an open window; children all watch, gathered around, curiously following the movements of her strong hands raising the hammer, and airy squadrons of flies, lifted by the light air, fly in boldly, like full masters, and, taking advantage of the old woman's poor eyesight and the sun bothering her eyes, scatter over the tempting pieces, some haphazardly, some in thick clusters. Satiated by the rich summer, which already offered tempting dishes at every turn, they flew in not at all to eat, but only to show themselves, to walk back and forth over the sugar pile, to rub their hind or front legs against each other, or to scratch themselves under their wings with them, or, extending both front paws, to rub them over their heads, turn around and fly away again, and fly back again with new annoying squadrons. Chichikov had not yet had time to look around,as he was already taken by the arm by the governor, who immediately introduced him to the governor's wife. The visiting guest did not lose his composure here either: he made some compliment, very appropriate for a middle-aged man of not too high and not too low a rank. When the established dancing couples pressed everyone against the wall, he, with his hands behind his back, watched them very attentively for about two minutes. Many ladies were well-dressed and fashionable, others wore whatever God sent to the provincial town. Men here, as everywhere, were of two kinds: some thin ones, who constantly hovered around the ladies; some of them were such that it was difficult to distinguish them from Petersburgers, they also had very thoughtfully and tastefully combed sideburns or simply presentable, very smoothly shaven oval faces, they also casually sat down next to ladies, also spoke French and amused ladies just like in Petersburg. The other kind of men were stout or like Chichikov, that is, not too stout, but not thin either. These, on the contrary, squinted and backed away from the ladies and only looked around to see if the governor's servant was setting up a green table for whist anywhere. Their faces were full and round, some even had warts, some were pockmarked, they did not wear their hair in tufts, or curls, or in the "devil take me" style, as the French say – their hair was either closely cropped or slicked back, and their facial features were mostly rounded and strong. These were the respected officials in the town. Alas! the stout ones know better how to manage their affairs in this world than the thin ones. The thin ones mostly serve on special assignments or are merely listed and waver here and there; their existence is somehow too light, airy, and completely unreliable. The stout ones, on the other hand, never occupy indirect positions, but always direct ones, and if they sit somewhere, they sit reliably and firmly, so much so that the place will sooner crack and bend under them, but they will not fall off. They do not like outward splendor; their tailcoat is not as skillfully cut as that of the thin ones, but in their coffers there is God's grace. A thin man in three years will not have a single soul left that is not pawned; a stout man calmly, look – and a house appears somewhere at the end of the town, bought in his wife's name, then another house at the other end, then a small village near the town, then a large village with all its amenities. Finally, the stout man, having served God and the sovereign, having earned universal respect, leaves service, moves and becomes a landowner, a glorious Russian gentleman, a hospitable host, and lives, and lives well. And after him, again, thin heirs, according to Russian custom, squander all their father's property on express couriers. It cannot be concealed that almost such reflections occupied Chichikov at that time, when he was observing society, and the consequence of this was that he finally joined the stout ones, where he met almost all familiar faces: the prosecutor with very black thick eyebrows and a somewhat winking left eye as if saying: "Let's go, brother, to another room, I'll tell you something there," – a man, however, serious and silent; the postmaster, a short man, but a wit and a philosopher; the president of the chamber, a very reasonable and amiable man – all of whom greeted him as an old acquaintance, to which Chichikov bowed somewhat sideways, not without pleasure, however. Here he also became acquainted with the very courteous and polite landowner Manilov and the somewhat clumsy-looking Sobakevich, who stepped on his foot at first, saying: "Excuse me." Here they also handed him a card for whist, which he accepted with the same polite bow. They sat down at the green table and did not get up until supper. All conversations completely ceased, as always happens when one finally devotes oneself to a serious occupation. Although the postmaster was very talkative, even he, taking the cards in his hands, immediately expressed a thoughtful expression on his face, covered his upper lip with his lower lip, and maintained this position throughout the game. Coming out of a figure, he struck the table firmly with his hand, saying, if it was a queen: "Go,"old priest's wife!", and if it's the king: "Go, Tambov peasant!"

The next day, Chichikov went to dinner and an evening party at the house of the police chief, where from three o'clock in the afternoon they sat down to whist and played until two in the morning. There, among others, he met the landowner Nozdryov, a man of about thirty, a dashing fellow, who after a mere three or four words began addressing him with the familiar "thou." Nozdryov was also on familiar terms with the police chief and the prosecutor, treating them in a friendly manner; but when they sat down to play a high-stakes game, the police chief and the prosecutor examined his tricks with extreme attention and watched almost every card he played. The next day Chichikov spent the evening at the home of the president of the chamber, who received his guests in a somewhat greasy dressing gown, and whose company included two ladies of some sort. After that, he was at an evening party at the vice-governor's, at a great dinner given by the tax-farmer, at a small dinner at the prosecutor's, which, however, was well worth a great one, and at a reception after mass given by the town mayor, which was also well worth a dinner. In a word, he did not have a single hour to remain at home, and he returned to the inn solely to sleep.

The newcomer somehow knew how to find his bearings in everything and showed himself to be an experienced man of the world. No matter what the conversation was about, he always knew how to sustain it: if the talk was about a stud-farm, he spoke about stud-farms; if they talked of good dogs, here too he offered very sensible remarks; if they discoursed regarding an investigation conducted by the crown treasury, he showed that he was no stranger to judicial tricks; if there was a discussion about the game of billiards, he did not miss his mark in billiards either; if they spoke of virtue, he discoursed on virtue very well, even with tears in his eyes; about the distillation of spirits, he knew the value of spirits too; about customs inspectors and officials, he judged them as though he were himself both an official and an inspector. But it was remarkable that he knew how to cloak all this with a certain gravity, and knew how to conduct himself well. He spoke neither loudly nor softly, but exactly as one ought. In a word, whichever way you turned him, he was a very gentlemanly man. All the officials were pleased with the arrival of the new personage. The governor declared of him that he was a well-intentioned man; the prosecutor, that he was a practical man; the colonel of the gendarmes said that he was a learned man; the president of the chamber, that he was a knowledgeable and venerable man; the police chief, that he was a venerable and amiable man; the police chief's wife, that he was the most amiable and agreeable man. Even Sobakevich himself, who rarely spoke well of anyone, having returned quite late from town and having completely undressed and gone to bed beside his skinny wife, said to her: "My dear, I was at the governor’s evening party, and dined at the police chief’s, and I made the acquaintance of Collegiate Councillor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov: a most pleasant man!" To which his spouse replied, "Hmm!" and nudged him with her foot.

Such was the opinion, highly flattering to the guest, that formed about him in the town, and it lasted until a certain strange trait of the guest and an enterprise of his—or, as they say in the provinces, a *passage*—of which the reader will soon learn, threw almost the entire town into utter bewilderment.

### CHAPTER TWO

The traveling gentleman had already lived in the town for more than a week, driving about to evening parties and dinners, and thus spending his time, as the saying goes, very pleasantly. At last, he resolved to extend his visits outside the town and call upon the landowners Manilov and Sobakevich, to whom he had given his word. Perhaps he was prompted to this by another, more substantial reason, a matter more serious and closer to his heart… But of all this the reader will learn gradually and in due time, if only he has the patience to read the story before him, which is very long and destined to expand wider and more spaciously as it approaches the end that crowns the work. Orders were given to the coachman Selifan to harness the horses to the familiar chaise early in the morning; Petrushka was ordered to stay at home, to look after the room and the portmanteau.

It will not be superfluous for the reader to become acquainted with these two serfs of our hero. Although, of course, they are not such prominent figures, being what one calls secondary or even tertiary characters—and although the main movements and springs of the poem are not fixed upon them and only here and there touch and lightly catch them—the author is extraordinarily fond of being circumstantial in everything and, in this respect, despite being a Russian himself, wishes to be as precise as a German. This will not, however, take up much time or space, because not much needs to be added to what the reader already knows: that is, that Petrushka went about in a somewhat oversized brown frock coat from his master's shoulder and had, according to the custom of people of his station, a large nose and thick lips. By character, he was more silent than talkative; he even possessed a noble impulse toward enlightenment, that is, the reading of books, with the contents of which he did not trouble himself: it was absolutely all the same to him whether it was the adventures of a lover-hero, a simple primer, or a prayer book—he read everything with equal attention; if chemistry had been put in his way, he would not have rejected even that. He liked not what he read about, but rather the reading itself, or, better to say, the process of the reading itself: the fact that out of letters there always emerged some word or other, which sometimes the devil only knew what it meant. This reading was performed for the most part in a reclining position in the anteroom, on a bed and on a mattress that had become, by virtue of this circumstance, flattened and thin as a pancake.

In addition to his passion for reading, he had two other habits that formed his other two characteristic features: sleeping without undressing, just as he was, in that same frock coat, and always carrying about with him a certain peculiar atmosphere of his own, a scent of his own making, which smacked somewhat of a lived-in room, so that it was enough for him merely to set up his bed somewhere, even in a room hitherto uninhabited, and to drag his overcoat and belongings there, and it already seemed that people had lived in that room for ten years. Chichikov, being a very fastidious and in some cases even squeamish man, inhaling the air with a fresh nose in the morning, would only wince and shake his head, saying: "You, brother, the devil knows what is wrong with you—are you sweating or what? You might at least go to the bathhouse." To which Petrushka made no reply and tried to occupy himself with some business on the spot; he would either approach his master’s hanging dress coat with a fly-whisk, or simply tidy something up. What he thought at the time while he remained silent—perhaps he said to himself: "And you, however, are a fine one yourself; aren't you tired of repeating the same thing forty times?"—God only knows; it is difficult to know what a domestic serf thinks at the time his master is giving him an admonition. So, this is what can be said about Petrushka for a start.

The coachman Selifan was a completely different man… But the author is very conscientious about occupying his readers for so long with people of the lower class, knowing by experience how reluctantly they make the acquaintance of the lower estates. Such is the Russian: a powerful passion to become acquainted with anyone who is even a single rank above him, and a passing acquaintance with a count or a prince is better to him than any close, friendly relations. The author even fears for his hero, who is only a Collegiate Councillor. Court Councillors will perhaps make his acquaintance, but those who have already scrambled up to general's ranks—they, God knows, may even cast upon him one of those contemptuous glances that are proudly cast by a man upon everything that crawls at his feet, or, what is even worse, may pass him by with an inattention fatal to the author.

But however distressing both may be, it is nevertheless necessary to return to the hero. And so, having given the necessary orders the evening before, waking up very early in the morning, washing and rubbing himself from head to foot with a wet sponge—which was done only on Sundays, and that day happened to be a Sunday—shaving in such a manner that his cheeks became real satin in point of smoothness and luster, putting on a dress coat of a lingonberry color with a sparkle, and then an overcoat lined with large bear-skins, he descended the stairs, supported under the arm first on one side and then on the other by the inn servant, and got into the chaise.

With a rumble, the chaise drove out from under the gates of the inn into the street. A passing priest took off his hat; a few boys in soiled shirts held out their hands, saying: "Master, give to a poor orphan!" The coachman, noticing that one of them was very fond of hanging onto the back of the carriage, flicked him with his whip, and the chaise began to bounce over the cobblestones. It was not without joy that the striped toll-gate was sighted in the distance, giving notice that the cobblestones, like every other torment, would soon come to an end; and after hitting his head quite hard against the carriage body a few more times, Chichikov flew along at last over the soft earth.

No sooner had the town vanished behind them than there began to appear, according to our custom, nonsense and wildness on both sides of the road: hummocks, fir woods, low, thin bushes of young pines, charred trunks of old ones, wild heather, and similar rubbish. Villages stretched out in a straight line came into view, built like old stacked logs, covered with gray roofs with carved wooden decorations under them in the shape of hanging embroidered hand-towels. A few peasants, as usual, were yawning, sitting on benches before the gates in their sheepskin coats. Women with fat faces and bound breasts looked out of the upper windows; from the lower ones a calf gazed out or a pig thrust forth its blind snout. In short, familiar views.

Having passed the fifteenth verst, he remembered that here, according to Manilov, his village ought to be, but the sixteenth verst flew past too, and still no village was to be seen; and had they not chanced upon two peasants coming toward them, they would hardly have managed to hit upon the right way. To the question of how far it was to the village of Zamanilovka, the peasants took off their hats, and one of them, who was the cleverer and wore a wedge-shaped beard, replied:
"Manilovka, perhaps, and not Zamanilovka?"
"Well, yes, Manilovka."
"Manilovka! Why, when you drive another verst, there it is for you—that is, straight to the right."
"To the right?" echoed the coachman.
"To the right," said the peasant. "That will be the road to Manilovka for you; but there is no Zamanilovka at all. That's what it's called—that is, its name is Manilovka, but there is no Zamanilovka here at all. Straight up on the hill there you'll see a house, a stone house, two stories high, the master's house, in which, that is, the master himself lives. That is Manilovka for you, but there is no Zamanilovka here at all and never has been."

They set off to look for Manilovka. After driving two versts, they came upon a turn into a country road, but they had already done two, three, and four versts, it seemed, and still the two-story stone house was nowhere to be seen. Here Chichikov remembered that if a friend invites you to his village fifteen versts away, it means it is a good thirty versts off.

The village of Manilovka could allure few people by its situation. The master's house stood isolated upon a bluff, that is, on an elevation exposed to all the winds that might choose to blow; the slope of the hill on which it stood was covered with clipped turf. Scattered upon it in the English fashion were two or three flowerbeds with bushes of lilacs and yellow acacias; five or six birches in small clusters here and there raised their small-leaved, sparse tops. Under two of them was visible an arbor with a flat green dome, wooden blue columns, and the inscription: "The Temple of Solitary Reflection"; lower down was a pond covered with green weed, which, however, is nothing unusual in the English gardens of Russian landowners. At the foot of this elevation, and partly along the slope itself, stretched lengthwise and crosswise gray log huts, which our hero, for reasons unknown, immediately set about counting and counted more than two hundred; nowhere among them was there a growing tree or any greenery; everywhere nothing but bare logs met the eye. The view was enlivened by two women who, picturesquely holding up their dresses and tucked in from all sides, were wading knee-deep in the pond, dragging by two wooden handles a torn drag-net, wherein two entangled crayfish were visible and a caught roach glistened; the women seemed to be at variance and were scolding each other for something. Further off, a pine forest loomed with a dull, bluish-gray color.

Even the weather itself was very obligingly accommodating: the day was neither bright nor dark, but of a certain light-gray color, such as is found only on the old uniforms of garrison soldiers—a peaceful troop, indeed, but partially intoxicated on Sundays. To complete the picture, there was no lack of a rooster, the herald of changeable weather, who, despite the fact that his head had been pecked to the very brain by the beaks of other roosters over certain affairs of gallantry, crowed very loudly and even flapped his wings, which were as tattered as old mats.

As he drove up to the courtyard, Chichikov noticed on the porch the master himself, who stood in a green coat of shalloon cloth, holding his hand to his forehead in the form of an umbrella over his eyes to get a better view of the approaching carriage. As the chaise drew nearer to the porch, his eyes grew more cheerful and his smile widened more and more.
"Pavel Ivanovich!" he cried out at last, as Chichikov was getting out of the chaise. "At last you have remembered us!"

The two friends kissed each other very warmly, and Manilov led his guest into the room. Although the time during which they will pass through the vestibule, the anteroom, and the dining room is rather short, let us try if we cannot somehow turn it to account and say something about the master of the house. But here the author must confess that such an enterprise is very difficult. It is much easier to depict characters of a large scale: there you simply fling colors upon the canvas with a full hand—black flashing eyes, overhanging eyebrows, a forehead furrowed by a wrinkle, a black or fire-red cloak thrown over the shoulder—and the portrait is ready; but all these gentlemen, of whom there are so many in the world, who look very much alike, and yet when you look closer you see many of the most elusive peculiarities—these gentlemen are terribly difficult for portraits. Here one will have to strain one's attention severely before making all the subtle, almost invisible features stand out, and in general one will have to direct a gaze already sharpened in the science of investigation deep down.

God alone, perhaps, could say what Manilov’s character was like. There is a class of people known by the name of: people so-so, neither one thing nor the other, neither Bogdan in town nor Selifan in the village, according to the proverb. Perhaps Manilov should be joined to them. In appearance, he was a fine-looking man; his facial features were not lacking in pleasantness, but into this pleasantness, it seemed, too much sugar had been passed; in his manners and turns of speech there was something that truckled for favor and acquaintance. He smiled alluringly, was fair-haired, with blue eyes. In the first minute of conversation with him, you cannot help saying: "What a pleasant and good man!" In the next minute you will say nothing, and in the third you will say: "The devil knows what this is!"—and you will walk further away; and if you do not walk away, you will feel a deadly boredom. From him you will never catch a single lively or even opinionated word, such as you may hear from almost anyone else if you touch upon a subject that provokes him. Everyone has his own hobby: one man's hobby has turned toward greyhounds; another fancies that he is a mighty lover of music and wonderfully feels all its deep passages; a third is a master at dining dashingly; a fourth at playing a role even an inch higher than the one assigned to him; a fifth, with a more limited desire, sleeps and dreams of how he might go for a stroll with an aide-de-camp, to show off before his friends, acquaintances, and even strangers; a sixth is already gifted with such a hand that it feels a supernatural desire to turn down the corner of a knave of diamonds or a deuce, while the hand of a seventh just itches to bring order somewhere, to get closer to the person of the stationmaster or the coachmen—in a word, everyone has his own, but Manilov had nothing.

At home he spoke very little and for the most part pondered and thought, but what he thought about, God alone likewise knew. It could not be said that he occupied himself with the estate; he never even went out into the fields, the estate ran somehow by itself. When the bailiff said: "It would be well, sir, to do this and that,"—"Yes, not bad," he would usually reply, puffing his pipe, which he had made a habit of smoking when he still served in the army, where he was considered the most modest, most delicate, and most educated officer. "Yes, precisely, not bad," he would repeat. When a peasant came to him and, scratching the back of his neck with his hand, said: "Master, allow me to absent myself for work, to earn money for the tax,"—"Go," he would say, smoking his pipe, and it never even entered his head that the peasant was going off to get drunk. Sometimes, looking from the porch into the courtyard and at the pond, he would speak of how fine it would be if suddenly an underground passage were dug from the house, or a stone bridge built across the pond, with shops on both sides, and shopkeepers sitting in them selling various small wares needed by the peasants. At this his eyes would become extraordinarily sweet and his face would assume the most contented expression; however, all these projects ended only in words.

In his study there always lay some book or other, marked with a bookmark at the fourteenth page, which he had been constantly reading for two years already. In his house something was eternally lacking: in the drawing room stood beautiful furniture covered in elegant silk material, which had certainly cost a pretty penny; but there was not enough of it for two armchairs, and the armchairs stood covered simply in coarse matting; however, for several years the master, every time, warned his guest with the words: "Do not sit in those armchairs, they are not ready yet." In another room there was no furniture at all, although it had been said in the first days after their marriage: "My dear, tomorrow we must trouble ourselves to put some furniture in this room, if only for the time being." In the evening, a very elegant candlestick of dark bronze with three antique graces and a mother-of-pearl shield was served at table, and beside it was placed some simple brass invalid, lame, twisted to one side and all covered in tallow, though neither the master, nor the mistress, nor the servants noticed this.

His wife… however, they were completely satisfied with each other. Despite the fact that more than eight years had passed since their marriage, each of them still brought the other either a piece of apple, a bonbon, or a nut, and said in a touchingly tender voice, expressive of perfect love: "Open your little mouth, my dear, I will put this piece in for you." It goes without saying that the little mouth opened on such occasions very gracefully. For birthdays, surprises were prepared: some beaded case for a toothpick. And very often, sitting on the sofa, suddenly, for reasons entirely unknown, one leaving his pipe and the other her work, if it happened to be in her hands at the time, they would imprint upon each other such a languid and long kiss that during its course one might easily have smoked a small straw cigar. In a word, they were what is called happy.

Of course, it might be remarked that there are many other occupations in a house besides prolonged kisses and surprises, and many various questions might be asked. Why, for instance, is the cooking done so foolishly and to no purpose in the kitchen? Why is the storeroom rather empty? Why is the housekeeper a thief? Why are the servants untidy and drunkards? Why does all the house-serf crew sleep in a merciless manner and play the truant all the rest of the time? But all these are low subjects, and Madame Manilova was well brought up. And a good education, as is well known, is obtained in boarding schools. And in boarding schools, as is well known, three main subjects constitute the foundation of human virtues: the French language, necessary for the happiness of family life; the pianoforte, for creating pleasant moments for a spouse; and, finally, the domestic part itself: the knitting of purses and other surprises. However, there are various improvements and changes in methods, especially in the present time; all this depends more upon the good sense and capabilities of the schoolmistresses themselves. In other boarding schools it happens in such a way that first comes the pianoforte, then the French language, and only then the domestic part. And sometimes it happens that first comes the domestic part, that is, the knitting of surprises, then the French language, and only then the pianoforte. Various are the methods.

It does no harm to make one more remark, that Madame Manilova… but, I confess, I am very much afraid to speak about ladies, and besides, it is time for me to return to our heroes, who had already been standing for several minutes before the drawing-room door, mutually entreating each other to pass forward.
"Do me the favor, do not distress yourself so for me, I will pass after," said Chichikov.
"No, Pavel Ivanovich, no, you are the guest," said Manilov, pointing him to the door with his hand.
"Do not trouble yourself, please, do not trouble yourself. Please, pass on," said Chichikov.
"No, you must excuse me, I will not allow such a pleasant, educated guest to pass behind."
"Why educated?.. Please, pass on."
"Well, be so good as to pass you."
"But why?"
"Well, just because!" Manilov said with a pleasant smile.
At last both friends entered the door sideways and squeezed each other somewhat.
"Allow me to present my wife to you," said Manilov. "My dear! Pavel Ivanovich!"

Chichikov did indeed see a lady whom he had completely failed to notice while bowing with Manilov in the doorway. She was not bad-looking, and dressed to suit her. A silk gown of a pale color sat well upon her; her small, slender hand hastily threw something or other on the table and squeezed a cambric handkerchief with embroidered corners. She rose from the sofa on which she was sitting; Chichikov, not without pleasure, approached to kiss her hand. Madame Manilova said, even lispering a little, that he had made them very happy by his arrival, and that not a day passed without her husband remembering him.
"Yes," added Manilov, "she used to keep asking me: 'Why doesn't your friend come?'—'Wait, my dear, he will come.' And now at last you have honored us with your visit. You have indeed afforded such a delight… a day in May… the nameday of the heart…"

Chichikov, hearing that matters had already reached the nameday of the heart, was even somewhat embarrassed and replied modestly that he had neither a great name nor even a prominent rank.
"You have everything," Manilov interrupted with the same pleasant smile, "you have everything, and even more."
"How did you like our town?" added Madame Manilova. "Have you spent your time pleasantly there?"
"A very good town, a beautiful town," replied Chichikov, "and I spent my time very pleasantly: the society is most agreeable."
"And how did you find our governor?" said Madame Manilova.
"Is it not true that he is a most venerable and most amiable man?" added Manilov.
"Perfectly true," said Chichikov, "a most venerable man. And how he has entered into his duties, how he understands them! One must wish for more such men."
"How he can, you know, receive everyone, maintain delicacy in his actions," added Manilov with a smile, and from pleasure almost completely shut his eyes, like a cat whose ears have been lightly tickled with a finger.
"A very agreeable and pleasant man," continued Chichikov, "and what a craftsman! I could not even have suspected it. How well he embroiders various domestic patterns! He showed me a purse of his own workmanship: rare is the lady who can embroider so skillfully."
"And the vice-governor, is he not a charming man?" said Manilov, again narrowing his eyes slightly.
"A very, very worthy man," replied Chichikov.
"Well, allow me—and how did the police chief appear to you? Is it not true that he is a very pleasant man?"
"Extremely pleasant, and what a clever, what a well-read man! We played whist at his house together with the prosecutor and the president of the chamber until the very latest cocks; a very, very worthy man."
"Well, and what is your opinion of the police chief's wife?" added Madame Manilova. "Is she not a most amiable woman?"
"Oh, she is one of the most worthy women I have ever known," replied Chichikov.

After this they did not pass over the president of the chamber, the postmaster, and in this manner went through almost all the officials of the town, all of whom turned out to be the most worthy people.
"Do you always spend your time in the country?" Chichikov asked at last, in his turn.
"Mostly in the country," replied Manilov. "However, we come to town solely to see educated people. One grows wild, you know, living locked up all the time."
"True, true," said Chichikov.
"Of course," continued Manilov, "it would be a different matter if the neighborhood were good, if, for instance, there were such a person with whom one could, in a way, speak of amiability, of good manners, follow some such science as would stir the soul, give it, so to say, a kind of soaring…" Here he wished to express something more, but noticing that he had gotten somewhat muddled, he only waved his hand in the air and continued: "Then, of course, the country and solitude would have very many pleasures. "But you know," added Manilov, "it is all nothing if there is no friend with whom one can share…"
"Oh, that is true, that is absolutely true!" Chichikov interrupted. "What are all the treasures in the world then! 'Have no money, but have good people for company,' said one sage."
"And you know, Pavel Ivanovich!" said Manilov, showing in his face an expression not merely sweet, but even cloying, like that mixture which a clever worldly doctor has sweetened unmercifully, imagining he is delighting the patient with it. "Mercy, what sort of pleasant conversation?.. An insignificant man, and nothing more," replied Chichikov.
"Oh! Pavel Ivanovich, allow me to be frank: I would gladly give half of my entire fortune to have a part of those qualities which you possess!.."
"On the contrary, I for my part would consider it the greatest..."

It is unknown to what lengths the mutual outpouring of feelings of both friends would have gone, if a servant entering had not announced that the meal was ready.
"I humbly beg you," said Manilov. "You will excuse us if we do not have such a dinner as is found on parquet floors and in capitals; with us, according to the Russian custom, it is simply cabbage soup (*shchi*), but from a pure heart. I humbly beg you."

Here they disputed for some time more as to who should enter first, and at last Chichikov entered the dining room sideways.

In the dining room two boys were already standing, Manilov’s sons, who were of an age when children are already seated at table, but still on high chairs. Beside them stood the tutor, who bowed politely and with a smile. The mistress sat down before her soup tureen; the guest was seated between the master and the mistress, and the servant tied napkins around the children’s necks.
"What charming children," said Chichikov, looking at them, "and what is their age?"
"The eldest is in his eighth year, and the younger turned six only yesterday," said Madame Manilova.
"Themistoclus!" said Manilov, turning to the eldest, who was trying to free his chin, which the footman had tied up in the napkin.
Chichikov raised his eyebrow slightly upon hearing such a somewhat Greek name, to which, for some unknown reason, Manilov had given the ending "us," but he tried immediately to bring his face back to its ordinary composure.
"Themistoclus, tell me, what is the best city in France?"
Here the tutor directed all his attention to Themistoclus and, it seemed, wanted to jump into his eyes, but at last he completely calmed down and nodded his head when Themistoclus said: "Paris."
"And what is our best city?" Manilov asked again.
The tutor again tuned his attention.
"Petersburg," replied Themistoclus.
"And what other?"
"Moscow," replied Themistoclus.
"Clever boy, darling!" Chichikov said to this. "Tell me, however…" he continued, turning immediately with a certain look of amazement to the Manilovs, "at such an age and already such knowledge! I must tell you that this child will have great abilities."
"Oh, you don't know him yet," replied Manilov, "he has an extraordinary amount of wit. Now the younger one, Alcides, is not so quick; but this one, the moment he meets anything, a bug, a beetle, his little eyes start scurrying at once; he runs after it and notices it immediately. I intend him for the diplomatic service. Themistoclus," he continued, turning to him again, "do you want to be an ambassador?"
"I do," replied Themistoclus, chewing bread and wagging his head to the right and left.

At this moment the footman standing behind wiped the ambassador’s nose, and did very well too, otherwise a decent-sized extraneous drop would have plunged into the soup. The conversation at table began about the pleasure of a quiet life, interrupted by remarks from the mistress about the town theater and the actors. The tutor looked very attentively at the speakers, and as soon as he noticed that they were ready to smile, he opened his mouth that very minute and laughed with zeal. Probably he was a grateful man and wished to repay the master in this way for his good treatment. Once, however, his face took on a severe look, and he tapped strictly on the table, fixing his eyes upon the children sitting opposite him. This was appropriate, because Themistoclus had bitten Alcides by the ear, and Alcides, shutting his eyes and opening his mouth, was ready to sob in the most pitiful manner; but feeling that for this he might easily be deprived of a dish, he brought his mouth back to its former position and began with tears to gnaw a mutton bone, from which both his cheeks glistened with grease.

The mistress very often turned to Chichikov with the words: "You are eating nothing, you have taken very little." To which Chichikov replied every time: "Most humble thanks, I am full; a pleasant conversation is better than any dish."

They had already risen from the table. Manilov was extraordinarily pleased and, supporting his guest’s back with his hand, was preparing in this manner to conduct him into the drawing room, when suddenly the guest announced with a very significant look that he intended to speak with him about a very necessary matter.
"In that case, allow me to beg you into my study," said Manilov, and led the way into a small room with a window facing the blue forest. "Here is my corner," said Manilov.
"A pleasant little room," said Chichikov, casting his eyes over it.

The room was, indeed, not without pleasantness: the walls were painted a kind of bluish color resembling gray, there were four chairs, one armchair, a table on which lay the book with the bookmark, which we have already had occasion to mention, a few written papers, but most of all there was tobacco. It was in various forms: in paper packets, in a tobacco jar, and finally, it was poured simply in a heap on the table. On the windowsills, too, were placed mounds of ash knocked out of his pipe, arranged, not without care, in very neat rows. It was noticeable that this sometimes afforded the master a pastime.
"Allow me to beg you to dispose yourself in this armchair," said Manilov. "Here it will be more comfortable for you."
"Allow me, I will sit on a chair."
"Allow me not to allow you that," Manilov said with a smile. "This armchair is already assigned by me for a guest: whether you wish it or not, you must sit."
Chichikov sat down.
"Allow me to regale you with a pipe."
"No, I do not smoke," Chichikov replied amiably and as if with a look of regret.
"Why?" said Manilov, likewise amiably and with a look of regret.
"I have not formed the habit, I am afraid; they say a pipe dries one up."
"Allow me to observe to you that this is a prejudice. I even suppose that smoking a pipe is much healthier than taking snuff. In our regiment there was a lieutenant, a most excellent and most educated man, who never let the pipe out of his mouth, not only at table, but even, if I may be permitted to say so, in all other places. And now he is already over forty, but, thank God, up to now he is as healthy as can be."

Chichikov remarked that this did, indeed, happen, and that in nature there are many things inexplicable even to a vast mind.
"But allow me first one request…" he uttered in a voice in which there echoed some strange or almost strange expression, and immediately after, for some unknown reason, looked behind him. "How long is it since you chose to submit your census return (*revizskaya skazka*)?"
"Why, a long time ago; or better to say, I don't remember."
"Since that time, have many of your peasants died?"
"I cannot know; about this, I suppose, it is necessary to ask the bailiff. Hey, man! Call the bailiff, he should be here today."

Thereupon Manilov, with the same amiability, explained the matter to the coachman and even said "you" to him once.

The coachman, hearing that he had to pass two turnings and turn at the third, said: "We'll hit it, your honor,"—and Chichikov drove away, accompanied for a long time by the bows and handkerchief-wavings of the hosts, who raised themselves on their tiptoes.

Manilov stood on the porch for a long time, following the retreating chaise with his eyes, and when it had become completely invisible, he was still standing there, smoking his pipe. At last he went into the room, sat on a chair, and surrendered himself to meditation, inwardly rejoicing that he had afforded his guest a small pleasure. Then his thoughts drifted imperceptibly to other subjects and at last wandered God knows where. He thought of the bliss of a friendly life, of how good it would be to live with a friend on the bank of some river; then a bridge began to build itself across this river in his mind, then an enormous house with such a high belvedere that one could see even Moscow from it, and there drink tea in the evening in the open air and discuss some pleasant subjects. Then, that he and Chichikov had arrived together in some assembly in fine carriages, where they charmed everyone by the pleasantness of their manners, and that the Tsar, learning of such a friendship of theirs, had made them generals, and further, at last, God knows what, which he himself could in no way make out. Chichikov's strange request suddenly interrupted all his daydreams. The thought of it somehow particularly failed to digest in his head: no matter how he turned it over, he could in no way explain it to himself, and all the while he sat and smoked his pipe, which lasted until supper itself.
