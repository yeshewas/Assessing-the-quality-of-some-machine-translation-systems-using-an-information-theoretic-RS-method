Anton Pavlovich Chekhov

The Lady with the Dog

I

They said that a new face had appeared on the promenade: a lady with a dog. Dmitry Dmitritch Gurov, who had been living in Yalta for two weeks already and had grown accustomed to it, also began to take an interest in new faces. Sitting in the pavilion at Verne's, he saw a young lady walk past on the promenade, a blonde of small stature, wearing a beret: a white Spitz ran after her.

And then he met her in the city garden and in the square several times a day. She walked alone, always in the same beret, with the white Spitz; no one knew who she was, and they simply called her the lady with the dog.

"If she is here without a husband and without acquaintances," Gurov reasoned, "then it would not be amiss to make her acquaintance."

He was not yet forty, but he already had a daughter of twelve years and two sons in gymnasium. He had been married young, when he was still a second-year student, and now his wife seemed to him one and a half times older than himself. She was a tall woman, with dark eyebrows, upright, dignified, solid, and, as she called herself, a thinking woman. She read a lot, did not use the hard sign in letters, called her husband not Dmitry, but Dimitry, and he secretly considered her narrow-minded, limited, inelegant, was afraid of her, and did not like to be at home. He had begun to be unfaithful to her long ago, was unfaithful often, and probably for that reason he almost always spoke badly of women, and when women were discussed in his presence, he would call them thus:

"The lower race!"

It seemed to him that he had been sufficiently taught by bitter experience to call them as he pleased, but still he could not have lived even two days without the "lower race." In the company of men he felt bored, ill at ease; with them he was taciturn and cold, but when he found himself among women, he felt free and knew what to talk to them about and how to behave; and even to be silent with them was easy for him. In his appearance, in his character, in his whole nature there was something attractive, elusive, that disposed women toward him, that drew them to him; he knew this, and some force also drew him to them.

Repeated experience, truly bitter experience, had taught him long ago that every intimacy, which at first so pleasantly diversifies life and seems a charming and easy adventure, inevitably grows, among respectable people, especially among Muscovites, who are slow to move, irresolute, into an extremely complex task, and the situation in the end becomes burdensome. But at every new meeting with an interesting woman, this experience somehow slipped from his memory, and he wanted to live, and everything seemed so simple and amusing.

And so one evening he was dining in the garden, and the lady in the beret approached slowly to take the neighboring table. Her expression, her gait, her dress, her hairstyle told him that she was from respectable society, married, in Yalta for the first time and alone, that she was bored here. In the stories about the immorality of local customs there was much falsehood; he despised them and knew that such stories were mostly invented by people who would themselves gladly sin if they knew how, but when the lady sat down at the neighboring table three steps away from him, he remembered these stories about easy conquests, about trips to the mountains, and the seductive thought of a quick, fleeting liaison, of a romance with an unknown woman whose name and surname he did not know, suddenly took possession of him.

He beckoned the Spitz to him affectionately, and when it came, he wagged his finger at it. The Spitz growled. Gurov wagged his finger again.

The lady glanced at him and immediately lowered her eyes.

"He does not bite," she said, and blushed.

"May I give him a bone?" And when she nodded affirmatively, he asked pleasantly: "Have you been in Yalta long?"

"About five days."

"And I am already dragging out my second week here."

They were silent for a moment.

"Time passes quickly, and yet it is so boring here!" she said, not looking at him.

"It is only customary to say that it is boring here. An ordinary person lives somewhere in Belev or Zhizdra and is not bored, but he comes here: 'Oh, how boring! Oh, the dust!' You would think he came from Granada."

She laughed.Then both continued eating in silence, like strangers; but after dinner they walked side by side — and began a playful, light conversation of free people, content, to whom it mattered not where to go or what to talk about. They walked and talked about how strangely the sea was lit; the water was lilac in color, so soft and warm, and across it from the moon ran a golden stripe. They talked about how stuffy it was after the hot day. Ghurov told her that he was a Muscovite, by education a philologist, but worked in a bank; once prepared to sing in a private opera, but gave it up, owns two houses in Moscow… And from her he learned that she grew up in Petersburg, but married in S., where she had been living for two years already, that she would stay in Yalta for about another month and her husband might come for her, who also wanted to rest. She could not explain where her husband worked — in the provincial administration or in the provincial zemstvo board, and this amused her herself. And Ghurov also learned that her name was Anna Sergeyevna.

Later in his room he thought about her, about how tomorrow she would surely meet with him. It had to be so. Going to bed, he remembered that she had so recently been a schoolgirl, studied, just like his daughter now, remembered how much timidity, awkwardness there still was in her laugh, in her conversation with a stranger, — it must be the first time in her life she had been alone, in such circumstances, when people followed her, and looked at her, and spoke to her only with one secret purpose, which she could not help but guess. He remembered her thin, delicate neck, her beautiful, gray eyes.

"There is something pitiful about her all the same," — he thought and began to fall asleep.

II

A week passed after their acquaintance. It was a holiday. The rooms were stuffy, and in the streets dust whirled like a whirlwind, blowing off hats. All day one wanted to drink, and Ghurov often went into the pavilion and offered Anna Sergeyevna either water with syrup or ice cream. There was nowhere else to go.

In the evening, when it calmed down a bit, they went to the pier to see the steamship arrive. There were many people strolling on the dock; they had gathered to meet someone, holding bouquets. And here two peculiarities of the elegant Yalta crowd stood out distinctly: the elderly ladies were dressed like young women, and there were many generals.

Due to the rough sea, the steamship arrived late, after the sun had set, and before docking at the pier, it turned for a long time. Anna Sergeyevna looked through her lorgnette at the steamship and the passengers, as if searching for acquaintances, and when she turned to Ghurov, her eyes shone. She talked a lot, and her questions were abrupt, and she herself immediately forgot what she had asked; then she lost her lorgnette in the crowd.

The elegant crowd dispersed, faces were no longer visible, the wind died down completely, and Ghurov and Anna Sergeyevna stood, as if waiting to see if anyone else would come off the steamship. Anna Sergeyevna was now silent and smelled the flowers, not looking at Ghurov.

— The weather has gotten better toward evening, — he said. — Where shall we go now? Shall we go somewhere?

She did not answer anything.

Then he looked at her intently and suddenly embraced her and kissed her on the lips, and he was enveloped in the scent and moisture of flowers, and at once he looked around fearfully: had anyone seen?

— Come to your place… — he said quietly.

And both walked quickly.

In her room it was stuffy, smelling of perfume, which she had bought in a Japanese shop. Ghurov, looking at her now, thought: "What kinds of meetings there are in life!" From the past he retained a memory of carefree, good-natured women, cheerful from love, grateful to him for happiness, even if very brief; and of such — like, for example, his wife — who loved without sincerity, with excessive talk, affectedly, with hysteria, with such an expression as if it were not love, not passion, but something more significant; and of such two or three, very beautiful, cold ones, in whom suddenly flashed a predatory expression on their face, a stubborn desire to take, to snatch from life more than it could give, and these were not young women, capricious, unreasoning, domineering, not intelligent women, and when Ghurov grew cold toward them,that beauty aroused hatred in him and the lace on their underwear seemed to him then like scales.

     But here it was all the same timidity, the angularity of inexperienced youth, an awkward feeling; and there was an impression of confusion, as if someone had suddenly knocked on the door. Anna Sergeyevna, this "lady with the dog," responded to what had happened in a peculiar way, very seriously, as if to her own fall, — so it seemed, and this was strange and out of place. Her features had drooped, wilted, and long hair hung sadly on either side of her face, she sat in a dejected pose, like a sinner in an old painting.

     — This is wrong, — she said. — You don't respect me now.

     There was a watermelon on the table in the room. Gurov cut himself a slice and began to eat slowly. At least half an hour passed in silence.

     Anna Sergeyevna was touching, she emanated the purity of a decent, naive woman who had lived little; a single candle burning on the table barely lit her face, but it was clear that she was troubled in spirit.

     — Why would I stop respecting you? — asked Gurov. — You don't know what you're saying.

     — May God forgive me! — she said, and her eyes filled with tears. — This is terrible.

     — You're justifying yourself.

     — How can I justify myself? I'm a bad, base woman, I despise myself and don't think of justification. I haven't deceived my husband, but myself. And not just now, but for a long time I've been deceiving myself. My husband, perhaps, is an honest, good man, but he's a lackey! I don't know what he does there, how he serves, but I know only that he's a lackey. When I married him, I was twenty years old, curiosity tormented me, I wanted something better; after all, there is, — I told myself, — another life. I wanted to live! To live and live… Curiosity burned me… you don't understand this, but I swear to God, I could no longer control myself, something was happening to me, I couldn't be restrained, I told my husband I was ill, and came here… And here I walked about as if in a daze, like a madwoman… and now I've become a vulgar, worthless woman, whom anyone can despise.

     Gurov was already bored listening, he was irritated by her naive tone, this repentance, so unexpected and out of place; if it weren't for the tears in her eyes, one might have thought she was joking or playing a role.

     — I don't understand, — he said quietly, — what do you want?

     She hid her face on his chest and pressed against him.

     — Believe me, believe me, I beg you… — she said. — I love an honest, pure life, and sin is repugnant to me, I myself don't know what I'm doing. Simple people say: the unclean one led me astray. And I can now say of myself that the unclean one led me astray.

     — Enough, enough… — he murmured.

     He looked into her motionless, frightened eyes, kissed her, spoke softly and tenderly, and she gradually calmed down, and cheerfulness returned to her; they both began to laugh.

     Then, when they went out, there was not a soul on the embankment, the city with its cypresses looked quite dead, but the sea still roared and beat against the shore; a single boat swayed on the waves, and a lantern flickered sleepily on it.

     They found a cab and drove to Oreanda.

     — I just learned your surname downstairs in the hall: it's written on the board, von Diderits, — said Gurov. — Is your husband German?

     — No, it seems his grandfather was German, but he himself is Orthodox.

     In Oreanda they sat on a bench, not far from the church, looked down at the sea and were silent. Yalta was barely visible through the morning mist, white clouds stood motionless on the mountain peaks. The foliage didn't stir on the trees, cicadas cried out, and the monotonous, muffled sound of the sea, coming from below, spoke of peace, of the eternal sleep that awaits us. So it roared below when there was yet neither Yalta nor Oreanda, now it roars and will roar the same way, indifferent and muffled, when we are gone. And in this permanence, in complete indifference to the life and death of each of us, lies, perhaps, the guarantee of our eternal salvation, the continuous movement of life on earth, continuous perfection. Sitting beside a young woman who at dawn seemed so beautiful,Calmed and enchanted by the sight of this fairy-tale setting — the sea, mountains, clouds, wide sky, Gurov thought about how, in essence, if one really thinks about it, everything is beautiful in this world, everything except what we ourselves think and do when we forget about the higher purposes of existence, about our human dignity.

Some man approached — apparently a watchman — looked at them and left. And this detail seemed so mysterious and also beautiful. One could see how a steamship from Feodosia had arrived, lit by the morning glow, already without lights.

— Dew on the grass, — said Anna Sergeyevna after a silence.

— Yes. It's time to go home.

They returned to the city.

Then every afternoon they met on the embankment, had breakfast together, dined, walked, admired the sea. She complained that she slept poorly and that her heart beat anxiously, asked the same questions over and over, troubled now by jealousy, now by fear that he did not respect her enough. And often in the square or in the garden, when no one was near them, he would suddenly draw her to him and kiss her passionately. Complete idleness, these kisses in broad daylight, with caution and fear lest someone see, the heat, the smell of the sea and the constant flickering before his eyes of idle, well-dressed, well-fed people seemed to have transformed him; he told Anna Sergeyevna how beautiful she was, how seductive, was impatiently passionate, did not leave her side for a moment, while she often grew thoughtful and kept asking him to confess that he did not respect her, did not love her at all, but only saw in her a vulgar woman. Almost every evening later they would go somewhere outside the city, to Oreanda or to the waterfall; and the walk was successful, the impressions were invariably beautiful and majestic each time.

They waited for her husband to arrive. But a letter came from him in which he informed her that his eyes had become inflamed, and he begged his wife to return home as soon as possible. Anna Sergeyevna hurried.

— It is good that I am leaving, — she said to Gurov. — It is fate itself.

She went by carriage, and he saw her off. They drove all day. When she was getting into the express train car and when the second bell rang, she said:

— Let me look at you once more… Look once more. Like this.

She did not cry, but was sad, as if ill, and her face trembled.

— I will think of you… remember, — she said. — God be with you, stay. Do not remember me with ill will. We are parting forever, it is so necessary, because we should never have met at all. Well, God be with you.

The train left quickly, its lights soon disappeared, and a minute later the sound could no longer be heard, as if everything had conspired on purpose to end this sweet oblivion, this madness as quickly as possible. And, left alone on the platform and gazing into the dark distance, Gurov listened to the cry of crickets and the hum of telegraph wires with such a feeling as if he had just woken up. And he thought about how there had been yet another affair or adventure in his life, and it too had already ended, and now only a memory remained… He was moved, sad and felt a slight regret; after all, this young woman, whom he would never see again, had not been happy with him; he had been kind and cordial with her, but still in his manner with her, in his tone and caresses, there was a shadow of light mockery, a somewhat crude superiority of a happy man who was moreover almost twice her age. All the time she called him kind, extraordinary, elevated; evidently, he seemed to her not what he really was, which meant he had unwittingly deceived her…

Here at the station it already smelled of autumn, the evening was cool.

"It is time for me to go north too," thought Gurov, leaving the platform. "It is time!"

III

At home in Moscow everything was already winter-like, the stoves were heated, and in the mornings, when the children gathered to go to the gymnasium and drank tea, it was dark, and the nanny lit the fire for a short while. The frosts had already begun. When the first snow falls, on the first day of riding in sleighs, it is pleasant to see the white earth, white roofs, to breathe softly and wonderfully, and at this time one remembers one's youthful years. The old lindens and birches, white with frost, have a good-natured expression, they are closer to the heart than cypresses and palms,and near them he no longer wanted to think about mountains and the sea.

     Gurov was a Muscovite, and he returned to Moscow on a fine, frosty day, and when he put on his fur coat and warm gloves and walked along Petrovka, and when on Saturday evening he heard the ringing of bells, the recent trip and the places he had been lost all their charm for him. Gradually he plunged into Moscow life, and already eagerly read three newspapers a day and said that he did not read Moscow newspapers on principle. He was already drawn to restaurants, clubs, formal dinners, anniversaries, and he was already flattered that he had famous lawyers and actors among his acquaintances and that in the doctors' club he played cards with a professor. He could already eat a whole portion of selyanka in a frying pan…

     A month would pass, and it seemed to him that Anna Sergeyevna would be covered in his memory by fog and would only occasionally appear in dreams with a touching smile, as others had. But more than a month passed, deep winter came, and in his memory everything was clear, as if he had parted from Anna Sergeyevna only yesterday. And the memories burned ever more intensely. Whether children's voices came to his study in the evening silence, preparing their lessons, whether he heard a romance or an organ in a restaurant, or the blizzard howled in the fireplace, suddenly everything would come alive in his memory: and what had happened on the pier, and the early morning with fog on the mountains, and the steamer from Feodosia, and the kisses. He walked about the room for a long time, remembering and smiling, and then the memories turned into dreams, and the past in his imagination mingled with what was to come. Anna Sergeyevna did not appear to him in dreams, but followed him everywhere, like a shadow, and watched over him. Closing his eyes, he saw her as if alive, and she seemed more beautiful, younger, more tender than she had been; and he himself seemed better to himself than he had been then, in Yalta. She looked at him in the evenings from the bookcase, from the fireplace, from the corner, he heard her breathing, the tender rustle of her dress. On the street he followed women with his gaze, searching to see if any resembled her…

     And now a strong desire tormented him to share his memories with someone. But at home he could not speak of his love, and outside the home there was no one to speak to. Not with the lodgers, and not at the bank. And what was there to say? Did he love her then? Was there anything beautiful, poetic, or instructive, or simply interesting in his relations with Anna Sergeyevna? And he had to speak vaguely about love, about women, and no one guessed what it was about, and only his wife moved her dark eyebrows and said:

     — It doesn't suit you at all, Dmitry, to play the role of a ladies' man.

     One night, leaving the doctors' club with his partner, a civil servant, he could not restrain himself and said:

     — If you only knew what a charming woman I met in Yalta!

     The civil servant got into the sleigh and drove off, but suddenly turned around and called out:

     — Dmitry Dmitritch!

     — What?

     — By the way, you were right earlier: the sturgeon does have a smell!

     These words, so ordinary, somehow suddenly disturbed Gurov, seemed to him humiliating, unclean. What wild manners, what faces! What senseless nights, what uninteresting, unremarkable days! Frenzied card games, gluttony, drunkenness, constant talk about the same thing. Unnecessary affairs and talk about the same thing take up the best part of one's time, the best of one's strength, and in the end what remains is some sort of truncated, wingless life, some sort of nonsense, and one cannot leave and flee, as if one were sitting in a madhouse or in a prison cell!

     Gurov did not sleep all night and was indignant, and then spent the whole day with a headache. And in the following nights he slept poorly, sat in bed thinking or walked from corner to corner. He was tired of the children, tired of the bank, did not want to go anywhere, did not want to talk about anything.

     In December, during the holidays, he prepared to travel and told his wife that he was leaving for Petersburg to attend to the affairs of a young man, — and he went to S. Why? He himself did not know well. He wanted to see Anna Sergeyevna and talk to her, to arrange a meeting, if possible.

     He arrived in S. in the morning and took the best room in the hotel, where the entire floor was covered with gray soldier's cloth and there was an inkwell on the table, gray with dust, with a horseman on a horse,to whom a hand with a hat was raised, and the head was knocked off. The doorman gave him the necessary information: von Dideritc lives on Staro-Goncharnaya Street, in his own
     IV

And Anna Sergeyevna began to come to him in Moscow. Once every two or three months she would leave S. and tell her husband that she was going to consult with a professor about her female ailment,— and her husband believed and did not believe. Having arrived in Moscow, she would stay at the Slavyansky Bazaar and immediately send a man in a red cap to Gurov. Gurov would go to her, and no one in Moscow knew about it.

One day he was going to her in this manner on a winter morning (the messenger had come the evening before and had not found him). His daughter was with him, whom he wanted to escort to the gymnasium, and it was on the way. Large wet snow was falling.

— Now it's three degrees above zero, and yet it's snowing,— Gurov said to his daughter. — But you see, this warmth is only on the surface of the earth; in the upper layers of the atmosphere it's quite a different temperature.

— Papa, but why is there no thunder in winter?

He explained that too. He spoke and thought about how he was going to a rendezvous and not a single living soul knew about it and probably never would know. He had two lives: one open, which everyone who needed to know saw and knew, full of conventional truth and conventional deception, resembling completely the life of his acquaintances and friends, and another— which proceeded in secret. And by some strange concurrence of circumstances, perhaps accidental, everything that was important to him, interesting, necessary, in which he was sincere and did not deceive himself, that which constituted the essence of his life, happened in secret from others; while everything that was his lie, his shell, in which he hid to conceal the truth, such as his work in the bank, arguments at the club, his "lower race," going with his wife to jubilees,— all this was open. And he judged others by himself, did not believe what he saw, and always assumed that every person had, beneath the cover of secrecy, as beneath the cover of night, his real, most interesting life. Every personal existence is sustained by secrecy, and perhaps partly for this reason the cultured man is so nervously concerned that personal secrecy be respected.

Having escorted his daughter to the gymnasium, Gurov went to the Slavyansky Bazaar. He took off his fur coat downstairs, went upstairs and knocked softly at the door. Anna Sergeyevna, dressed in his favorite gray dress, tired from the journey and waiting, had been expecting him since the evening before; she was pale, looked at him and did not smile, and as soon as he entered, she already pressed herself to his chest. As if they had not seen each other for two years, their kiss was long, prolonged.

— Well, how are you getting on there?— he asked. — What's new?

— Wait, I'll tell you in a moment… I can't.

She could not speak because she was crying. She turned away from him and pressed a handkerchief to her eyes.

"Well, let her cry, and I'll sit here for a while,"— he thought and sat down in an armchair.

Then he rang and asked for tea to be brought; and then, while he drank tea, she stood the whole time, turned toward the window… She was crying from agitation, from the sorrowful consciousness that their life had turned out so sadly; they see each other only in secret, hide from people like thieves! Was their life not ruined?

— Well, stop it!— he said.

It was clear to him that this love of theirs would not end soon, no one knew when. Anna Sergeyevna was becoming more and more attached to him, adored him, and it would be unthinkable to tell her that all this must someday come to an end; besides, she would not have believed it.

He went up to her and took her by the shoulders to caress her, to joke, and at that moment saw himself in the mirror.

His head was already beginning to turn gray. And it seemed strange to him that he had aged so much in recent years, had become so ugly. The shoulders on which his hands lay were warm and trembled. He felt compassion for this life, still so warm and beautiful, but probably already close to beginning to fade and wither, like his own life. Why does she love him so? He had always seemed to women not what he was, and they loved in him not him himself, but the man created by their imagination, whom they eagerly sought in their lives; and then, when they noticed their mistake, they still loved. And not one of them had been happy with him. Time passed, he became acquainted, grew close,He had parted ways with women many times, but had never truly loved; there had been everything imaginable, but never love itself.

     And only now, when his hair had turned gray, did he fall in love as one should, genuinely — for the first time in his life.

     Anna Sergeyevna and he loved each other like very close, kindred people, like husband and wife, like tender friends; it seemed to them that fate itself had destined them for each other, and it was incomprehensible why he was married and she was married; and they were exactly like two migratory birds, male and female, that had been caught and forced to live in separate cages. They forgave each other for what they had been ashamed of in their past, forgave everything in the present, and felt that this love of theirs had changed them both.

     Before, in sad moments, he would comfort himself with all sorts of reasoning that came to mind, but now he had no time for reasoning; he felt deep compassion, wanted to be sincere, tender…

     "Stop now, my dear," he said. "You've cried enough… Now let's talk, let's think of something."

     Then they deliberated for a long time, spoke about how to free themselves from the need to hide, to deceive, to live in different cities, to go long periods without seeing each other. How to break free from these unbearable bonds?

     "How? How?" he asked, clutching his head. "How?"

     And it seemed that just a little more — and a solution would be found, and then a new, beautiful life would begin; and both felt clearly that there was still a very long way to go and that the most complex and difficult part was only just beginning.

The Black Monk

I

Andrey Vasilievich Kovrin, a master of arts, had grown weary and had upset his nerves. He did not seek treatment, but somehow, in passing, over a bottle of wine, he spoke with a doctor friend, who advised him to spend the spring and summer in the country. A long letter from Tanya Pesotskaya came at just the right moment, in which she asked him to come to Borisovka and stay with them. And he decided that he really did need to make the trip.

     At first — this was in April — he went to his own ancestral estate, Kovrinка, and lived there in solitude for three weeks; then, waiting for the roads to improve, he set out on horseback to his former guardian and tutor Pesotsky, a renowned horticulturist in Russia. From Kovrinка to Borisovka, where the Pesotskys lived, was considered no more than seventy versts, and traveling along the soft spring road in a comfortable sprung carriage was a true pleasure.

     Pesotsky's house was enormous, with columns and lions, from which the plaster had flaked off, and with a footman in a tailcoat at the entrance. An ancient park, gloomy and austere, laid out in the English manner, stretched for nearly a whole verst from the house to the river and ended there with a steep, abrupt clay bank, on which grew pines with exposed roots resembling shaggy paws; below, the water gleamed inhospitably, sandpipers flew about with plaintive cries, and there was always such a mood there that one might as well sit down and write a ballad. But near the house itself, in the courtyard and in the fruit garden, which together with the nurseries occupied about thirty acres, it was cheerful and full of life even in bad weather. Such wonderful roses, lilies, camellias, such tulips of every conceivable color, from bright white to black as soot, in general such a wealth of flowers as Kovrin had never seen anywhere else. Spring was still only beginning, and the true luxury of the flower beds was still hidden in the greenhouses, but even what was blooming along the paths and here and there on the flower beds was enough to feel, while walking through the garden, as though in a realm of delicate colors, especially in the early hours, when dew sparkled on every petal.

     What was the decorative part of the garden and what Pesotsky himself contemptuously called trifles had once made an impression on Kovrin in childhood like something from a fairy tale. What whimsies were not there, refined deformities and mockeries of nature! There were espaliers of fruit trees, a pear shaped like a pyramidal poplar, spherical oaks and lindens, an apple tree umbrella, arches, monograms, candelabras, and even 1862 made of plums — a number indicating the year when Pesotsky first took up horticulture.There were also beautiful slender little trees with straight and sturdy trunks like those of palms, and only upon close inspection could one recognize in these little trees gooseberry or currant bushes. But what most delighted in the garden and gave it a lively appearance was the constant movement. From early morning until evening, around the trees, bushes, on the paths and flower beds, people bustled about like ants with wheelbarrows, hoes, watering cans…

Kovrin arrived at the Pesotskys' in the evening, at ten o'clock. He found Tanya and her father, Yegor Semyonich, in great anxiety. The clear, starry sky and the thermometer predicted frost by morning, and meanwhile the gardener Ivan Karlych had gone to town and there was no one to rely on. During supper they spoke only of the frost and it was decided that Tanya would not go to sleep and at one o'clock would walk through the garden and check that everything was in order, while Yegor Semyonich would get up at three o'clock and even earlier.

Kovrin sat with Tanya all evening and after midnight set off with her into the garden. It was cold. In the yard there was already a strong smell of smoke. In the large fruit garden, which was called the commercial garden and brought Yegor Semyonich several thousand rubles of net income annually, black, thick, acrid smoke lay on the ground and, enveloping the trees, protected these thousands from frost. The trees here stood in a checkerboard pattern, their rows were straight and regular, like soldiers in formation, and this strict pedantic regularity and the fact that all the trees were the same height and had absolutely identical crowns and trunks made the picture monotonous and even tedious. Kovrin and Tanya walked along the rows where fires of manure, straw, and all sorts of refuse smoldered, and occasionally they encountered workers who wandered in the smoke like shadows. Only cherries, plums, and some varieties of apples were in bloom, but the entire garden was shrouded in smoke, and only near the nurseries did Kovrin breathe deeply.

– I used to sneeze from the smoke here as a child, – he said, shrugging his shoulders, – but I still don't understand how smoke can protect against frost.

– Smoke replaces clouds when there are none… – Tanya answered.

– And what are clouds needed for?

– There are no frosts on cloudy and overcast days.

– Is that so!

He laughed and took her hand. Her broad, very serious, chilled face with thin black eyebrows, the raised collar of her coat that prevented her from moving her head freely, and she herself, thin, slender, in a dress dampened by dew, touched his heart.

– Good Lord, she's already grown up! – he said. – When I left here for the last time, five years ago, you were still just a child. You were so thin, long-legged, with your hair down, wore a short dress, and I teased you calling you a heron… What time does!

– Yes, five years! – Tanya sighed. – Much water has flowed under the bridge since then. Tell me, Andryusha, honestly, – she spoke animatedly, looking him in the face, – have you grown distant from us? Though why do I ask? You're a man, you're already living your own, interesting life, you're a person of importance… Estrangement is so natural! But however it may be, Andryusha, I want you to consider us yours. We have the right to that.

– I do consider you that, Tanya.

– On your word of honor?

– Yes, on my word of honor.

– You were surprised today that we have so many photographs of you. You know, my father adores you. Sometimes it seems to me that he loves you more than he loves me. He's proud of you. You're a scholar, an extraordinary man, you've made a brilliant career for yourself, and he's convinced that you turned out this way because he raised you. I don't prevent him from thinking so. Let him.

Dawn was already beginning, and this was especially noticeable by the clarity with which the clouds of smoke and the crowns of the trees began to stand out in the air. Nightingales sang, and from the fields came the cry of quails.

– However, it's time to sleep, – said Tanya. – And it's cold. – She took his arm. – Thank you, Andryusha, for coming. We have uninteresting acquaintances, and there are few of them. We have only the garden, garden, garden, – and nothing else. Standard, half-standard, – she laughed, – aport, ranet, borovinka, budding, grafting… Our entire life has gone into the garden, I don't even dream of anything except apple trees and pears. Of course, it's good, useful, but sometimes one wants something else for variety.I remember when you used to come to us on vacation or just like that, the house would become somehow fresher and brighter, as if they were removing covers from the chandelier and furniture. I was a girl then and still understood.

     She spoke for a long time and with great feeling. For some reason it suddenly occurred to him that during the summer he might become attached to this small, weak, talkative creature, become fascinated and fall in love – in their situation it was so possible and natural! This thought both touched and amused him; he bent down to the dear, worried face and sang softly:

     Onegin, I shall not conceal,

     I love Tatyana with mad zeal…

     When they came home, Egor Semenyich had already gotten up. Kovrin did not want to sleep, he started a conversation with the old man and returned with him to the garden. Egor Semenyich was tall, broad-shouldered, with a large belly and suffered from shortness of breath, but always walked so quickly that it was hard to keep up with him. He looked extremely worried, always hurrying somewhere with such an expression as if, should he be late even by a minute, everything would be lost!

     – Well, brother, here's a puzzle… – he began, stopping to catch his breath. – On the surface of the earth, as you see, there's frost, but raise a thermometer on a stick two fathoms above the ground, and there it's warm… Why is that?

     – I really don't know, – said Kovrin and laughed.

     – Hm… One can't know everything, of course… However broad the mind may be, you can't fit everything into it. You're mostly into philosophy, aren't you?

     – Yes. I read psychology, and I'm generally occupied with philosophy.

     – And doesn't it bore you?

     – On the contrary, this is all I live for.

     – Well, God bless you… – said Egor Semenyich, thoughtfully stroking his gray whiskers. – God bless you… I'm very glad for you… glad, brother…

     But suddenly he listened intently and, making a terrible face, ran off to the side and soon disappeared behind the trees, in clouds of smoke.

     – Who tied the horse to the apple tree? – came his desperate, soul-wrenching cry. – What scoundrel and villain dared tie the horse to the apple tree? My God, my God! They've ruined it, frozen it, defiled it, spoiled it! The garden is lost! The garden is ruined! My God!

     When he returned to Kovrin, his face was exhausted, offended.

     – What can you do with this accursed people? – he said in a tearful voice, throwing up his hands. – Stepka was hauling manure at night and tied the horse to the apple tree! The scoundrel wrapped the reins so tight that the bark was rubbed off in three places. Can you imagine! I tell him, and he – just pushes and pushes and only blinks his eyes! Hanging is too good for him!

     Having calmed down, he embraced Kovrin and kissed him on the cheek.

     – Well, God bless you… God bless you… – he muttered. – I'm very glad you came. Unspeakably glad… Thank you.

     Then, with the same quick gait and worried expression, he walked through the entire garden and showed his former pupil all the greenhouses, hothouses, ground sheds and his two apiaries, which he called a marvel of our century.

     While they walked, the sun rose and brightly lit up the garden. It became warm. Anticipating a clear, cheerful, long day, Kovrin remembered that it was still only the beginning of May and that a whole summer lay ahead, just as clear, cheerful and long, and suddenly a joyful youthful feeling stirred in his chest, the kind he had experienced in childhood when he ran through this garden. And he himself embraced the old man and tenderly kissed him. Both, moved, went into the house and began to drink tea from old porcelain cups, with cream, with rich, sweet pretzels – and these trifles again reminded Kovrin of his childhood and youth. The beautiful present and the impressions of the past awakening in him merged together; from them his soul felt crowded, but good.

     He waited until Tanya woke up, and had coffee with her, took a walk, then went to his room and sat down to work. He read attentively, made notes and occasionally raised his eyes to look at the open windows or at the fresh flowers still wet with dew standing in vases on the table, and again lowered his eyes to the book, and it seemed to him that every fiber in him trembled and played with pleasure.

II

In the village he continued to lead the same nervous and restless life as in the city.He read and wrote a great deal, studied Italian, and when he took walks, he enjoyed thinking about how soon he would sit down to work again. He slept so little that everyone was amazed; if he happened to fall asleep during the day for half an hour, he would not sleep all night afterward, and after a sleepless night, he felt cheerful and vigorous as if nothing had happened.

He talked a great deal, drank wine and smoked expensive cigars. The Pesotskys were often visited, almost every day, by young ladies from neighboring estates, who played the piano and sang together with Tanya; sometimes a young man, a neighbor, who played the violin well, would come. Kovrín listened to the music and singing with avidity and was exhausted by them, and this exhaustion was expressed physically in that his eyes would droop and his head would loll to the side.

One evening after tea he was sitting on the balcony and reading. In the drawing room at that time Tanya – soprano, one of the young ladies – contralto, and the young man on violin were rehearsing the famous serenade by Braga. Kovrín listened to the words – they were in Russian – and could not understand their meaning at all. Finally, putting down his book and listening carefully, he understood: a girl, sick with imagination, heard mysterious sounds at night in the garden, so beautiful and strange that she had to recognize them as sacred harmony, which we mortals cannot understand and therefore flies back to the heavens. Kovrín's eyes began to droop. He stood up and, exhausted, walked through the drawing room, then through the hall. When the singing stopped, he took Tanya by the arm and went out with her onto the balcony.

– A legend has occupied my mind since this morning, – he said. – I don't remember whether I read it somewhere or heard it, but it's a strange legend, not consistent with anything. To begin with, it lacks clarity. A thousand years ago some monk, dressed in black, was walking through the desert somewhere in Syria or Arabia… Several miles from where he was walking, fishermen saw another black monk who was slowly moving across the surface of a lake. This second monk was a mirage. Now forget all the laws of optics, which the legend seems not to recognize, and listen further. From the mirage came another mirage, then from that one a third, so that the image of the black monk began to be transmitted endlessly from one layer of the atmosphere to another. He was seen now in Africa, now in Spain, now in India, now in the Far North… Finally, he went beyond the limits of the earth's atmosphere and now wanders throughout the entire universe, never quite falling into those conditions under which he could fade away. Perhaps he is being seen now somewhere on Mars or on some star of the Southern Cross. But, my dear, the very essence, the very crux of the legend lies in the fact that exactly a thousand years after the monk walked through the desert, the mirage will again enter the earth's atmosphere and appear to people. And it seems that this thousand years is already coming to an end… According to the meaning of the legend, we should expect the black monk not today – tomorrow.

– A strange mirage, – said Tanya, who did not like the legend.

– But the most amazing thing, – Kovrín laughed, – is that I cannot for the life of me remember where this legend came into my head. Did I read it somewhere? Did I hear it? Or perhaps the black monk came to me in a dream? I swear by God, I don't remember. But the legend occupies me. I've been thinking about it all day.

After sending Tanya back to the guests, he left the house and walked thoughtfully around the flower beds. The sun was already setting. The flowers, because they had just been watered, gave off a damp, irritating smell. In the house they sang again, and from a distance the violin produced the impression of a human voice. Kovrín, straining his mind to remember where he had heard or read the legend, made his way slowly into the park and imperceptibly reached the river.

Along the path that ran along the steep bank past exposed roots, he descended to the water, disturbed some sandpipers there, and startled two ducks. On the gloomy pines here and there the last rays of the setting sun still glimmered, but on the surface of the river it was already true evening. Kovrín crossed to the other side on the logs. Before him now lay a wide field covered with young rye not yet in bloom. Not a human dwelling, not a living soul in the distance, and it seems that the path, if one were to follow it,will lead to that very unknown mysterious place,
– Well, you see… – Kovrin mumbled. – So there is truth in the legend.

     Without trying to explain to himself this strange phenomenon, satisfied merely that he had managed to see so closely and so clearly not only the monk's black clothing, but even his face and eyes, pleasantly agitated, he returned home.

     People were walking peacefully in the park and in the garden, people were playing in the house – which meant only he alone had seen the monk. He very much wanted to tell Tanya and Egor Semenovich everything, but he realized that they would probably consider his words to be delirium, and this would frighten them; it was better to keep silent. He laughed loudly, sang, danced the mazurka, he was cheerful, and everyone, the guests and Tanya, found that today he had a special kind of face, radiant, inspired, and that he was very interesting.

III

     After dinner, when the guests had left, he went to his room and lay down on the sofa: he wanted to think about the monk. But a minute later Tanya came in.

     – Here, Andryusha, read father's articles, – she said, handing him a bundle of pamphlets and offprints. – Excellent articles. He writes very well.

     – Well, excellent indeed! – said Egor Semenovich, coming in after her and laughing awkwardly; he felt ashamed. – Don't listen, please, don't read! Though, if you want to fall asleep, then perhaps read: an excellent sleeping draught.

     – In my opinion, magnificent articles, – said Tanya with deep conviction. – You read them, Andryusha, and convince papa to write more often. He could write a complete course on horticulture.

     Egor Semenovich laughed tensely, turned red, and began to speak the phrases that embarrassed authors usually speak. Finally, he began to give in.

     – In that case, read first the article by Goshe and these Russian little articles, – he mumbled, turning over the pamphlets with trembling hands, – otherwise it will be incomprehensible to you. Before reading my objections, you need to know what I am objecting to. Though, it's nonsense… tedious stuff. And it's time to sleep, I think.

     Tanya left. Egor Semenovich sat down next to Kovrin on the sofa and sighed deeply.

     – Yes, my friend… – he began after some silence. – So it is, my dear master. Here I am writing articles, and participating in exhibitions, and receiving medals… Pesotsky, they say, has apples the size of a head, and Pesotsky, they say, made his fortune from the garden. In a word, Kochubey is rich and famous. But the question is: what is all this for? The garden is indeed beautiful, exemplary… It is not a garden, but a whole institution of high state importance, because it is, so to speak, a step into a new era of Russian agriculture and Russian industry. But what for? What is the goal?

     – The work speaks for itself.

     – I don't mean it in that sense. I want to ask: what will happen to the garden when I die? In the form in which you see it now, it won't last a month without me. The whole secret of success is not that the garden is large and there are many workers, but that I love the work – do you understand? – love it, perhaps more than myself. Look at me: I do everything myself. I work from morning to night. I do all the grafting myself, the pruning myself, the planting myself, everything myself. When people help me, I become jealous and irritable to the point of rudeness. The whole secret is in love, that is, in a watchful master's eye, and in a master's hands, and in that feeling when you go somewhere as a guest for an hour, you sit there, but your heart is not in place, you are beside yourself: you fear that something might happen in the garden. And when I die, who will look after it? Who will work? The gardener? The workers? Yes? So here is what I will tell you, my dear friend: the first enemy in our business is not the hare, not the beetle, and not the frost, but a stranger.

     – What about Tanya? – asked Kovrin, laughing. – It cannot be that she is more harmful than a hare. She loves and understands the work.

     – Yes, she loves and understands. If after my death the garden falls to her and she becomes the mistress, then of course nothing better could be wished for. But what if, God forbid, she gets married? – whispered Egor Semenovich and looked at Kovrin in fright. – That's just it! She gets married, children come, and then there is no time to think about the garden.

     – What I am mainly afraid of: she marries some young fellow, and he will be stingy and lease the garden to merchants,and everything will go to hell in the first year! In our business, women are a scourge of God!

     Egor Semenyich sighed and was silent for a moment.

     – Perhaps this is selfish, but I'll speak frankly: I don't want Tanya to get married. I'm afraid! There's a dandy who comes to visit us with a violin and plays; I know that Tanya won't marry him, I know it well, but I can't stand to see him! In general, brother, I'm quite an eccentric. I confess it.

     Egor Semenyich stood up and walked about the room in agitation, and it was clear that he wanted to say something very important but didn't dare.

     – I love you dearly and will speak to you frankly, – he finally resolved, thrusting his hands into his pockets. – I approach certain delicate questions simply and speak directly what I think, and I can't stand so-called secret thoughts. I'll speak plainly: you are the only man I wouldn't be afraid to give my daughter to. You are a clever man, with a good heart, and you wouldn't let my beloved work perish. And the main reason is that I love you like a son… and I'm proud of you. If you and Tanya were to have a romance somehow, then – what of it? I would be very glad and even happy. I say this plainly, without affectation, as an honest man.

     Kovrin laughed. Egor Semenyich opened the door to leave and stopped on the threshold.

     – If you and Tanya had a son, I would make him a gardener, – he said, after thinking. – However, this is an empty dream… Good night.

     Left alone, Kovrin lay down more comfortably and took up the articles. One had such a title: "On Intermediate Culture," another: "A Few Words Regarding the Note of Mr. Z. on Replanting Soil for a New Garden," a third: "More on Budding with a Dormant Eye" – and all of that sort. But what a restless, uneven tone, what a nervous, almost morbid fervor! Here is an article, it seems, with the most peaceful title and indifferent content: it speaks of the Russian Antonovka apple. But Egor Semenyich begins it with "audiatur altera pars" 1 and ends with "sapienti sat" 2, and between these sayings a whole fountain of various poisonous words addressed to "the learned ignorance of our patented gentlemen gardeners, observing nature from the heights of their chairs," or Mr. Goche, "whose success was created by laymen and dilettantes," and here an ill-timed and insincere regret that peasants who steal fruit and break trees in the process can no longer be beaten with rods.

     "The work is beautiful, lovely, healthy, but even here there are passions and war," thought Kovrin. "It must be that everywhere and in all fields ideological people are nervous and distinguished by heightened sensitivity. Probably, this is necessary."

     He remembered Tanya, who loves Egor Semenyich's articles so much. Of small stature, pale, thin, so that her collarbones are visible; eyes wide open, dark, intelligent, always looking somewhere and searching for something; gait, like her father's, small, hurried. She talks a lot, loves to argue, and with every even insignificant phrase she accompanies it with expressive mimicry and gesticulation. She must be nervous in the highest degree.

     Kovrin continued reading, but understood nothing and gave up. The pleasant excitement, the very same with which he had danced the mazurka and listened to music earlier, now tormented him and aroused in him many thoughts. He rose and began to walk about the room, thinking of the black monk. It occurred to him that if this strange, supernatural monk was seen only by him alone, then it meant he was ill and had already reached hallucinations. This thought frightened him, but not for long.

     "But I feel well, and I do no harm to anyone; therefore, there is nothing bad in my hallucinations," – he thought, and he felt good again.

     He sat on the sofa and embraced his head with his hands, restraining the incomprehensible joy that filled his entire being, then walked again and sat down to work. But the thoughts he read from the book did not satisfy him. He wanted something gigantic, boundless, striking. Toward morning he undressed and reluctantly lay down in bed: he had to sleep!

     When the footsteps of Egor Semenyich, who was leaving for the garden, were heard, Kovrin rang and ordered the footman to bring wine. He drank several glasses of Lafite with pleasure,then hid under the blanket; his consciousness grew foggy, and he fell asleep.

IV

Egor Semenyich and Tanya often quarreled and said unpleasant things to each other.

One morning they had a disagreement about something. Tanya cried and went to her room. She did not come out for lunch or tea. Egor Semenyich at first walked about looking important and offended, as if wishing to make it clear that for him the interests of justice and order were above all else in the world, but soon could not maintain his composure and lost heart. He wandered sadly through the park and kept sighing: "oh, my God, my God!" and at lunch he did not eat a single crumb. Finally, feeling guilty and tormented by conscience, he knocked on the locked door and called out timidly:

– Tanya! Tanya?

And in response, from behind the door came a weak voice, exhausted from tears and at the same time resolute:

– Leave me alone, I beg you.

The anguish of the hosts was reflected in the entire house, even in the people who worked in the garden. Kovrin was absorbed in his interesting work, but toward the end even he became bored and uncomfortable. To somehow dispel the general bad mood, he decided to intervene and knocked on Tanya's door before evening. He was let in.

– Oh dear, how shameful! – he began jokingly, looking with surprise at Tanya's tear-stained face, covered with red blotches, sorrowful and sad. – Is it really so serious? Oh dear!

– But if you only knew how he torments me! – she said, and tears, burning, abundant tears burst from her large eyes. – He has tormented me! – she continued, wringing her hands. – I said nothing to him… nothing… I only said that there is no need to keep… extra workers, if… if one can, whenever one wishes, hire day laborers. After all… after all the workers have done nothing for a whole week… I… I only said that, and he started shouting and said many hurtful things to me… deeply insulting things. Why?

– Come now, come now, – said Kovrin, adjusting her hair. – You quarreled, you cried, and that's that. One cannot stay angry for long, it is not good… especially since he loves you infinitely.

– He… he has ruined my whole life, – Tanya continued, sobbing. – All I hear are insults and… and offenses. He thinks I am superfluous in his house. Well? He is right. I will leave tomorrow, I will become a telegraph operator… Let him…

– Now, now, now… Do not cry, Tanya. Do not, my dear… You are both hot-tempered, irritable, and both are to blame. Come, I will make peace between you.

Kovrin spoke tenderly and persuasively, and she continued to cry, her shoulders trembling and her hands clenched, as if she had truly been struck by some terrible misfortune. He felt sorry for her all the more because her grief was not serious, yet she suffered deeply. What trifles were enough to make this creature unhappy for a whole day, and perhaps for her whole life! Comforting Tanya, Kovrin thought about the fact that besides this girl and her father, in the whole world you could not find with a candle in daylight people who loved him as their own, as a blood relative; if it were not for these two people, then perhaps he, having lost his father and mother in early childhood, would never have known until his death what sincere affection was and that naive, unquestioning love that is given only to very close, blood relations. And he felt that his semi-sick, frayed nerves responded to the nerves of this crying, trembling girl as iron responds to a magnet. He could never have loved a healthy, strong, rosy-cheeked woman, but pale, weak, unhappy Tanya pleased him.

And he willingly stroked her hair and shoulders, squeezed her hands and wiped away her tears… Finally, she stopped crying. She complained for a long time more about her father and her heavy, unbearable life in this house, begging Kovrin to understand her position; then gradually began to smile and sigh that God had given her such a bad character, and finally, laughing loudly, called herself a fool and ran out of the room.

When shortly after Kovrin went out into the garden, Egor Semenyich and Tanya were already walking side by side along the path as if nothing had happened, and both were eating rye bread with salt, as both were hungry.

V

Pleased that he had succeeded so well in the role of peacemaker, Kovrin went into the park. Sitting on a bench and reflecting,He heard the sound of carriages and women's laughter – guests had arrived. When evening shadows began to fall in the garden, the faint sounds of a violin, singing voices were heard, and this reminded him of the black monk. Somewhere, in what country or on what planet does this optical absurdity now wander?

Hardly had he recalled the legend and drawn in his imagination that dark phantom which he had seen in the rye field, when from behind the pine tree, directly opposite, there came out silently, without the slightest rustle, a man of medium height with an uncovered gray head, all in dark clothes and barefoot, resembling a beggar, and on his pale, as if dead face, black eyebrows stood out sharply. Nodding affably, this beggar or wanderer approached the bench noiselessly and sat down, and Kovrin recognized in him the black monk. For a moment both looked at each other – Kovrin with astonishment, and the monk affectionately and, as before, somewhat slyly, with an expression of having something on his mind.

– But you are a mirage, – said Kovrin. – Why then are you here and sitting in one place? This does not accord with the legend.

– It is all the same, – answered the monk not at once, in a quiet voice, turning his face toward him. – The legend, the mirage and I – all this is a product of your excited imagination. I am a phantom.

– So you do not exist? – asked Kovrin.

– Think as you wish, – said the monk and smiled weakly. – I exist in your imagination, and your imagination is part of nature, therefore, I exist in nature as well.

– You have a very old, wise and extremely expressive face, as if you had really lived more than a thousand years, – said Kovrin. – I did not know that my imagination was capable of creating such phenomena. But why do you look at me with such rapture? Do you like me?

– Yes. You are one of those few who are justly called the chosen of God. You serve eternal truth. Your thoughts, intentions, your wonderful science and your entire life bear the divine, heavenly seal, because they are devoted to the rational and the beautiful, that is, to that which is eternal.

– You said: eternal truth… But is eternal truth accessible and necessary to people if there is no eternal life?

– Eternal life exists, – said the monk.

– Do you believe in the immortality of people?

– Yes, of course. A great and brilliant future awaits you, people. And the more such as you there are on earth, the sooner this future will be realized. Without you, servants of the higher principle, living consciously and freely, humanity would be insignificant; developing in the natural order, it would wait much longer for the end of its earthly history. You will introduce it into the kingdom of eternal truth several thousand years sooner – and in this lies your high merit. You embody the blessing of God which has rested upon people.

– And what is the purpose of eternal life? – asked Kovrin.

– Like all life – enjoyment. True enjoyment lies in knowledge, and eternal life will present countless and inexhaustible sources for knowledge, and in this sense it is said: in my Father's house are many mansions.

– If you knew how pleasant it is to listen to you! – said Kovrin, rubbing his hands with pleasure.

– I am very glad.

– But I know: when you leave, I will be troubled by the question of your essence. You are a phantom, a hallucination. So I am mentally ill, abnormal?

– Even if so. Why be troubled? You are ill because you worked beyond your strength and became exhausted, and this means that you have sacrificed your health to an idea and the time is near when you will give it your very life. What could be better? This is what all gifted noble natures strive for.

– If I know that I am mentally ill, can I trust myself?

– But why do you know that the brilliant people whom the whole world trusts also did not see phantoms? Scientists now say that genius is akin to madness. My friend, only ordinary, herd people are healthy and normal. Considerations about the nervous age, overwork, degeneration and so forth can seriously trouble only those who see the purpose of life in the present, that is, herd people.

– The Romans said: mens sana in corpore sano.

– Not everything is true,what the Romans or Greeks used to say. Elevated mood, excitement, ecstasy – all that which distinguishes prophets, poets, martyrs for an idea from ordinary people, is contrary to the animal side of man, that is, to his physical health. I repeat: if you want to be healthy and normal, go join the herd.

     – Strange, you're repeating what often comes to my own mind, – said Kovrin. – It's as if you've peeped and overheard my innermost thoughts. But let's not talk about me. What do you understand by eternal truth?

     The monk did not answer. Kovrin looked at him and could not make out his face: its features grew hazy and blurred. Then the monk's head and hands began to disappear; his torso merged with the bench and the evening twilight, and he vanished completely.

     – The hallucination is over! – said Kovrin and laughed. – What a pity.

     He walked back to the house cheerful and happy. The little that the black monk had told him flattered not his vanity, but his entire soul, his entire being. To be chosen, to serve eternal truth, to stand among those who thousands of years earlier will make humanity worthy of God's kingdom, that is, will save people from several thousand extra years of struggle, sin and suffering, to give one's entire self to an idea – youth, strength, health, to be ready to die for the common good, – what a lofty, what a happy destiny! His past flashed through his memory, pure, chaste, full of labor; he recalled what he had learned and what he himself had taught others, and decided that there was no exaggeration in the monk's words.

     Tanya was walking toward him through the park. She was wearing a different dress now.

     – You're here? – she said. – We've been looking for you, looking… But what's wrong with you? – she wondered, looking at his rapturous, radiant face and eyes full of tears. – How strange you are, Andryusha.

     – I'm content, Tanya, – said Kovrin, placing his hands on her shoulders. – I'm more than content, I'm happy! Tanya, dear Tanya, you are an extraordinarily charming being. Dear Tanya, I'm so glad, so glad!

     He warmly kissed both her hands and continued:

     – I have just experienced bright, wonderful, unearthly moments. But I cannot tell you everything, because you will call me mad or not believe me. Let's talk about you. Dear, wonderful Tanya! I love you and have already grown accustomed to loving you. Your presence, our meetings ten times a day have become a necessity of my soul. I don't know how I'll manage without you when I leave for home.

     – Well! – laughed Tanya. – You'll forget about us in two days. We are small people, and you are a great man.

     – No, let's speak seriously! – he said. – I'll take you with me, Tanya. Yes? Will you come with me? Do you want to be mine?

     – Well! – said Tanya and wanted to laugh again, but the laughter didn't come, and red patches appeared on her face.

     She began to breathe heavily and walked quickly, quickly, but not toward the house, but further into the park.

     – I didn't think about this… didn't think! – she said, as if in despair, wringing her hands.

     And Kovrin walked after her and spoke with the same radiant, rapturous face:

     – I want a love that would seize me entirely, and only you, Tanya, can give me this love. I'm happy! Happy!

     She was stunned, bent over, shrank, and seemed to age ten years all at once, while he found her beautiful and loudly expressed his rapture:

     – How beautiful she is!
