Nikolai Vasilyevich Gogol

Dead Souls





Dead Souls

Poem

VOLUME ONE



CHAPTER ONE





     A rather pretty small spring carriage, the kind bachelors drive in—retired lieutenant colonels, staff captains, landowners owning about a hundred souls of peasants—in short, all those called gentlemen of moderate means—drove through the gates of the provincial town NN’s inn. In the carriage sat a gentleman, not handsome, but not unattractive either, neither too fat nor too thin; one couldn’t say he was old, yet neither was he very young. His arrival caused absolutely no noise in town and was accompanied by nothing special; only two Russian peasants standing by the tavern door opposite the inn made some remarks, which, however, related more to the carriage than its occupant. “Look at that,” said one to the other, “what a wheel! What do you think, would that wheel make it to Moscow if something happened, or wouldn’t it?”—“It would make it,” answered the other. “And to Kazan, I guess it wouldn’t?”—“It wouldn’t make it to Kazan,” the other replied. That ended the conversation. Also, when the carriage approached the inn, a young man wearing white broadcloth pantaloons, very tight and short, a frock coat with attempts at fashion, under which a shirt-front was visible fastened with a Tula pin shaped like a bronze pistol, came by. The young man turned back, looked at the carriage, held his cap with his hand, almost blown off by the wind, and walked on.

     When the carriage entered the courtyard, the gentleman was met by the inn’s servant, or “polovoy,” as they are called in Russian inns—a lively and nimble person to such a degree that it was impossible even to make out what his face looked like. He darted out briskly, a napkin in hand—tall and in a long demi-cotton tailcoat with the back nearly at the nape—shook his hair, and promptly led the gentleman above along the wooden gallery to show him the rest God had granted him. The rest was of a known sort, for the inn was of a known kind, that is, exactly like inns found in provincial towns where, for two rubles a day, travelers get a resting room with cockroaches peering out like prunes from every corner, and a door to the next room, always blocked by a dresser, where a neighbor is set up—a silent and calm person but extremely curious, eager to know all the details about the traveler. The inn’s exterior matched its interior: it was very long, two stories; the lower floor was not plastered and remained in dark red bricks, even more darkened by nasty weather changes and already somewhat dirty; the upper was painted with everlasting yellow paint; below were benches with harnesses, ropes, and loops. In the coal stand of these benches, or rather in the window, stood a sbiten vendor with a red copper samovar and a face just as red as the samovar’s, so that from afar one might think there were two samovars on the window if one samovar weren’t adorned with a beard black as pitch.

     While the arriving gentleman inspected his room, his belongings were brought in: first of all, a white leather suitcase, somewhat worn, showing it had been on the road more than once. The suitcase was carried in by coachman Selifan, a short man in a sheepskin coat, and footman Petrushka, a lad about thirty years old, wearing a roomy worn frock coat, evidently from his master’s wardrobe, a somewhat stern-looking fellow with very thick lips and nose. After the suitcase, a small redwood chest with inlays of Karelian birch, cobbler’s lasts, and a fried chicken wrapped in blue paper were brought in. When all this was brought in, coachman Selifan went to the stable to tend to the horses, and footman Petrushka began settling into the small anteroom, a very dark doghouse where he had already managed to drag his overcoat and with it some personal scent, which was also imparted to a bag brought in afterward containing various footman toiletries. In this doghouse, he attached a narrow three-legged bed to the wall, covering it with a small kind of mattress, worn out and flat as a pancake.and maybe as greasy as the pancake he managed to demand from the innkeeper.

     Meanwhile, while the servants were managing and fussing, the gentleman went to the common room. Every traveler knows very well what these common rooms are like: the same walls, painted with oil paint, darkened at the top from pipe smoke and polished below by the backs of various travelers, and even more so by the local merchants, because merchants on trading days came here themselves to drink their usual pair of teas; the same soot-covered ceiling; the same smoked chandelier with many hanging glass pieces that jumped and tinkled every time the waiter ran over the worn oilcloths waving a tray on which sat as many tea cups as there are birds on the seashore; the same wall-sized paintings, painted with oil colors—in short, everything was just the same as everywhere else; only the difference was that one painting depicted a nymph with breasts so enormous that, surely, the reader has never seen the like. Such a play of nature, however, appears in various historical paintings, of unknown time, origin, and by whom brought to Russia, sometimes even by our nobles, art lovers, who bought them in Italy on the advice of their couriers. The gentleman took off his cap and untied a woolen, rainbow-colored kerchief from his neck, the kind a wife prepares with her own hands for married men, providing proper instructions on how to wrap it, and for bachelors—well, I can’t say who makes those, God knows, I’ve never worn such kerchiefs. Untying the kerchief, the gentleman ordered his meal.

     While he was being served various usual dishes in inns, such as cabbage soup with puff pastry deliberately saved for travelers over several weeks, brains with peas, sausages with cabbage, fried guinea fowl, salted cucumber, and the eternal puff pastry sweet pie always ready to serve; while all this was being served both hot and just cold, he made the servant, or waiter, tell all sorts of nonsense—about who used to run the inn, who runs it now, whether it brings much income, and whether their owner was a great scoundrel; to which the waiter usually replied, "Oh, a great one, sir, a swindler." Just like in enlightened Europe, there are now many respectable people in enlightened Russia who cannot eat in an inn without talking to a servant, and sometimes amusingly joking with them. However, the traveler did not ask just empty questions; he inquired with extraordinary accuracy who the governor of the city was, who the chairman of the chamber was, who the prosecutor was—in short, he missed no significant official; but with even greater precision, if not with interest, he asked about all the significant landlords: how many serfs each had, how far they lived from the city, what kind of character each had, and how often they came to town; he carefully asked about the state of the region: whether there were any diseases in their province—widespread fevers, any deadly fevers, smallpox, and the like, and all so thoroughly and with such accuracy, which showed more than simple curiosity.

     During his receptions, the gentleman had a certain dignity and sneezed extremely loudly. It’s unknown how he did it, but his nose sounded like a trumpet. This, in my opinion, completely innocent trait gained him a lot of respect from the inn’s servant, so that every time he heard this sound, he shook his hair, straightened up more respectfully, and, bowing his head from above, asked: was anything needed? After lunch, the gentleman drank a cup of coffee and sat on the sofa, putting a pillow behind his back that in Russian inns, instead of elastic wool, is stuffed with something very much like a brick and a cobblestone. Then he began to yawn and ordered to be taken to his room, where, lying down, he slept for two hours. Rested, he wrote on a scrap of paper, at the inn servant’s request, his rank, first and last name for notification to the proper authorities, the police. On the paper, the waiter, descending the stairs, read syllable by syllable the following:Collegiate Counselor Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov, landowner, according to his needs. When the orderly was still sorting the note by warehouses, Pavel Ivanovich Chichikov himself went to see the city, which he seemed satisfied with, as he found that the city was in no way inferior to other provincial cities: the yellow paint on the stone houses caught the eye sharply and the gray on the wooden ones modestly darkened. The houses were one, two, and one and a half stories high, with a permanent mezzanine, very beautiful, according to the provincial architects. In places, these houses seemed lost among the wide, field-like streets and endless wooden fences; in other places, they clustered together, and there was noticeably more movement of people and liveliness here. Almost rain-washed signs appeared with pretzels and boots, somewhere with painted blue trousers and the signature of some Arshavsky tailor; where a shop with caps, forage caps, and the inscription: "Foreigner Vasily Fedorov"; where a billiard was painted with two players in tailcoats, like those worn in our theaters by guests entering the stage in the last act. The players were depicted aiming with cues, their hands somewhat turned backward and their legs crooked, just having made an entrench in the air. Under all this was written: "And here the establishment." Somewhere simply on the street stood tables with nuts, soap, and gingerbread that looked like soap; where a tavern had a painted fat fish with a fork stuck in it. Most often, there were noticeably darkened double-headed state eagles, which were now replaced by a laconic inscription: "Drinking house." The pavement everywhere was rather poor. He also glanced into the city garden, which consisted of thin trees, poorly taken root, with supports below in the form of triangles, very beautifully painted with green oil paint. However, although these trees were no higher than reeds, the newspapers said about them in the description of the illumination that "our city was adorned, thanks to the care of the civil governor, with a garden consisting of shady, broad-branched trees giving coolness on a hot day," and that it was "very touching to see how the hearts of citizens trembled with abundant gratitude and shed streams of tears in sign of appreciation to the city governor." After inquiring in detail from the watchman where one could get closer, if necessary, to the cathedral, to the government offices, to the governor, he set off to look at the river flowing through the middle of the city, tearing off a poster nailed to a post on the way so that, having come home, he could read it carefully, looked intently at a woman of not bad appearance passing on the wooden sidewalk, followed by a boy in military livery with a bundle in his hand, and, once again scanning everything with his eyes, as if to remember the layout of the place well, he went straight home to his room, supported slightly on the stairs by the inn servant. Having eaten some tea, he sat down at the table, ordered a candle to be brought, took the poster out of his pocket, held it up to the candle, and began to read, slightly squinting his right eye. However, there was little remarkable in the poster: it announced a drama by Mr. Kotzebue, in which Rolla was played by Mr. Poplvin, Cora by Miss Zyablova, the other roles were even less remarkable; nevertheless, he read them all, even got to the price of the parterre and found out that the poster was printed at the provincial government printing house, then he flipped it over to see if there was anything on the other side, but finding nothing, rubbed his eyes, neatly folded it, and put it in his little chest, where he usually kept everything that came his way. The day, it seems, was concluded with a portion of cold veal, a bottle of sour cabbage soup, and a deep sleep in the whole pump casing, as they say in other parts of the vast Russian state.

The entire next day was devoted to visits; the newcomer went to pay visits to all the city dignitaries. He was respectfully with the governor, who, it turned out, like Chichikov, was neither fat nor thin, wore an Anna around his neck, and it was even said that he had been presented with a star; however, he was a great kind man and sometimes even embroidered on tulle himself. Then he went to the vice-governor,then he visited the prosecutor, the chairman of the chamber, the police chief, the tax farmer, the head of the state factories… it’s a pity that it’s somewhat difficult to recall all the powerful people of the world; but it’s enough to say that the newcomer was extraordinarily active in terms of visits: he even came to pay his respects to the inspector of the medical administration and the city architect. And then he sat in the carriage for a long time, thinking about whom else he might visit, but there were no more officials left in the city. In conversations with these rulers, he was very skillful at flattering each one. To the governor, he once hinted in passing that entering his province was like entering paradise, the roads were all velvety, and that those governments that appointed wise officials deserved great praise. To the police chief, he said something very flattering about the city watchmen; and in conversations with the vice-governor and the chairman of the chamber, who were still only state councillors, he even mistakenly said twice: “your excellency,” which they liked very much. The result of this was that the governor invited him to come to his home party later that same day, and the other officials also, on their part, invited him—some for lunch, some for a boston tea, some for a cup of tea.

About himself, the newcomer seemed to avoid speaking much; if he did speak, it was with generalities and noticeable modesty, and his conversation in such cases took somewhat literary turns: that he was an insignificant worm of this world and not worthy of much attention, that he had experienced a lot in his life, endured service in pursuit of the truth, had many enemies, some even attempting on his life, and that now, wishing to find peace, he sought to finally choose a place to live, and that having arrived in this city, he considered it his unwavering duty to pay his respects first to its officials. That was all the city knew about this new face, who very soon did not fail to show himself at the governor’s party. Preparations for this party took just over two hours, and here the newcomer showed such attention to his outfit as had not even been seen everywhere. After a short afternoon nap, he ordered to wash up and rubbed both cheeks with soap for an extraordinarily long time, supporting them with his tongue from the inside; then, taking a towel from the inn servant’s shoulder, he wiped his entire face on all sides, starting from behind his ears and snorting twice in the servant’s face beforehand. Then he put on a dickey in front of the mirror, plucked two hairs that had grown out of his nose, and immediately after that appeared in a cranberry-colored tailcoat with a sparkle. Dressed this way, he rolled in his own carriage along the endlessly wide streets, lit by a meager lighting from the occasionally flashing ocean. However, the governor’s house was so illuminated, whether for the ball or not; a carriage with lanterns, two gendarmes in front of the entrance, porter’s shouts in the distance—in short, everything as it should be. Upon entering the hall, Chichikov had to close his eyes for a moment because the shine from the candles, lamps, and ladies’ dresses was overwhelming. Everything was flooded with light. Black tailcoats flickered and hurried separately and in bunches here and there, like flies on white glistening refined sugar during a hot July summer when the old housekeeper chops and divides it into sparkling fragments before an open window; all the children watch, gathered around, curiously following the movements of her stiff hands raising the hammer, and airy squadrons of flies, raised by a light breeze, fly boldly as though they are the full masters, and, taking advantage of the old woman’s poor eyesight and the sun bothering her eyes, scatter over the tasty pieces, sometimes scattered, sometimes in thick bunches. Saturated with the rich summer, which already at every step places out delicious dishes, they flew in not at all to eat, but merely to show themselves, to walk back and forth on the sugar mound, rub their hind or front legs against each other, scratch themselves under their wings with them, or, stretching out both front paws, rub above their heads, turn around, and fly away again, and fly in again with new annoying squadrons. Chichikov barely managed to look around,As he had already been caught under the arm by the governor, who immediately introduced him to the governor’s wife. The visiting guest didn’t embarrass himself here either: he made some compliment, quite decent for a middle-aged person holding a rank neither too high nor too low. When the dancing couples settled and pressed everyone against the wall, he, hands behind his back, watched them very intently for about two minutes. Many of the ladies were well dressed and fashionable; others wore whatever God had sent for the provincial town. The men here, as everywhere else, were of two kinds: some slender ones who busied themselves around the ladies; some of them were such that it was difficult to distinguish them from those in Petersburg, having just as thoughtfully and tastefully styled sideburns or simply respectable, very smoothly shaved oval faces, just as casually sitting close to the ladies, just as speaking French, and amusing the ladies just like in Petersburg.

The other kind of men were the fat ones or the same as Chichikov, that is, not too fat but definitely not slender either. These, on the contrary, squinted and backed away from the ladies and only glanced around to see if the governor’s servant had set up the green table for whist somewhere. Their faces were full and round; some even had warts, some were blotchy, and they wore no hair on their heads in tufts, curls, or in the “devil take me” style, as the French say – their hair was either cut very short or slicked down, and their facial features were more rounded and strong. These were the honorable officials in the city. Alas! The fat ones know better how to handle their affairs in this world than the slender ones. The slender serve more on special assignments or just hold positions and drift here and there; their existence is somehow too light, airy, and completely unreliable. The fat ones never take indirect positions, only direct ones, and once they sit somewhere, they sit reliably and firmly, so that sooner the place would crack and bend under them than they would fly off.

They do not like outward show; their frock coats are not as neatly tailored as those of the slender ones, but their coffers are a blessing from God. The slender one at three years old has not a single soul left but what is pawned; the fat one calmly owns a house somewhere on the outskirts of the city, bought in his wife’s name, then another house at the other end, then a village near the city, then a countryside estate with all its lands. Finally, the fat one, having served God and the state, having earned universal respect, leaves the service, moves and becomes a landowner, a glorious Russian gentleman, a generous host, and lives well, and lives well indeed. And after him, once again the slender heirs squander all the father’s estate on couriers, as is the Russian custom.

It cannot be denied that almost such thoughts occupied Chichikov at that time when he was observing the company, and the result was that he finally joined the fat ones, where he met almost all familiar faces: the prosecutor with very dark thick eyebrows and a somewhat winking left eye, as if saying: “Come on, brother, to another room, I'll tell you something there” – a man, however, serious and silent; the postmaster, a short man but a wit and philosopher; the chairman of the chamber, a very sensible and amiable person – all of whom greeted him as an old acquaintance, to which Chichikov bowed slightly to the side, however not without pleasure.

Right there he also made the acquaintance of the very courteous and polite landowner Manilov and the somewhat clumsy at first glance Sobakevich, who stepped on his foot from the very start, saying: “I beg your pardon.” Right then they slipped him a card for whist, which he accepted with the same polite bow. They sat down at the green table and did not get up until dinner. All conversation completely ceased, as always happens when finally given to serious business. Although the postmaster was very talkative, even he, taking the cards in hand, immediately showed a thoughtful expression on his face, covering his upper lip with his lower and kept this position throughout the game. Playing a trick, he would hit the table firmly with his hand, saying, if it was a queen: “She’s gone,”old hag!", but if it's the king: "Go away, Tambov man!"
CHAPTER TWO





     For more than a week, the visiting gentleman had been living in the city, going around to parties and lunches and thus spending, as they say, a very pleasant time. Finally, he decided to move his visits out of town and pay a call on the landowners Manilov and Sobakevich, to whom he had given his word. Perhaps another, more significant reason urged him to do this, a more serious matter, closer to his heart… But the reader will gradually learn about all this in due time, if only they have the patience to read the story offered, a very long one, which will unfold more broadly and spaciously as it approaches the end, crowning the whole thing. The coachman Selifan was given orders to hitch up the horses early in the morning to the well-known carriage; Petrushka was ordered to stay at home, watch over the room and the trunk. For the reader, it will not be superfluous to get acquainted with these two serfs of our hero. Although, of course, they are not such noticeable figures, what one calls secondary or even tertiary, though the main moves and springs of the poem are not established on them and perhaps only touch and lightly catch them here and there – but the author loves to be extremely detailed in everything, and from this side, despite being Russian himself, wants to be precise like a German. This will take, however, little time and space because not much needs to be added to what the reader already knows, namely that Petrushka wore a somewhat loose brown frock coat handed down from his master and had, as is customary for people of his rank, a large nose and lips. His character was more silent than talkative; he even had a noble inclination toward enlightenment, that is, reading books, the content of which did not trouble him: it was completely indifferent to him whether it was the adventures of a lovesick hero, a simple primer, or a prayer book – he read everything with equal attention; if he had come across a book on chemistry, he would not have refused it either. What he liked was not so much what he read, but rather the reading itself, or better said, the process of reading itself, that from letters some kind of word always emerges, which sometimes God knows what it means. This reading took place mostly while lying down in the anteroom, on the bed and on the mattress, which had become worn out and thin as a result of this circumstance, like a flatbread. Besides the passion for reading, he had two other habits, which constituted two other characteristic features of his nature: to sleep without undressing, as he was, wearing the same frock coat, and always carry with him a certain special air, his own particular scent, which recalled somewhat a lived-in tranquility, so that it was enough for him just to set up his bed somewhere, even in a hitherto uninhabited room, drag over his greatcoat and belongings, and it already seemed as if people had lived in that room for ten years. Chichikov, being a very sensitive person and even in some cases a picky one, drawing fresh air to his nose in the morning, only frowned and shook his head, saying: “You, brother, who knows, are you sweating or what? You should at least go to the bathhouse.” To which Petrushka said nothing and immediately tried to busy himself with some task; he either approached the master’s frock coat hanging with a whip or simply tidied something up. What he thought at that time when he was silent – maybe he said to himself: “And you, however, good as you are, aren’t you tired of repeating the same thing forty times” – God knows, it is hard to tell what a household serf thinks at the time when the master is giving him instructions. So, that is what can be said about Petrushka for now. The coachman Selifan was a completely different person… But the author is very ashamed to occupy the readers so long with low-class people, knowing from experience how reluctantly they get acquainted with the lower estates. Such is the Russian person: a strong passion to boast to someone who would be at least one rank above them, and a superficial acquaintance with a count or prince is better for them than any close friendly relations. The author even fears for his hero, who is only a collegiate counselor. Court councilors, perhaps, will get acquainted with him, but those who have already reached the general ranks, those, God knows, perhaps, will...even cast one of those contemptuous glances that a proud person throws at everything that crawls at their feet, or, worse yet, perhaps pass by with a deadly indifference to the author. But as regrettable as both are, all the same, we must return to the hero. So, having given the necessary orders the evening before, waking up very early in the morning, having washed, wiped from head to toe with a wet sponge—which was done only on Sundays—and if that day happened to be a Sunday—having shaved in such a way that the cheeks became a true satin in the matter of smoothness and shine, having put on a cranberry-colored tailcoat with a sparkle and then a greatcoat with large bears, he descended the stairs, supported under the arm first on one side, then the other, by the inn’s servant, and got into the carriage. The carriage thundered out from under the hotel gates onto the street. A passing priest took off his hat, several boys in dirty shirts held out their hands, saying, “Master, please give to the orphans!” The coachman, noticing that one of them was a great enthusiast of standing on the backs of horses, cracked his whip at him, and the carriage began to bounce over the cobblestones. Not without joy was the distant striped barrier seen, signaling that the paved road, like any other torment, would soon come to an end; and after several more fairly hard bumps against the carriage body, Chichikov finally rushed onto soft earth.

No sooner had the city receded behind than nonsense and wild growth began to be written on both sides of the road following our custom: hummocks, spruce groves, low, sparse young pine bushes, burnt trunks of old ones, wild heather, and similar nonsense. There were elongated, string-like villages, built like old stacked firewood, covered with gray roofs with carved wooden decorations underneath resembling embroidered hanging towels. Several men, as usual, yawned while sitting on benches in front of gates wearing their sheepskin coats. Women with thick faces and bandaged chests looked out from the upper windows; from the lower windows looked a calf or a blind-faced pig stuck out its snout. In short, familiar scenes. Having covered the fifteenth verst, he remembered that, according to Manilov, his village should be here, but the sixteenth verst also flew by without any village in sight, and if it weren’t for two men encountered on the way back, they probably would not have had the pleasure of meeting each other. When asked how far the village of Zamanilovka was, the men took off their hats, and one of them, who was smarter and wore a pointed beard, answered:

     – Manilovka, maybe, not Zamanilovka?

     – Well, yeah, Manilovka.

     – Manilovka! And if you go another verst, then here you are, that is, straight to the right.

     – To the right? – the coachman replied.

     – To the right, – said the man. – That will be the road to Manilovka; there’s no such thing as Zamanilovka. It’s called that, that is, its nickname is Manilovka, but there’s no Zamanilovka here at all. There, right on the hill, you’ll see a house, stone, two stories, the landlord’s house, in which, that is, the master himself lives. That’s Manilovka for you, and there’s absolutely no Zamanilovka here and never was.

They set off to find Manilovka. After two versts, they came upon a turn onto a country road, but already two, three, and four versts seemed to have passed, and still no two-story stone house was seen. Here Chichikov recalled that if a friend invites you to his village fifteen versts away, it means that there are actually thirty versts to it. Manilovka could attract few with its location. The landlord’s house stood alone on a yura—that is, an elevation exposed to all winds that might blow; the slope of the hill on which it stood was covered with closely clipped turf. On it were scattered two or three flower beds with lilac and yellow acacia bushes, arranged in English style; five or six birches, in small groups, here and there raised their small-leaved sparse tops. Beneath two of them was visible a gazebo with a flat green dome, blue wooden columns, and the inscription: “Temple of Solitary Reflection”; lower down, a pond covered with greenery, which, however, was not uncommon in the English-style gardens of Russian landowners. At the base of this elevation, partly along the very slope,The gray log huts, darkened lengthwise and crosswise, which our hero, for unknown reasons, immediately began to count and counted more than two hundred; nowhere among them was a growing tree or any greenery; everywhere there was only one log visible. The scene was enlivened by two women, who, artistically gathering their dresses and nudging each other from all sides, waded knee-deep in the pond, dragging behind two wooden mares a torn fish trap, where two entangled crayfish were visible and a caught roach gleamed; the women seemed to be quarrelling with each other and exchanging arguments about something. Nearby, the pine forest darkened with a dull bluish color. Even the weather served quite aptly: the day was neither clear nor gloomy, but of some light gray color, the kind only found on the old uniforms of garrison soldiers, that peaceful army, though partially intoxicated on Sundays. To complete the picture, there was no lack of a rooster, the harbinger of changeable weather, which, despite its head being pecked hollow to the brain by the beaks of other roosters due to known delays, crowed very loudly and even flapped its wings, stripped like old mats. Approaching the yard, Chichikov noticed the owner himself on the porch, standing in a green shawl coat, holding his hand to his forehead like an umbrella over his eyes to better see the approaching carriage. As the carriage neared the porch, his eyes became merrier, and his smile widened more and more.

     – Pavel Ivanovich! – he finally exclaimed as Chichikov climbed out of the carriage. – You hardly remembered us after all!

     Both friends kissed each other warmly, and Manilov led his guest into the room. Although the time during which they would pass through the entryway, the front room, and the dining room was somewhat short, let us try to make use of it somehow and say a few words about the homeowner. But here the author must confess that such an undertaking is very difficult. It is much easier to depict large-character personalities: just throw paint with your whole hand onto the canvas, black piercing eyes, overhanging brows, a forehead cut by wrinkles, a black or scarlet cloak thrown over the shoulder—and the portrait is ready; but these gentlemen, who are many in the world, who look very similar to each other, yet when you look closely, you see many of the most elusive peculiarities—these gentlemen are terribly difficult subjects for portraits. Here you will have to concentrate intensely until you make all the subtle, almost invisible traits emerge before you, and in general, you will have to delve deeply with an already refined eye accustomed to careful scrutiny.

     Only God perhaps could say what Manilov’s character was like. There is a kind of people known by the phrase: people neither here nor there, neither in the town of Bogdan nor the village of Selifan, according to the proverb. Perhaps Manilov should be counted among them. At first glance, he was a noticeable person; his facial features were not without pleasantness, but it seemed this pleasantness was excessively sugared; in his manners and expressions, there was something ingratiating, seeking favor and acquaintance. He smiled enticingly, was fair-haired, with blue eyes. In the first minute of talking to him, you cannot help but say: “What a pleasant and kind person!” In the next minute, you say nothing, and in the third, you say: “Who knows what that is!”—and step away; if you do not, you will feel deadly boredom. From him, you will not hear any lively or even haughty word, like you can from almost anyone if you touch on the subject that irritates them. Everyone has their own zest: one’s passion is turned toward greyhounds; another thinks himself a great lover of music and extraordinarily feels all its deep parts; the third is a master of a flashy dinner; the fourth plays a role at least a height above the one assigned to him; the fifth, with a more limited desire, sleeps and dreams of going to a walk with the aide-de-camp, showing off to friends, acquaintances, and even strangers; the sixth is already gifted with such a hand,who feels the supernatural desire to corner some ace or two of diamonds, while the seven’s hand is itching to restore order somewhere, to get closer to the personality of the stationmaster or the coachmen—in short, everyone has their own thing, but Manilov had nothing. At home, he spoke very little and mostly pondered and thought, but what he thought about, perhaps only God knew.

He couldn’t be said to manage the household; he never even went to the fields, and the household somehow ran on its own. When the steward said, "It would be good, sir, to do this or that," he usually replied, smoking his pipe, which he had gotten used to smoking back when he served in the army, where he was considered the most modest, delicate, and educated officer. "Yes, quite good," he would repeat.

When a peasant came to him, scratching the back of his head, and said, "Sir, allow me to go to work, to earn some money," he said, "Go ahead," smoking his pipe, and it never even occurred to him that the peasant was going to drink.

Sometimes, looking from the porch at the yard and the pond, he would talk about how nice it would be if suddenly an underground passage were built from the house or a stone bridge over the pond, with benches on both sides where merchants could sit and sell various small goods needed by the peasants.      Our hero was quite frightened, indeed. Although the carriage was rushing at full speed and Nozdrev’s village had long disappeared from sight, hidden behind fields, slopes, and hills, he still looked back fearfully, almost expecting a chase to come any moment. Many heavy and intense desires were promised to Nozdrev; some unpleasant words slipped out too. What could be done? A Russian person, especially in such a state of mind. Besides, this was no trivial matter. "Whatever you say," he told himself, "if the captain-police officer hadn’t arrived, I might never have seen the light of day again! I would have disappeared like a bubble on the water, leaving no trace, no descendants, no fortune, nor an honest name to future generations!" Our hero cared a great deal about his descendants.

     "What a nasty master!" Selifan thought to himself. "I haven’t seen such a master yet. I’d spit on him for that! It’s better not to give a person food than not feed the horse because the horse loves oats. That’s his sustenance: what is food to us is oats to him."

     The horses seemed to think poorly of Nozdrev as well: not only the bay one and Zasedatel but even the dappled one was out of sorts. Although he always got the better oats, and Selifan must have poured the oats into his trough while muttering, "You scoundrel!" still, it was oats, not just hay, and he chewed it with pleasure, often dipping his long muzzle into the troughs of his comrades to sample their feed, especially when Selifan wasn’t in the stable. But now there was only hay… not good; everyone was unhappy.

     But soon all the disgruntled ones were interrupted amidst their complaints in a sudden and completely unexpected way. Everyone, including the coachman himself, came to their senses only when a carriage with a team of six horses galloped up to them, and almost over their heads shouted the voices of the ladies sitting in the carriage, the swearing and threats of a foreign coachman: "You cheat like no other; I shouted at you aloud: turn right, crow! Are you drunk or what?" Selifan realized his mistake, but since a Russian person dislikes admitting fault before others, he immediately replied, straightening up: "Why are you shouting? Did you leave your eyes in the tavern or what?" After that, he began backing the carriage up to free himself from the hitching with the strangers, but no such luck, everything got tangled. The dappled horse curiously sniffed his new companions who had appeared on either side of him. Meanwhile, the ladies sitting in the carriage watched all this with fear on their faces. One was an old woman, the other a young one, sixteen years old, with golden hair very neatly and sweetly smoothed on a small head. Her pretty oval face was rounded like a fresh egg and, like it, gleamed with a kind of translucent whiteness, just like a freshly laid egg held against the light by the dark hands of a kitchen maid testing it, letting the sunshine’s rays pass through. Her delicate ears glowed too, blushing from the warm light passing through them. Along with the fright, her lips were parted, her eyes were teary—all of this was so lovely that our hero looked at her for several minutes, paying no attention to the commotion happening between the horses and coachmen. "Back up, will you, Nizhny Novgorod crow!" shouted the foreign coachman. Selifan pulled the reins back, the foreign coachman did the same, the horses stepped back a few paces, then ran into each other again, stepping over the traces. During this, the dappled horse enjoyed the new acquaintance so much that he refused to leave the path he found himself in due to unforeseen circumstances and, resting his muzzle on the neck of his new friend, seemed to whisper something in his ear, probably frightening nonsense, because the newcomer kept shaking his ears incessantly.
