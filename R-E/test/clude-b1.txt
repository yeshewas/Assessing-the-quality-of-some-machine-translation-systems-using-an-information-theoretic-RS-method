
CHAPTER THREE

Chichikov sat in his carriage in a satisfied mood, which rolled along the main road for some time. From the previous chapter it is already evident what his main interests and inclinations were, so it is no wonder that he soon became absorbed in them, both body and soul. The suppositions, calculations, and considerations that played across his face were evidently very pleasant, for every moment they left traces of a satisfied smile. Occupied with them, he paid no attention to how his coachman, pleased with the reception given by Manilov's household servants, was making very sensible remarks to the dappled trace horse harnessed on the right side. This dappled horse was extremely cunning and was only pretending to pull, while the chestnut shaft horse and the bay trace horse, called Assessor because he had been purchased from some assessor, labored with all their hearts, so much so that pleasure could be seen in their eyes. "Be clever, be clever! I'll outwit you yet!" said Selifan, rising in his seat and striking the lazy one with his whip. "You know your business, you German in trousers! The chestnut is a respectable horse; he does his duty. I'll gladly give him an extra measure because he's respectable, and Assessor too is a good horse... Well, well! What are you shaking your ears for? You fool, listen when I speak! I won't teach you anything bad, you ignorant creature. Look how he crawls!" Here he struck him with his whip again, saying: "You scoundrel! You damned Bonaparte!" Then he shouted to all three: "Hey, my good fellows!" and struck all three, now not as punishment but to show he was pleased with them. Having given them this pleasure, he turned his speech again to the dappled horse: "You think you'll hide your conduct. No, you live honestly if you want to be shown respect. Look at the landowner we were just at—good people. I'm glad to talk when there's a good man; with a good man we're always friends, best friends; whether it's tea or a bite—gladly, if he's a good man. Every man shows respect to a good man. Look at our master—everyone respects him because, you hear, he served the government, he's a collegiate councillor..."

Thus reasoning, Selifan finally wandered into the most remote abstractions. Had Chichikov listened, he would have learned many details relating personally to him; but his thoughts were so occupied with his subject that only a loud clap of thunder made him wake up and look around; the entire sky was covered with clouds, and the dusty post road was sprinkled with drops of rain. Finally another clap of thunder sounded louder and nearer, and rain suddenly poured down as if from a bucket. At first, taking an oblique direction, it lashed at one side of the carriage body, then at the other, then, changing its method of attack and becoming completely vertical, drummed directly on top of its roof; splashes finally began to reach his face. This forced him to draw the leather curtains with two round little windows for viewing the road views, and order Selifan to drive faster. Selifan, interrupted also in the middle of his speech, understood that truly there was no need to delay, immediately pulled from under the seat some trash of gray cloth, put his arms in it, grabbed the reins, and shouted to his trio, which was barely moving its feet, having felt a pleasant relaxation from the instructive speeches. But Selifan could not recall whether he had passed two or three turns. Thinking and trying to remember the road somewhat, he guessed that there had been many turns, which he had all passed by. As a Russian coachman finds something to do in decisive moments without engaging in lengthy reasoning, he turned to the right at the first crossroads, shouted: "Hey, you good fellows!" and galloped off, thinking little about where the road he had taken would lead.

The rain, however, seemed to have settled in for a long time. The dust that lay on the road quickly turned to mud, and the horses found it harder and harder to pull the carriage every moment. Chichikov began to grow anxious, not having seen Sobakevich's village for so long. By his calculation, they should have arrived long ago. He peered about, but the darkness was such that you could not see your hand before your face.

"Selifan!" he said finally, leaning out of the carriage.

"What, sir?" answered Selifan.

"Look, don't you see a village anywhere?"

"No, sir, nowhere to be seen!" After which Selifan, waving his whip, began a song—not really a song, but something long and endless. Everything went into it: all the encouraging and urging cries with which horses are treated throughout all of Russia from one end to the other; adjectives of all genders without any further discrimination, just whatever came first to his tongue. So it came to pass that he finally began calling them secretaries.

Meanwhile, Chichikov began to notice that the carriage was swaying on all sides and giving him some very hard jolts; this made him feel that they had left the road and were probably dragging themselves across a plowed field. Selifan seemed to sense this himself, but said nothing.

"What road are you traveling on, you scoundrel?" said Chichikov.

"Well, sir, what's to be done, the weather's like this; you can't see the whip, it's so dark!" Having said this, he tilted the carriage so much that Chichikov was forced to hold on with both hands. Then he noticed that Selifan was tipsy.

"Hold, hold, you'll overturn us!" he cried to him.

"No, sir, how could I overturn us," said Selifan. "That would be bad, to overturn. I know better than that; I certainly won't overturn." Then he began to turn the carriage slightly, turned and turned, and finally turned it completely on its side. Chichikov and legs went splash into the mud. Selifan stopped the horses, though they would have stopped themselves anyway, as they were greatly exhausted. This unexpected occurrence completely amazed him. Getting down from the seat, he stood before the carriage, put both hands on his hips, while his master was struggling in the mud trying to get out, and said after some thought: "See how it turned over!"

"You're drunk as a shoemaker!" said Chichikov.

"No, sir, how could I be drunk! I know that's a bad thing. I just had a talk with a fellow because you can talk with a good man, there's nothing wrong in that; and we had a bite together. A bite is nothing to be ashamed of; you can have a bite with a good man."

"And what did I tell you the last time you got drunk? eh? Did you forget?" said Chichikov.

"No, your honor, how could I forget! I know my business. I know that it's bad to get drunk. I just had a talk with a good man, because..."

"I'll beat you so that you'll know how to talk with a good man!"

"As you wish, sir," answered the always agreeable Selifan, "if you beat, then beat; I don't object to it at all. Why shouldn't you beat? It's necessary to beat because the peasant is acting up; order must be maintained. If it's for a good reason, then beat; why shouldn't you?"

To such reasoning the master found absolutely nothing to answer. But at this moment it seemed fate itself decided to take pity on him. From a distance came the sound of barking dogs. Delighted, Chichikov gave orders to drive the horses on. A Russian coachman has good intuition instead of eyes; that is why he sometimes, closing his eyes, gallops at full speed and always arrives somewhere. Selifan, seeing nothing, directed the horses so straight to a village that he stopped only when the carriage struck a fence with its shafts and there was clearly nowhere else to go. Chichikov only noticed through the thick veil of falling rain something resembling a roof. He sent Selifan to look for a gate, which undoubtedly would have taken a long time if Russia had not had instead of doormen fierce dogs who announced him so loudly that he brought his fingers to his ears. Light flickered in one window and reached in a misty beam to the fence, indicating our road-weary travelers where the gate was. Selifan began knocking, and soon, after opening a little gate, some figure appeared, covered with a military coat, and master and servant heard a hoarse woman's voice:

"Who's knocking? Why are you wandering about?"

"Travelers, mother, let us stay the night," said Chichikov.

"Look at you, quick-footed one," said the old woman, "arriving at such a time! This is not an inn here; a landowner's lady lives here."

"What's to be done, mother: you see we've lost our way. We can't spend such a time in the steppe."

"Yes, it's a dark time, a bad time," added Selifan.

"Shut up, fool," said Chichikov.

"But who are you?" said the old woman.

"A nobleman, mother."

The word "nobleman" seemed to make the old woman think for a moment.

"Wait, I'll tell the mistress," she said, and a couple of minutes later she returned with a lantern in her hand.

The gate opened. A light flickered in another window too. The carriage, having driven into the courtyard, stopped before a small house which was difficult to make out in the darkness. Only one half of it was illuminated by light coming from the windows; a puddle before the house was also visible, which the same light struck directly. Rain drummed loudly on the wooden roof and flowed in gurgling streams into a barrel that had been placed to catch it. Meanwhile the dogs barked in all possible voices: one, having thrown its head back, drew out such a prolonged note and with such effort, as though it were receiving God knows what kind of payment; another snapped quickly, like a sexton; between them rang, like a post bell, the tireless soprano, probably of a young puppy, and all this was finally crowned by a bass, perhaps of an old dog, blessed with stout canine nature, because it rasped like a singing contrabass when a concert is in full sway: tenors rise on tiptoe from the strong desire to produce a high note, and everything rushes upward, throwing back its head, but it alone, having tucked its unshaven chin into its collar, crouching and bending almost to the ground, issues its note from there, which makes the glasses shake and rattle. From the very barking alone, composed of such musicians, one could suppose that the little village was quite respectable; but our soaked and chilled hero thought of nothing but a bed. Before the carriage had completely stopped, he had already jumped onto the porch, staggered, and nearly fell. Another woman came out onto the porch, younger than the first but very much resembling her. She showed him into a room. Chichikov cast two hasty glances: the room was hung with old striped wallpaper; pictures of some birds; between the windows old small mirrors with dark frames in the form of curled leaves; behind each mirror were placed either a letter, or an old deck of cards, or a stocking; wall clocks with drawn flowers on the face... it was impossible to notice anything more. He felt his eyes sticking together as if someone had covered them with honey. A moment later the mistress entered, a woman of middle age, in some hasty nightcap, with flannel around her neck—one of those small landowners who complain about poor harvests and losses and hold their heads somewhat to the side, and meanwhile gradually accumulate money in striped cloth bags, distributed among the drawers of a chest. In one bag they put all the whole rubles, in another the half-rubles, in the third the quarters, although to the eye it seems as though the chest contains nothing but linen, night jackets, thread spools, and a torn cloak intended to be turned into a dress if the old one somehow burns during the baking of holiday pies with all sorts of pastries, or wears out by itself. But it won't burn and it won't wear out by itself: the thrifty old woman is careful, and the cloak is destined to lie a long time in its torn state, and then pass by will to a niece of a grand-niece together with all other junk.

Chichikov apologized for troubling her with his unexpected arrival.

"Nothing, nothing," said the mistress. "At what a time God has brought you! Such a turmoil and blizzard... You should have eaten something on the road, but it's nighttime, we can't prepare anything."

The mistress's words were interrupted by a strange hissing, so that the guest was frightened; the sound resembled the whole room being filled with snakes; but glancing up, he calmed down, for he understood that the wall clock had taken it into its head to strike. After the hissing came wheezing, and finally, straining all its strength, it struck two with a sound as though someone were hitting a broken pot with a stick, after which the pendulum went again calmly clicking back and forth.

Chichikov thanked the mistress, saying that he needed nothing, that she should not trouble herself with anything, that besides a bed he required nothing, and was merely curious to know in what places he had arrived and how far it was from here to the landowner Sobakevich, to which the old woman said that she had never heard of such a name and that there was no such landowner at all.

"At least do you know Manilov?" said Chichikov.

"And who is Manilov?"

"A landowner, mother."

"No, I haven't heard of him, there's no such landowner."

"What landowners are there then?"

"Bobrov, Svinyin, Kanapataev, Harpakin, Trepakin, Pleshakov."

"Are they rich people or not?"

"No, father, there are no really rich ones. One has twenty souls, another thirty, but there are none with a hundred."

Chichikov noted that he had gotten into quite a remote backwater.

"How far is it at least to the town?"

"About sixty versts, I'd say. How sorry I am that there's nothing for you to eat! Would you like, my dear, to drink some tea?"

"Thank you, mother. I need nothing but a bed."

"True, after such a journey you certainly need to rest. Here, lie down, my dear, on this sofa. Hey, Fetunya, bring a feather bed, pillows, and a sheet. What weather God has sent: such thunder—I had a candle burning before the icon all night. Oh, my father, you're all over mud, front and back, like a boar! Where did you get so dirty?"

"Thank God I only got dirty; I should be grateful I didn't break my ribs completely."

"Holy saints, what a plight! Don't you need something to wipe your back with?"

"Thank you, thank you. Don't trouble yourself, just order your girl to dry and clean my clothes."

"Did you hear, Fetunya!" said the mistress, turning to the woman who came out onto the porch with a candle and had already managed to drag a feather bed and, fluffing it on both sides with her hands, sent a whole stream of feathers throughout the room. "Take their coat along with the underthings and first dry them before the fire, as you did for the late master, and then rub and beat them well."

"I hear you, madam!" said Fetunya, spreading a sheet over the feather bed and placing pillows.

"Well, here's your bed ready," said the mistress. "Goodbye, my dear, I wish you a good night. Do you need anything else? Are you accustomed, father, to having someone scratch your heels at night? My late husband couldn't fall asleep without it."

But the guest also refused the scratching of heels. The mistress left, and he at once hastened to undress, giving Fetunya all the things he had removed, both upper and lower garments, and Fetunya, wishing him also a good night on her part, carried away these wet garments. Left alone, he looked with pleasure at his bed, which was almost up to the ceiling. Fetunya, it seemed, was a master at fluffing feather beds. When, having placed a chair, he climbed onto the bed, it sank under him almost to the floor, and the feathers, displaced by him from their bounds, scattered to all corners of the room. Having blown out the candle, he covered himself with a calico quilt and, curling up under it like a pretzel, fell asleep that very minute. He woke the next day quite late in the morning. The sun blazed directly into his eyes through the window, and the flies, which yesterday had slept peacefully on the walls and ceiling, all turned to him: one settled on his lip, another on his ear, a third was trying to settle right on his eye, but the one that had the carelessness to settle near his nostril, he, half asleep, pulled right into his nose, which caused him to sneeze sharply—a circumstance that was the cause of his waking. Glancing around the room, he now noticed that not all the pictures were birds: among them hung a portrait of Kutuzov and painted with oils some old man with red cuffs on his uniform, as they were sewn on during Paul's reign. The clock again issued a hiss and struck ten; a woman's face peeped in at the door and in that same moment hid, because Chichikov, wishing to sleep better, had thrown off everything. The face that peeped seemed to him somewhat familiar. He tried to recall: who could it be, and finally remembered that it was the mistress. He put on his shirt; the clothes, already dried and cleaned, lay beside him. Having dressed, he approached the mirror and sneezed again so loudly that the Indian rooster that came up to the window at that moment—the window being very close to the ground—suddenly chattered something to him and very quickly in his strange language, probably "I wish you good health," to which Chichikov told him he was a fool. Coming up to the window, he began to examine the views before him: the window looked almost directly into the chicken coop; at least, the narrow courtyard before him was completely filled with birds and all kinds of domestic animals. There was no end of turkeys and chickens; among them strode a rooster with measured steps, shaking his comb and turning his head to the side as though listening to something; a sow with her family happened to be there too; there too, raking through a pile of garbage, she ate a chick in passing, and, not noticing this, continued to devour watermelon rinds as was her custom. This small courtyard, or chicken coop, was separated by a wooden fence, beyond which stretched spacious gardens with cabbage, onions, potatoes, bright vegetables and other vegetables. Various apple trees and other fruit trees were scattered about the garden, covered with nets for protection from magpies and sparrows, the latter of which moved in whole slanted clouds from one place to another. For this same reason several scarecrows had been set up on long poles with arms outstretched; one of them was wearing the mistress's own bonnet. Beyond the gardens came peasant huts, which although built in scattered fashion and not enclosed in regular streets, showed, by Chichikov's observation, the contentment of the inhabitants, as they were maintained properly: the weathered shingles on the roofs everywhere had been replaced with new ones; gates nowhere sagged, and in the peasant sheds facing him he noticed here a spare almost new cart, and there two carts. "So she has quite a sizable village," he said to himself and immediately decided to become better acquainted with the mistress. He peeped through a crack in the door from which she had thrust her head, and, seeing her sitting at a tea table, entered with a cheerful and affable air.

"Good morning, father. How did you sleep?" said the mistress, rising from her seat. She was dressed better than yesterday—in a dark dress and no longer in a nightcap, but something was still tied around her neck.

"Well, well," said Chichikov, sitting in an armchair. "And you, mother?"

"Badly, father."

"How so?"

"Insomnia. My loins ache, and my leg, just above the ankle, aches so."

"It will pass, it will pass, mother. That's nothing to worry about."

"God grant it passes. I smeared it with pork fat and even soaked it with turpentine. And what will you have with your tea? I have some fruit liqueur in a bottle."

"Not bad, mother, I'll have some fruit liqueur."

The reader, I think, has already noticed that Chichikov, despite his affable air, nevertheless spoke with much greater freedom than with Manilov, and was not at all ceremonious. It must be said that if we Russians have not yet caught up with foreigners in many things, we have far surpassed them in the art of social behavior. It is impossible to count all the shades and subtleties of our manner. A Frenchman or a German would not figure out or understand all its peculiarities and differences in a lifetime; he speaks in almost the same voice and the same language to both a millionaire and a petty tobacco trader, though of course in his soul he grovels appropriately before the first. With us it's different: we have such wise men who will speak one way to a landowner with two hundred souls, a completely different way to one with three hundred, and to him with three hundred they speak differently than to one with five hundred, and to him with five hundred differently than to one with eight hundred—in short, climb even to a million and everyone will find gradations. Suppose, for example, there exists a chancellery, not here, but in some thrice-nine kingdom, and in this chancellery there exists a chancellor. Look at him when he sits among his subordinates—you simply cannot say a word for fear! pride and nobility, and what else does not his face express? simply take a brush and paint: Prometheus, a real Prometheus! He surveys like an eagle, walks smoothly and measured. That same eagle, as soon as he leaves the room and approaches his superior's office, scurries with papers under his arm like a partridge, so one can hardly see him. In society and at a party, if everyone is of low rank, Prometheus remains Prometheus, but if anyone is even slightly above him, Prometheus undergoes such a transformation as Ovid himself could not invent: a fly, even smaller than a fly, vanished into a speck of dust! "That's not Ivan Petrovich," you say, looking at him. "Ivan Petrovich is taller, and this one is short and thin; that one speaks loudly, with a bass voice, and never laughs, but this devil knows what: he chirps like a bird and always laughs." You approach closer, look—it really is Ivan Petrovich! "Eh-heh," you think to yourself... But, however, let us return to our characters. Chichikov, as we have already seen, had decided not to be ceremonious at all, and so, having taken a cup of tea and poured some fruit liqueur into it, he spoke as follows:

"You have a nice village, mother. How many souls are in it?"

"Souls, father, there are about eighty without exaggeration," said the mistress, "but misfortune, times are bad, and last year there was such a harvest failure that God forbid."

"Nevertheless, the peasants look sturdy, the little houses are solid. But permit me to learn your family name. I was so scattered arriving at nighttime..."

"Korobochka, collegiate secretary."

"I am most grateful. And your name and patronymic?"

"Nastasya Petrovna."

"Nastasya Petrovna? A good name Nastasya Petrovna. I have an aunt, my mother's sister, also named Nastasya Petrovna."

"And what is your name?" asked the landowner. "Are you, I suppose, an assessor?"

"No, mother," answered Chichikov, smiling, "not an assessor, but I travel about my own business."

"Ah, so you're a buyer! It's such a shame, really, that I sold honey to the merchants so cheaply, but you, father, surely would have bought it from me."

"But I wouldn't have bought honey."

"What else then? Perhaps hemp? I have very little hemp now; only half a pud altogether."

"No, mother, merchandise of another kind: tell me, have your peasants been dying?"

"Oh, father, eighteen people!" said the old woman, sighing. "And such good people died, all workers. After that, true, some were born, but what are they: all small fry; and the assessor came by—pay, he says, the tax for each soul. Dead people, but pay as for the living. Last week a blacksmith burned to death with me, such a skilled blacksmith and he knew tinsmith work."

"Did you have a fire, mother?"

"God protected us from such a disaster, fire would be even worse; he burned himself, father. Something caught fire inside him, he drank too much, just a blue flame came from him, he was all consumed, consumed and turned black like coal, and such a skilled blacksmith he was! and now I have no way to go anywhere: there's no one to shoe the horses."

"God's will, mother!" said Chichikov, sighing, "against God's wisdom nothing can be said... Would you cede them to me, Nastasya Petrovna?"

"Whom, father?"

"Why, all those who have died."

"But how can I cede them?"

"Well, simply. Or perhaps sell them. I will give you money for them."

"But how? Really, I don't understand. Do you want to dig them up from the ground?"

Chichikov saw that the old woman had gone too far and that it was necessary to explain to her what it was all about. In a few words, he explained to her that the transfer or purchase would be noted only on paper and the souls would be registered as if alive.

"But why do you need them?" said the old woman, staring at him.

"That's my business."

"But they're dead."

"Yes, who said they're alive? That's why it's a loss for you, that they're dead: you pay for them, and now I will relieve you of the trouble and payment. Understand? And not only relieve you, but give you fifteen rubles on top. Now is it clear?"

"Really, I don't know," pronounced the mistress slowly. "After all, I've never sold dead people."

"Of course not! It would be a marvel if you had sold them to someone. Or do you think they're actually useful for something?"

"No, that I don't think. What good is there in them, no good at all. It's only that it troubles me that they're already dead."

"Good lord, she's such a blockhead!" thought Chichikov to himself.

"Listen, mother. Just think it through properly: you're being ruined, you pay tax for them as for the living..."

"Oh, father, don't speak of it!" the landowner exclaimed. "Just last week I paid more than a hundred and a half. And I greased the assessor's palm."

"Well, you see, mother. And now consider that you no longer need to grease the assessor's palm, because now I pay for them; I, not you; I assume all obligations. I shall even complete the deed of purchase with my own money, do you understand?"

The old woman grew thoughtful. She saw that the matter was indeed, so to speak, profitable, but it was all too new and unprecedented; and so she began to fear greatly that this buyer might cheat her; he came from God knows where and arrived at nighttime too.

"So what, mother, is it a deal?" said Chichikov.

"Really, father, I've never happened to sell dead people. I've ceded living ones; just three years ago I gave two girls to the archpriest for a hundred rubles each, and he was very grateful; such fine workers they turned out to be: they weave napkins themselves."

"Well, never mind the living ones; God be with them. I'm asking about the dead."

"Really, I'm afraid at first, lest I suffer a loss somehow. Maybe you, father, are deceiving me, and they cost... they might be worth more."

"Listen, mother... eh, what are you! What could they be worth? Think about it: they're dust. Understand? Simply dust. You take any useless, last thing, for example even a simple rag, and the rag has a price: it's bought at least for the paper factory, but that's not needed for anything. Well, tell me, what is it needed for?"

"That's certainly true. Completely useless; but still, it's only that it troubles me that they're already dead."

"Ugh, what a blockhead she is!" said Chichikov to himself, now beginning to lose patience. "Try to deal with her! She's driven me into a sweat, the cursed old woman!" Then, pulling a handkerchief from his pocket, he began to wipe away the sweat that had indeed appeared on his forehead. However, Chichikov was wrong to be angry: even a respectable and even governmental man sometimes turns out to be a complete Korobochka in practice. Once she fastens something in her head, nothing can move her; however many clear as day arguments you present to her, everything bounces off her like a rubber ball bounces off a wall. Having wiped away the sweat, Chichikov decided to try whether he could not guide her onto the path from some other angle.

"You, mother," he said, "either do not wish to understand my words, or are deliberately speaking, just to say something... I give you money: fifteen paper rubles. Understand? That's money. You won't find it in the street. Well, come on, at what price did you sell the honey?"

"At twelve rubles a pood."

"You've sinned a little for your soul, mother. You didn't sell it at twelve."

"By God, I did."

"Well, you see? But that's because it's honey. You gathered it, perhaps, around a year, with care, with effort, with trouble; you traveled, you fed the bees, you kept them in the cellar all winter; but dead souls are not of this world. You, for your part, made no effort, it was God's will that they left this world, causing harm to your household. There you received twelve rubles for labor, for effort, but here you're taking fifteen simply, for nothing, for free, and not twelve, but fifteen, and not in silver, but all in blue assignats." After such strong arguments, Chichikov almost did not doubt that the old woman would finally yield.

"Really," answered the mistress, "my experience as a widow is so inexperienced! Better I wait a bit, perhaps merchants will come by and I can compare prices."

"Shame, shame, mother! Simply shame! Well, what do you say, think about it! Who would buy them? What use could he make of them?"

"And perhaps somehow in the household... if the occasion should arise..." objected the old woman, and did not finish, opened her mouth and looked at him almost in fear, wishing to know what he would say.

"Dead in the household! Good heavens, what a notion! Perhaps to scare sparrows at night in your garden, eh?"

"Lord preserve us! What passions you speak of!" uttered the old woman, crossing herself.

"Where else would you place them? But, in any case, the bones and the graves—all of that remains with you; the transfer is only on paper. Well, so what? How? Answer at least."

The old woman thought again.

"What are you thinking about, Nastasya Petrovna?"

"Really, I can't figure out what to do; better I sell you the hemp."

"But what's the hemp? Forgive me, I'm asking you about something completely different, and you keep thrusting hemp at me! Hemp is hemp; another time I'll come and take the hemp too. So what, Nastasya Petrovna?"

"By God, merchandise is so strange, completely unprecedented!"

At this point Chichikov went completely beyond the bounds of all patience, struck a chair against the floor in his vexation, and promised her the devil.

The devil frightened the mistress extraordinarily.

"Oh, don't mention him, God willing!" she cried out, turning completely pale. "The cursed thing was in my dreams all last night. I was going to guess on the cards after prayers, but apparently as punishment God sent him to me. Such a nasty thing appeared; and the horns—longer than a bull's."

"I'm amazed you don't dream of them by the dozens. Out of purely Christian humanity I wanted to help: I see a poor widow suffering, enduring hardship... but let her die with all her village!..."

"Ah, what oaths you're swearing!" said the old woman, looking at him in fear.

"There are no words for you! Really, you're like some, if you'll pardon the expression, yard dog that lies on the hay and neither eats the hay itself nor lets others eat it. I wanted to buy various household products from you, because I also handle government contracts..." Here he lied, though casually and without any further thought, but unexpectedly successfully. Government contracts had a strong effect on Nastasya Petrovna, at least she pronounced her words already in an almost pleading voice:

"Why did you get so angry? If I had known before that you were so quick-tempered, I wouldn't have contradicted you at all."

"What is there to get angry about! The matter isn't worth an egg, and I'd get angry about it!"

"Well, all right, I'm ready to give it to you for fifteen assignats! Only look, father, and concerning the contracts: if it happens that you take flour, rye or buckwheat, or groats, or butchered cattle, then please, don't offend me."

"No, mother, I won't offend you," he said, and meanwhile wiped away with his hand the sweat that was streaming down his face in three rivulets. He asked her whether she had in town some trustee or acquaintance whom she could authorize to complete the deed of purchase and everything else required.

"Well, the archpriest, Father Kiril; his son serves in the chamber," said Korobochka.

Chichikov asked her to write a letter of attorney to him and, to spare her excessive trouble, even took it upon himself to compose it.

"It would be good," thought Korobochka meanwhile, "if he took flour and butchered cattle from me for the government. I ought to please him: there's dough left from yesterday evening, so I'll tell Fetunya to bake some pancakes; it would also be good to bake a simple pie with eggs, I do them nicely, and it doesn't take much time."      The face of Nozdrev is probably already somewhat familiar to the reader. Such people are encountered by everyone in abundance. They are always talkers, revelers, reckless fellows, a conspicuous people. Nozdrev at thirty-five was exactly the same as he had been at eighteen and twenty: a lover of revelry. His marriage did not change him in the least, especially since his wife soon departed to the other world, leaving two little children who were decidedly of no use to him. However, a pretty nanny looked after the children. At home he could not sit still for more than a day. His keen nose could smell from dozens of versts away where there was a fair with all sorts of gatherings and balls; he would be there in the blink of an eye, arguing and causing confusion at the gaming table, for he had, like all such people, a passion for cards. At cards, as we have already seen from the first chapter, he played not entirely honestly and purely, knowing many different tricks and other subtleties, and therefore the game very often ended in another kind of game: either they beat him with their boots, or they pulled his thick and very fine whiskers, so that he sometimes returned home with only one whisker, and that rather sparse. But his healthy and full cheeks were so well made and contained so much vital force that the whiskers soon grew back, even better than before. And what is strangest of all, what can only happen in Russia, after some time he would already meet again with those friends who had beaten him, and he would meet them as if nothing had happened, and he,as they say, nothing, and they are nothing.
