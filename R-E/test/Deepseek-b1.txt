**CHAPTER THREE**

Meanwhile, Chichikov, in a contented frame of mind, was sitting in his britzka, which had for some time been rolling along the main road. From the preceding chapter it is already clear what constituted the main object of his taste and inclinations, and therefore it is no wonder that he soon became immersed in it body and soul. The suppositions, estimates, and considerations that wandered across his face were evidently very pleasant, for every minute they left behind traces of a satisfied smirk. Engrossed in these, he paid no attention whatsoever to the way his coachman, pleased with the reception from Manilov's household servants, was making very sensible remarks to the piebald trace horse harnessed on the right side. This piebald horse was very crafty and only pretended to be pulling, while the shaft horse, a bay, and the other trace horse, a sorrel, called the Assessor because he had been bought from some assessor, toiled with all their hearts, so that even in their eyes one could notice the pleasure they derived from it. "Be tricky, be tricky! I'll out-tricky you!" said Selifan, rising up and lashing the lazy one with his whip. "Mind your own business, you German pantaloon! The bay is a respectable horse, he does his duty; I'll gladly give him an extra measure, because he's a respectable horse, and the Assessor is a good horse too… Now, now! why are you twitching your ears? You fool, listen when you're spoken to! I won't teach you ill, you ignoramus. Look where he's crawling!" Here he lashed him again with the whip, adding: "Oh, you barbarian! You cursed Bonaparte!" Then he shouted at all three: "Hey there, my dears!"—and lashed all three, not as punishment, but to show he was pleased with them. Having bestowed this pleasure, he again addressed the piebald: "You think you can hide your behavior. No, you must live honestly if you want to be shown respect. Now, that landowner we visited, he has good people. I'll gladly talk if the person is good; with a good man we're always friends, close pals; whether to drink tea or have a bite—with pleasure, if he's a good man. Everyone respects a good man. Now, our master, everyone respects him, because, hear me, he performs his government service, he's a collegiate councilor…"

Thus discoursing, Selifan eventually wandered into the most remote abstractions. If Chichikov had listened, he would have learned many details relating personally to himself; but his thoughts were so occupied with his own subject that only a powerful clap of thunder made him come to his senses and look around him. The entire sky was completely overcast with clouds, and the dusty post road was sprinkled with raindrops. At last a thunderclap sounded a second time, louder and nearer, and the rain suddenly poured down as from a bucket. First, taking an oblique direction, it lashed one side of the wagon body, then the other; then, changing its mode of attack and becoming completely straight, it drummed directly on the top of the carriage; finally, splashes began to reach his face. This made him draw the leather curtains with two round little windows, intended for viewing roadside sights, and order Selifan to drive faster. Selifan, also interrupted in the middle of his speech, realized that indeed he shouldn't dawdle; he immediately pulled out from under the driver's seat some old gray cloth, put his arms through the sleeves, took hold of the reins, and shouted at his trio, which was barely moving its feet, feeling a pleasant relaxation from the instructive discourse. But Selifan could not remember whether he had passed two or three turnings. Figuring and recalling the road somewhat, he guessed that there had been many turns, all of which he had missed. Since a Russian man in decisive moments will find something to do without resorting to lengthy reasoning, he turned right onto the first crossroad, shouted, "Hey, you honorable friends!"—and set off at a gallop, giving little thought to where the chosen road would lead.

The rain, however, seemed set in for a long time. The dust lying on the road was quickly kneaded into mud, and it became increasingly harder for the horses to pull the britzka. Chichikov was beginning to get very worried, not seeing Sobakevich's village for so long. According to his reckoning, it was high time to have arrived. He peered around, but the darkness was so thick you could poke your eye out.

"Selifan!" he finally said, sticking his head out of the britzka.

"What, master?" answered Selifan.

"See if you can see the village!"

"No, master, nowhere in sight!" After which Selifan, swinging his whip, began to sing a song—not exactly a song, but something so long that it had no end. Into it went all the encouraging and urging cries with which horses are treated throughout Russia from one end to the other; adjectives of all genders without further discrimination, whatever first popped onto his tongue. In this way, he ended up finally calling them secretaries.

Meanwhile, Chichikov began to notice that the britzka was swaying in all directions and giving him very strong jolts; this made him feel they had turned off the road and were probably dragging across a harrowed field. Selifan himself seemed to realize it but said nothing.

"What, you scoundrel, what road are you driving on?" said Chichikov.

"Well, what can you do, master, such a time it is; you can't see the whip, such darkness!" Having said this, he skewed the britzka so much that Chichikov was forced to hold on with both hands. Only then did he notice that Selifan was tipsy.

"Steady, steady, you'll tip us over!" he shouted at him.

"No, master, how could I tip it over?" said Selifan. "That's not good, tipping over; I know that myself; I won't tip it over at all." Then he began to turn the britzka slightly; he turned, and turned, and finally tilted it completely onto its side. Chichikov plopped into the mud with hands and feet. Selifan, however, stopped the horses, though they would have stopped themselves, being completely exhausted. Such an unforeseen event completely bewildered him. Climbing down from the box, he stood before the britzka, arms akimbo, while the master floundered in the mud, trying to climb out, and said after some reflection: "See now, it's tipped over!"

"You're drunk as a cobbler!" said Chichikov.

"No, master, how can I be drunk? I know it's a bad thing to be drunk. I talked with a friend, because you can talk with a good man, there's nothing bad in that; and we had a bite together. A bite isn't a harmful thing; you can have a bite with a good man."

"What did I tell you the last time you got drunk, eh? You've forgotten?" said Chichikov.

"No, your honor, how could I forget? I know my business. I know it's bad to be drunk. I talked with a good man, because…"

"Then I'll have you flogged, and you'll know how to talk to a good man!"

"As your grace wishes," replied Selifan, agreeing to everything. "If you flog me, then flog me; I'm not at all against it. Why not flog me if it's deserved? That's the master's will. It's necessary to flog, because the peasant plays tricks; order must be maintained. If it's deserved, then flog me; why not flog me?"

To such reasoning, the master had no idea what to answer. But at that moment, it seemed as if fate itself decided to take pity on him. From afar, a dog's barking was heard. Delighted, Chichikov gave orders to urge the horses on. A Russian coachman has a good instinct instead of eyes; it happens that, with his eyes shut, he sometimes rattles along at full speed and always gets somewhere. Selifan, seeing nothing, directed the horses so straight to the village that he stopped only when the britzka's shafts struck the fence and there was absolutely nowhere else to go. Chichikov saw through the thick veil of pouring rain only something like a roof. He sent Selifan to look for the gate, which would no doubt have taken a long time, if in Russia there were not, instead of porters, spirited dogs, which announced him so loudly that he put his fingers to his ears. A light flickered in one window and reached the fence in a misty stream, showing our travelers the gate. Selifan began knocking, and soon, a gate was opened, and some figure in a peasant coat stuck out, and the master and servant heard a hoarse woman's voice:

"Who's knocking? What's all this commotion?"

"Travelers, dear mother, let us spend the night," said Chichikov.

"See what a sharp-stepping fellow," said the old woman. "What a time to arrive! This isn't an inn; a gentlewoman lives here."

"What can we do, mother? You see, we've lost our way. We can't spend the night in the steppe at such a time."

"Yes, the time is dark, a bad time," added Selifan.

"Shut up, fool," said Chichikov.

"But who are you?" said the old woman.

"A gentleman, mother."

The word "gentleman" seemed to make the old woman think for a while.

"Wait, I'll tell the mistress," she said, and returned two minutes later with a lantern in her hand.

The gates opened. A light flickered in another window as well. The britzka drove into the yard and stopped before a small house, which was hard to make out in the darkness. Only one half of it was lit by the light coming from the windows; a puddle before the house was also visible, directly illuminated by the same light. The rain drummed loudly on the wooden roof and flowed in murmuring streams into a barrel placed beneath. Meanwhile, the dogs filled the air with all possible barks: one, throwing his head up, produced a sound so drawn out and with such effort, as if he were receiving God knows what salary for it; another chopped off rapidly like a sexton; between them rang, like a postal bell, a restless treble, probably of a young puppy; and all this was finally topped off by a bass, perhaps an old fellow endowed with a stout doggy nature, because he wheezed like a singing double bass when the concert is in full flood: the tenors rise on tiptoes from a strong desire to hit a high note, and everything strains upward, throwing back its head, while he alone, having shoved his unshaven chin into his cravat, crouched and dropped almost to the ground, lets out his note from there, making the windows shake and rattle. By the dog's barking alone, composed of such musicians, one could suppose that the village was respectable; but our soaked and chilled hero thought only of a bed. The britzka had hardly come to a complete stop when he jumped onto the porch, staggered, and nearly fell. Some woman, younger than the previous one but very much like her, came out onto the porch. She led him into the room. Chichikov threw a couple of glances around: the room was hung with old striped wallpaper; pictures with some birds; between the windows were old small mirrors with dark frames in the shape of curled leaves; behind every mirror was stuck either a letter, or an old deck of cards, or a stocking; wall clock with painted flowers on the face… he could not notice anything more. He felt his eyes sticking together as if someone had smeared them with honey. A minute later, the hostess entered, an elderly woman, wearing some sort of sleeping cap put on hurriedly, with a flannel around her neck—one of those mothers, small estate-owners, who complain about crop failures, losses, and keep their heads somewhat to one side, while at the same time gradually accumulate little sums of money in colorful cloth bags placed in the drawers of chests. In one bag they pick out all the rubles, in another fifty-kopeck pieces, in a third quarter-rubles, although it seems as if there is nothing in the chest but linen, night jackets, cotton reels, and a ripped-open wadded cloak, which later is to be turned into a dress, if the old one somehow gets burnt while baking holiday pancakes with various fry-ups, or wears out by itself. But the dress will not burn or wear out by itself: the old woman is thrifty, and the cloak is destined to lie long in its ripped-open state, and then go, by testament, to her sister's granddaughter, along with all the other junk.

Chichikov apologized for disturbing her with his unexpected arrival.

"It's nothing, nothing," said the hostess. "What a time God has brought you! Such turmoil and blizzard… You should eat something from the road, but it's night-time, nothing can be prepared."

The hostess's words were interrupted by a strange hissing, which made the guest start; the noise was as if the whole room had filled with snakes; but, looking up, he calmed down, realizing that the wall clock had taken a fancy to strike. After the hissing came immediately a wheezing, and finally, straining with all its might, it struck two o'clock with a sound like someone hitting a broken pot with a stick, after which the pendulum again began its quiet ticking to the right and left.

Chichikov thanked the hostess, saying he needed nothing, that she should not trouble herself about anything, that he wanted nothing but a bed, and was only curious to know what place he had come to and how far it was from there to the landowner Sobakevich; to which the old woman replied that she had never heard such a name, and that there was no such landowner.

"Then at least you know Manilov?" said Chichikov.

"And who is Manilov?"

"A landowner, mother."

"No, I've never heard of him; there's no such landowner."

"What ones are there, then?"

"Bobrov, Svinin, Kanapatyev, Kharpakin, Trepakin, Pleshakov."

"Are they rich or not?"

"No, father, there aren't any too rich. Some have twenty souls, some thirty, but as for those with a hundred, there aren't any."

Chichikov noticed that he had come to quite a backwater.

"How far, at least, to the town?"

"About sixty versts. What a pity that I have nothing to feed you with! Would you like some tea, father?"

"Thank you, mother. Nothing but a bed, please."

"Indeed, after such a journey you must rest. Here, father, lie down on this sofa. Hey, Fetinya, bring the featherbed, pillows, and a sheet. What a time God has sent: such thunder! I've had a candle burning before my icon all night. Oh, my dear, but you've got mud all over your back and side, like a hog! Where did you manage to get so soiled?"

"Thank God it's only soiled; I should be thankful my sides aren't broken."

"Saints preserve us, what horrors! But don't you need something to rub your back with?"

"Thank you, thank you. Don't trouble yourself; just tell your girl to dry and clean my clothes."

"Do you hear, Fetinya?" said the hostess, turning to the woman who had come out onto the porch with a candle, who had already managed to drag in a featherbed and, beating it on both sides with her hands, had sent a whole flood of feathers flying all over the room. "Take his coat along with the rest, and first dry them before the fire, as you used to do for the late master, and then rub them and beat them thoroughly."

"Yes, mistress," said Fetinya, spreading a sheet over the featherbed and laying down the pillows.

"Well, here's your bed ready," said the hostess. "Goodbye, father, I wish you a good night. But don't you need anything else? Perhaps you're used, my dear, to having your heels scratched at night? My late husband could never sleep without that."

But the guest declined even the heel scratching. The hostess left, and he hurried to undress at once, handing over to Fetinya all his trappings, both upper and lower; and Fetinya, also wishing him good night on her part, carried off these wet garments. Left alone, he looked at his bed not without pleasure; it was almost up to the ceiling. Fetinya was evidently a mistress at fluffing up featherbeds. When, having brought up a chair, he climbed onto the bed, it sank down under him almost to the floor, and the feathers, forced out of their bounds, flew into all corners of the room. Blowing out the candle, he covered himself with the chintz quilt and, curling up under it like a pretzel, fell asleep that very minute. He woke up the next day quite late in the morning. The sun was shining straight into his eyes through the window, and the flies that had slept peacefully on the walls and ceiling yesterday had all turned toward him: one sat on his lip, another on his ear, a third tried to settle right on his eye, and that fly which had the carelessness to approach his nostril he snuffled into his nose in his half-sleep, which made him sneeze violently—an event that caused his awakening. Glancing around the room, he now noticed that not all the figures in the pictures were birds: between them hung a portrait of Kutuzov and an old man painted in oils with red cuffs on his uniform, such as were worn in the time of Pavel Petrovich. The clock again let out a hiss and struck ten; a woman's face peered in at the door and immediately disappeared, for Chichikov, wishing to sleep better, had taken off absolutely everything. The face that peered in seemed to him somewhat familiar. He began to recall who it might be, and finally remembered that it was the hostess. He put on his shirt; his clothes, now dried and cleaned, lay next to him. Dressing, he approached the mirror and sneezed again so loudly that an Indian rooster that had come up to the window just then—and the window was very close to the ground—suddenly chattered something to him very quickly in its strange language, probably "God bless you!" to which Chichikov called him a fool. Going up to the window, he began to survey the view before him: the window looked out onto what was practically the henhouse; at least the narrow little yard before it was filled with birds and all sorts of domestic creatures. There were countless turkeys and hens; among them a rooster paced with measured steps, shaking its comb and turning its head sideways as if listening to something; a sow with her family was also there, rooting through a pile of rubbish, and in passing she ate a chick, and, not noticing this, continued munching watermelon rinds in her own manner. This little yard, or henhouse, was enclosed by a wooden fence, beyond which stretched spacious kitchen gardens with cabbage, onions, potatoes, turnips, and other kitchen vegetables. Here and there among the gardens were scattered apple trees and other fruit trees, covered with nets to protect them from magpies and sparrows, the latter of which flew in whole oblique clouds from one place to another. For the same purpose, several scarecrows were mounted on long poles, with outstretched arms; one of them wore the mistress's own cap. Beyond the gardens came the peasant huts, which, though built in a scattered fashion and not enclosed in proper streets, nevertheless, according to Chichikov's observation, showed the prosperity of the inhabitants, for they were well maintained: the rotten boards on the roofs had been replaced everywhere with new ones; the gates were not crooked anywhere; and in the peasant-covered sheds facing him he noticed where a spare, nearly new wagon stood, and where even two. "She has quite a village," he said to himself, and resolved right then to talk and become more closely acquainted with the hostess. He peeped through the crack in the door from which she had stuck out her head, and, seeing her sitting at the tea table, went in with a cheerful and amiable air.

"Good morning, father. How did you sleep?" said the hostess, rising from her place. She was dressed better than yesterday, in a dark gown and no longer in her sleeping cap, but her neck was still tied up with something.

"Good, good," said Chichikov, sitting down in an armchair. "And how are you, mother?"

"Badly, my dear."

"How so?"

"Insomnia. All my lower back aches, and my leg, above the bone, just aches and aches."

"It will pass, it will pass, mother. Don't you worry about that."

"God grant it pass. I rubbed it with lard and soaked it with turpentine too. What will you have your tea with? There's fruit cordial in the flask."

"Not bad, mother, let's have a drop of fruit cordial."

The reader, I believe, has already noticed that Chichikov, despite his amiable air, spoke with much more freedom than he had with Manilov, and was not at all ceremonious. It must be said that in Russia, if we have not caught up with foreigners in some things, we have far surpassed them in the ability to behave. It is impossible to enumerate all the shades and subtleties of our behavior. A Frenchman or a German will never figure out or understand all its peculiarities and differences; he will speak in almost the same voice and the same language to a millionaire and to a small tobacco merchant, though, of course, he will grovel inwardly in due measure before the former. With us it's different: we have such wise men who will speak quite differently to a landowner who owns two hundred souls than to one who owns three hundred, and to the one with three hundred, again not as to the one with five hundred, and to the one with five hundred, again not as to the one with eight hundred—in short, though you go up to a million, you'll still find shades. Let us suppose, for example, there is an office, not here, but in the thirtieth kingdom, and in that office, suppose there is an office director. Look at him when he sits among his subordinates—you simply can't utter a word out of fear! Pride and nobility, and what doesn't his face express! Just take a brush and paint: Prometheus, a defiant Prometheus! He looks out like an eagle, steps smoothly, measuredly. But the same eagle, as soon as he leaves the room and approaches his superior's office, rushes about like a partridge with papers under his arm, straining every muscle. In society or at a party, if everyone is of a low rank, Prometheus will remain Prometheus, but if someone a little higher comes along, such a transformation takes place with Prometheus as Ovid himself never invented: a fly, even smaller than a fly, he is annihilated into a grain of sand! "This isn't Ivan Petrovich," you say, looking at him. "Ivan Petrovich is taller, and this one is short and thin; the former speaks loudly, in a bass voice, and never laughs, but this one, the devil knows what: he squeaks like a bird and keeps laughing." You come closer, look—it is Ivan Petrovich, sure enough! "Heh-heh," you think to yourself… But, however, let us return to the characters. Chichikov, as we have already seen, decided to stand on no ceremony at all, and so, taking his cup of tea and pouring fruit cordial into it, began as follows:

"You have a nice little village, mother. How many souls are in it?"

"Souls, my dear, nearly eighty," said the hostess, "but the trouble is, times are bad, and last year there was such a crop failure, God forbid."

"Yet the peasants look sturdy, the little huts are solid. Allow me to ask your name. I was so distracted… I arrived at night…"

"Korobochka, Collegiate Secretary's wife."

"Most humbly grateful. And your first name and patronymic?"

"Nastasya Petrovna."

"Nastasya Petrovna? A good name, Nastasya Petrovna. I have an aunt, my mother's sister, Nastasya Petrovna."

"And what is your name?" asked the landowner. "You're an assessor, I suppose?"

"No, mother," replied Chichikov, smiling. "Not an assessor, just traveling on our own business."

"Ah, so you're a buyer! What a pity, really, that I sold my honey to the merchants so cheap; you, my dear, would certainly have bought it from me."

"Well, I wouldn't have bought the honey."

"What then? Hemp, perhaps? But I have too little hemp now: only about half a pood."

"No, mother, a different sort of goods: tell me, have any of your peasants died?"

"Oh, my dear, eighteen of them," said the old woman, sighing. "And all such fine people died, all of them workers. After that, true, some were born, but what good are they? All such little runts. And then the assessor came—he says, pay the tax per soul. The people are dead, but you pay as for the living. Last week my blacksmith burned up, such a skillful blacksmith, and knew locksmithing too."

"Did you have a fire, mother?"

"God spared me from such a calamity; a fire would be worse; he burned up himself, my dear. Something inside him caught fire; he drank too much; only a blue flame came out of him; he smoldered and smoldered, turned black as charcoal, and he was such a skillful blacksmith! And now I've got nothing to drive out in: no one to shoe the horses."

"God's will in all things, mother!" said Chichikov, sighing. "Nothing can be said against God's wisdom… Won't you cede them to me, Nastasya Petrovna?"

"Whom, father?"

"Well, all these dead ones."

"But how can you cede them?"

"Just like that. Or, if you prefer, sell them. I'll give you money for them."

"But how? I really can't make it out. Do you want to dig them out of the ground?"

Chichikov saw that the old woman had gone far afield and that it was necessary to explain the matter to her. In a few words he explained to her that the transfer or purchase would only be on paper, and the souls would be written down as though alive.

"But what do you need them for?" said the old woman, staring at him with wide eyes.

"That's my business."

"But they're dead."

"Who says they're alive? That's why they're a loss to you, because they're dead: you pay for them, and now I will rid you of the trouble and the payment. Do you understand? And not only will I rid you of it, but I'll give you fifteen rubles besides. Now, is it clear?"

"Really, I don't know," the hostess said slowly. "I've never sold dead ones before."

"Of course not! It would be more like a miracle if you had sold them to anyone. Or do you think there's any profit in them?"

"No, I don't think so. What profit could there be? There's no profit at all. What bothers me is that they're already dead."

"Well, this woman seems thick-skulled!" Chichikov thought to himself.

"Listen, mother. Just think it over carefully: you're ruining yourself, paying taxes for him as if he were alive…"

"Oh, my dear, don't mention it!" the landowner chimed in. "Just three weeks ago I paid out more than a hundred and fifty rubles. And I bribed the assessor too."

"Well, you see, mother. Now just consider that you won't need to bribe the assessor anymore, because now I'll pay for them; I, not you. I take all the obligations upon myself. I'll even draw up the deed with my own money. Do you understand that?"

The old woman became thoughtful. She saw that the business certainly seemed profitable, but it was too new and unprecedented; and so she began to be very afraid that this buyer might somehow cheat her; he came God knows from where, and at night, too.

"So what, mother, do we have a deal?" said Chichikov.

"Really, my dear, I've never happened to sell the dead. I've let live ones go; two years ago I sold two girls to the archpriest, a hundred rubles each, and he was very grateful. Such fine workers they turned out: they weave napkins themselves."

"Well, let's not talk about the living; God be with them. I'm asking about the dead."

"Really, I'm afraid at first of somehow incurring a loss. Maybe you, my dear, are deceiving me, and they… they're somehow worth more."

"Listen, mother… oh, what are you like! What can they possibly be worth? Just consider: they're dust. Do you understand? It's simply dust. Take any worthless, cheapest thing—even a simple rag—and a rag has some value; at least they'll buy it for a paper mill, but this isn't needed for anything at all. Now tell me yourself, what is it good for?"

"It certainly is true. It's good for nothing at all. But the only thing that stops me is that they're dead."

"What a blockhead you are!" Chichikov said to himself, beginning to lose patience. "Try and deal with her! She's got me sweating, the cursed old woman!" Whereupon he took out his handkerchief and began to wipe away the sweat that had indeed appeared on his brow. In fact, Chichikov was angry in vain: even a respectable, even a state figure, can turn out to be a perfect Korobochka. Once something gets stuck in his head, you can't overpower him with anything; no matter how many arguments you present, clear as day, everything bounces off him like a rubber ball from a wall. Having wiped off the sweat, Chichikov decided to try whether he could lead her onto the path from another direction.

"You, mother," he said, "either don't want to understand my words, or you're speaking on purpose just to be saying something… I'm giving you money: fifteen rubles in bank notes. Do you understand? That's money. You won't find it on the street. Now, admit it, what did you sell your honey for?"

"Twelve rubles a pood."

"You've sinned a bit against your soul, mother. You didn't sell it for twelve."

"By God, I did."

"Well, you see. But honey is one thing. You collected it for perhaps a year, with cares, efforts, troubles; you traveled around, tormented the bees, fed them in the cellar all winter; but dead souls are not of this world. You, on your part, exerted no effort; it was God's will that they left this world, causing damage to your household. There, you got twelve rubles for your labor, your effort; but here you'll be getting it for nothing, for free, and not twelve, but fifteen, and not in silver, but all in blue banknotes." After such strong persuasions, Chichikov had almost no doubt that the old woman would finally give in.

"Really," replied the landowner, "it's my inexperienced widow's lot! I'd better wait a little; maybe merchants will come, and I'll get a sense of the prices."

"Shame, shame, mother! Simply shame! What are you saying, just think for yourself! Who would ever buy them? What use could he possibly make of them?"

"Maybe they might come in handy in the household somehow…" the old woman retorted, and didn't finish, opened her mouth, and looked at him almost with fear, wanting to know what he would say to that.

"Dead souls in a household! What a notion! To scare sparrows with them in your garden at night, perhaps?"

"God save us! What horrors you speak!" said the old woman, crossing herself.

"Where else did you mean to put them? But besides, the bones and graves all remain yours; the transfer is only on paper. Well, then? What do you say? Answer me at least."

The old woman began to think again.

"What are you thinking about, Nastasya Petrovna?"

"Really, I just can't decide what to do; I'd better sell you some hemp."

"Why hemp? For goodness' sake, I'm asking you about something quite different, and you shove hemp at me! Hemp is hemp, I'll come another time and take the hemp too. So what about it, Nastasya Petrovna?"

"By God, it's such a strange commodity, quite unheard of!"

Here Chichikov went completely beyond the bounds of all patience, slammed a chair on the floor in a rage, and promised her the devil.

The landowner was extraordinarily frightened of the devil.

"Oh, don't mention him, God be with him!" she cried, turning pale all over. "Just the other night, that accursed one haunted me all night. I decided to tell fortunes on cards after prayers, and evidently God sent him as a punishment. He looked so ugly; and his horns were longer than a bull's."

"I'm surprised they don't appear to you by the dozens. Out of Christian charity I wanted to—I see a poor widow grieving, suffering need… but go to the devil and perish with all your village!…"

"Oh, what a temper you have!" said the old woman, looking at him fearfully.

"I can't find words to talk to you! Truly, like some—pardon my language—like a mongrel lying on the hay, that neither eats the hay itself nor lets others eat. I was planning to buy various household products from you, because I also handle government contracts…" Here he told a lie, though casually and without further thought, but it turned out unexpectedly well. Government contracts had a strong effect on Nastasya Petrovna; at least she said, almost in a pleading voice:

"But why did you get so angry? If I had known you were so irritable, I wouldn't have argued with you at all."

"There's something to be angry about! The matter isn't worth a hen's egg, and I'm supposed to get angry over it?"

"Well then, I'm ready to turn them over for fifteen rubles in banknotes! But see here, my dear, concerning the contracts: if it happens you need to take rye flour, or buckwheat, or groats, or slaughtered meat, then please don't cheat me."

Since the conversation the travelers had among themselves was not very interesting to the reader, we would do better to say something about Nozdryov himself, who may perhaps be destined to play not the least role in our poem.

Nozdryov's face is probably already somewhat familiar to the reader. Everyone has come across many such people. They are called "dashing fellows," and are known even in childhood and at school as good comrades, and yet they get beaten rather badly. There is always something open, direct, and daring in their faces. They make friends quickly, and before you know it, they are already addressing you as "thou." They seem to strike up a friendship forever; but it almost always happens that he who has become friends with them will fight with them that same evening at a friendly banquet. They are always talkers, carousers, daredevils, and prominent people. At thirty-five, Nozdryov was exactly the same as he had been at eighteen and twenty: a lover of revelry. His marriage had changed him not at all, especially since his wife had soon departed for the next world, leaving two children whom he had absolutely no use for. However, a pretty nursemaid looked after the children. He could not stay at home for more than a day. His keen nose could hear for several tens of versts where a fair with various gatherings and balls was taking place; in an instant he was there, arguing and creating a commotion at the green table, for he had, like all such people, a passion for cards. At cards, as we have already seen from the first chapter, he did not play entirely without sin and purity, knowing many different sorts of dealing tricks and other subtleties, and so the game very often ended with another kind of game: either he was beaten with boots, or they gave his thick and very fine side-whiskers a working-over, so that he sometimes returned home with only one side-whisker, and that quite sparse. But his healthy, plump cheeks were so well made and contained so much vegetative power that his side-whiskers soon grew back, even better than before. And what is strangest of all, something that can happen only in Russia, he would meet again with the same friends who had beaten him up a short while later, and he would meet them as if nothing had happened, and he, as they say, was all right, and they were all right.

Nozdryov was in some respects a historical person. Not a single meeting where he was present could be without a story. Some story was bound to happen: either the gendarmes would lead him out of the hall by the arm, or his own friends would be forced to give him a push. If that didn't happen, then something else would: he would down so much in the buffet that he would only laugh; or he would get so carried away in the middle of the boulevard that he would cut a caper, and the worst of it was that he would always let slip some tall tale, so that finally everyone would get fed up with him. He lied completely gratuitously: he would suddenly tell that he had a blue or pink horse, and such nonsense that the listeners would finally walk away saying, "Well, brother, you seem to have started letting off hot steam." There are people who have a passion to spoil their neighbor, sometimes for no reason at all. For example, a person of high standing, even with a noble appearance, with a star on his chest, would shake your hand, talk with you about deep subjects that provoke thought, and then, right there before your eyes, would proceed to spoil you. And he would spoil you like a simple collegiate registrar, not like a man with a star on his chest, talking about the brother of a *bon-ton* and the *demimonde*. He felt the same urge as Nozdryov. Nozdryov could easily spoil his closest friend without any reason at all: spread a rumor that so-and-so had sold some counterfeit horses, or something like that. Strange as it seems, he had an inexplicable passion for lying. In another situation, if they met again, he would again be friendly and would even say: "Ah, you so-and-so, why don't you come to my house?" In many respects, he was a multifaceted man, that is, a man of all trades. At that very moment, he proposed that you go with him anywhere, even to the ends of the world; enter whatever enterprise you like; trade in anything you like. The fact that he had been cleaned out at cards did not make him lose his composure. It's likely that one could not even call him a gambler in the full sense: he was not a player who wants to win; the very process of the game, the excitement, was what he loved, and the fact that his partners laughed at the mistakes of his rather short-sighted play was also to his liking. The reader has probably learned that he brought a puppy from the fair, a *mordash* dog, because he had long had his eye on a *mordash*; that he did not bring his own horses, but came in his brother-in-law's britzka; that he had already lost, like a simpleton, not only his four trotters, but also his chain and watch—all this did not sadden him in the least. A quarter of an hour before his wedding he was ready to do anything he was asked, just so long as there was some fun. It could be seen that he was already beginning to feel the urge to do something, to stir up some story. It seemed that at any moment he would propose some impossible stunt, the likes of which no one else would ever dream of, such as: go to the next village with whips to play a game of *bank*, and so that his hands would sweat from the excitement. But let us not dwell too long on Nozdryov's passions; for we have a long road ahead of us, and there is not much room for digressions.

The fellow traveler, Nozdryov's brother-inbow, Mr. Mizhuev, was also a man of some substance. He wore a mustache, and his face was so lean that it looked like a lot of people had ridden on it. He turned out to be a landowner, and, as he said, he loved a good dog. To everything Nozdryov said, he added a short, "And that's the truth," but he never finished his sentences. He was one of those people who are not at all mild-tempered, but who, seeing that they are dealing with an obstinate man like Nozdryov, agree out of cowardice and therefore spend their time in a kind of constant vexation. From time to time he cast oblique glances at Nozdryov's britzka, into which he had been forced to transfer, and Chichikov noticed that from those glances he felt he wanted to escape, but he was caught: there was no way he could jump out and run away into the field.

They arrived. The village of Nozdryov's was already visible: a field, a grove, and then the manor house. On the way, they met many obstacles: some muddy, some washed-out. They had to cross, at one point, a river that was shallow in some places, but so full of water that it was necessary to splash through in such a way that the water covered the wheel hubs. They had to wander a good deal, especially as it was getting dark. Nozdryov, who did not know the local roads well, gave many orders, but he only made them drive around in circles. At last they approached the gates, and Nozdryov's own carriage, which had been lagging behind for a long time, also drove in.

Nozdryov's house, as it turned out, was not very large, but the yard, despite the onset of darkness, could be seen to be filled with all sorts of things. In the middle of the yard stood a well, and from it a trough, and around the trough there were many puddles. The stable yard was also there, and what looked like a forge. As they drove in, they heard, first of all, the clatter of an iron-covered wheel, then the snorting of horses, then the noise of a human voice that seemed to be scolding someone: "Get up! Get up! I'll give it to you, you rascal!" And at the same time, quite a large grey dog jumped out of the gate, barking, and then two more dogs.

When the britzka drove into the yard, the dogs started barking from all sides. Some ran off to the stables, others jumped out of the sheds, some ran straight at the carriage. Their barks merged into one general, terrible concert. The puppies also got involved; they squeaked and barked. Finally, Nozdryov himself appeared on the porch, barefoot, in a green dressing gown, which, as he said, was "light and comfortable." He was bareheaded, with his hair disheveled. "Aha, Chichikov! So you haven't forgotten me," he said, and then, turning to his brother-in-law: "Well, Mizhuev, you see, I told you he was a kind man." Then he turned to Chichikov: "Why did you stand so long? Eh? I was almost going to send Porfiry to get you. Well, come in, come in! The samovar is ready. Now do you want to play cards? Perhaps we'll play a little *bank*; but I'll set it up for you, and then we'll have supper. We'll have a good evening!"

"Let's go into the house," said Chichikov. "But first, I should tell you: I have a small request for you."

"What request?"

"Give me a moment."

"Very well."

With that, they went into the house. And, as they entered, Nozdryov immediately began to show his guest everything: first, the stable, where there were two mares, one dapple-grey, the other brown, a bay stallion—so-so—and a horse that was not at all distinguished.

"He's not much," said Nozdryov, "but I bought him for three thousand."

"Three thousand!" Mizhuev exclaimed in surprise. "You overpaid, you overpaid a lot!"

"But that's the way it is: he's not worth a thousand."

"By God, he's not worth a thousand," Mizhuev repeated.

"But I say he's worth three thousand," Nozdryov insisted, and began to show the other things in the stable: the saddles, bridles, etc. All the things were in good condition. He showed a stall with a trough, then a pond with fish, and then the dogs. The dogs surprised everyone. There were dogs of all kinds: both big and small; long-haired and smooth-haired; black, red, spotted, black-and-tan, etc. There were dogs that were tied to iron rings, dogs that were loose, dogs that were not yet broken. There were so many of them that you could call Nozdryov a dog breeder. In the middle of the yard, they showed the dogs a dead horse. They also showed a file, a chisel, and a whole pile of iron.

"Here," said Nozdryov, "I have all sorts of things. Here is a knife, fine workmanship; this is a knife, this is a pocket knife; this is a hunting knife. And this is a file; I bought a file in Tula; it's good. And here is a chisel. You see how sharp it is. And here is a horseshoe; I bought it to make a good luck charm."

"And why do you need a horseshoe?" asked Mizhuev.

"What do you mean why? I'll hang it over the door; you can't have too many good luck charms. They're all good."

Finally, they went into the house. The rooms were fairly large. In the first room, which was like a reception room, there were chairs, a sofa, a table, and a small mirror. In the corners there were pictures: a portrait of Suvorov, another of a general, whose uniform was so red that it seemed to have been painted with carrots. In the other room, there were more pictures, but all of them were poor, in cheap frames: some were of fruit, some of animals, and one was of a woman with a very long nose. At the window there was a guitar with a broken neck; on the table, a deck of cards, an inkwell, a quill pen, a penknife, and a pen tray. The room was very dusty, and it was clear that the servant did not clean it often.

"Sit down, sit down," said Nozdryov, "here, on the sofa. You, Mizhuev, sit here. Well, Chichikov, will you play *bank*?"

"Perhaps," said Chichikov, "but I have a small request for you."

"What request?"

"Well, here's the thing: I would like to buy some dead souls from you. Of course, you won't keep them for yourself; they're just a burden to you."

"Dead souls? Why do you need them?"

"That's my business."

"Well, if you want to buy them, then buy them. But I warn you, I won't sell them cheap. They're not like some other things; they're not something you can just give away."

"Perhaps we can come to an agreement," said Chichikov. "But first I'd like to know how many of them you have?"

"How many? Well, I'll tell you: I have a whole heap. I don't know exactly how many; but there are a lot. And I've never counted them."

"Maybe we can count them?"

"Why count them? I know they're all dead; they've been dead a long time. The revision list is in order. I paid taxes for them like for living ones. They've been dead for three years already. I could sell them to you at a good price, I suppose."

"At what price?" asked Chichikov.

"At a hundred rubles apiece."

"A hundred rubles apiece! That's unheard of!" said Chichikov. "What do you take them for? Are they made of gold?"

"Well, why not? After all, they're mine. I can name any price I want."

"But that's too expensive. I'll give you fifty kopecks apiece."

"Fifty kopecks! What a miser you are! I'll give them to you for nothing, but I won't sell them for fifty kopecks. They're my own, you know. I'd rather not sell them at all. I'll keep them as souvenirs, as a memory."

Chichikov saw that Nozdryov was not joking. He also knew that Nozdryov was a man who loved to brag and to make things up. And so he began to bargain.

"Listen, Nozdryov, let's not argue. I'll give you one ruble and fifty kopecks."

"One ruble and fifty kopecks! That's too little. I'll give them to you for two rubles and fifty kopecks, but no less."

"Two rubles and fifty kopecks? That's still too much."

"But they're such souls, you know. They're real souls."

"What do you mean, real? They're dead. They're just names on a list."

"Well, you see, they're on the list. And besides, I know one merchant who sells them for a hundred rubles apiece; he's a reliable man."

"A merchant? What merchant?"

"A friend of mine. He's a reliable man, I'm telling you. He doesn't play games. He gave me his word."

Chichikov thought: "This fellow is lying through his teeth." "Listen, Nozdryov," he said, "you just want to sell them at a high price; but I'm telling you honestly that I won't buy them for more than a ruble and a half."

"Well, then, take them for two rubles."

"Alright, I'll give you two rubles," said Chichikov, and he thought to himself: "I can afford to give him two rubles."

"But I said two rubles and fifty kopecks," Nozdryov insisted. "I can't go lower than that."

"But we just agreed on two rubles."

"We didn't agree on anything. You said two rubles, I said two and a half; that's not an agreement."

"Then let's not talk about it anymore," said Chichikov, and he got up from the sofa. "I can see you don't want to sell them; good-bye."
