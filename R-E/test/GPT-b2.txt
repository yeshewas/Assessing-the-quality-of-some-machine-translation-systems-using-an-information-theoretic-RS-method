VI



     After learning from Kovrin that not only was the novel going well, but there would even be a wedding, Yegor Semenych paced back and forth for a long time, trying to hide his excitement. His hands began to tremble, his neck swelled and turned purple, he ordered the harness fitted to the carriage and rode off somewhere. Tanya, who saw how he whipped the horse and how deeply he pulled his cap down almost over his ears, understood his mood, locked herself away, and cried all day.



     In the greenhouses, the peaches and plums were already ripe; packing and sending this delicate and finicky cargo to Moscow required a lot of care, work, and trouble. Thanks to the very hot and dry summer, each tree had to be watered, which took a lot of time and labor, and there was an abundance of caterpillars that the workers and even Yegor Semenych and Tanya, to Kovrin’s great disgust, crushed with their fingers. Despite all this, orders for fruit and trees had to be taken for the fall, and extensive correspondence managed. And in the hottest time, when it seemed no one had a free minute, the fieldwork began, taking more than half of the garden’s workers; Yegor Semenych, deeply tanned, exhausted, and angry, galloped between the garden and the field, shouting that he was being torn apart and that he would shoot himself in the forehead.



     And then there was the fuss over the dowry, which the Pesotskys attached great importance to; from the clatter of scissors, the pounding of sewing machines, the steam from irons, and the whims of the milliner, a nervous and touchy lady, everyone in the house felt dizzy. And as if on purpose, guests arrived every day who had to be entertained, fed, and even put up overnight. But all this drudgery passed unnoticed, like in a fog. Tanya felt as if love and happiness had caught her off guard, although since she was fourteen, she had somehow been certain that Kovrin would marry her. She was amazed, puzzled, unable to believe herself… Sometimes such joy would flood over her that she wanted to fly up to the clouds and pray to God there, and then suddenly she would remember that in August she would have to leave her native nest and part from her father, or, God knows from where, the thought would come that she was insignificant, petty, and unworthy of such a great person as Kovrin – and she would retreat within herself, lock the door, and cry bitterly for hours. When guests were present, suddenly she would think Kovrin was extraordinarily handsome and that all the women were in love with him and envied her, and her soul would fill with rapture and pride as if she had conquered the whole world; but the moment he smiled kindly at some young lady, she would tremble with jealousy, withdraw to herself – and tears again. These new feelings completely possessed her; she helped her father mechanically and did not notice the peaches, nor the caterpillars, nor the workers, nor how quickly time flew.



     Almost the same thing was happening to Yegor Semenych. He worked from morning till night, was always rushing somewhere, losing his temper, getting irritated, but all this in some kind of magical half-sleep. It was as if two people lived inside him: one was the real Yegor Semenych who, listening to gardener Ivan Karlych report on the disorder, was upset and despairingly grabbed his head, and the other, not real, exactly half-drunk, who suddenly interrupted the business conversation halfway through, touched the gardener on the shoulder, and began to mumble:



     – No matter what you say, blood means a lot. His mother was an extraordinary, noblest, and smartest woman. It was a pleasure to look at her kind, clear, pure face, like an angel’s. She painted beautifully, wrote poetry, spoke five foreign languages, sang… Poor thing, may she rest in heaven, died of consumption.



     The unreal Yegor Semenych sighed and, after a pause, continued:



     – When he was a boy and growing up with me, he had just such an angelic face, clear and kind. His gaze, movements, and speech are gentle and graceful, like his mother’s. And mind? He always amazed us with his intellect. And mind you, it’s no accident he’s a master! No accident! But wait, Ivan Karlych, what he’ll be like in ten years! You won’t reach him by hand!



     But then the real Yegor Semenych, suddenly coming to his senses, would make a terrible face,He grabbed his head and shouted:

     – Devils! They have desecrated, defiled, and frozen over! The garden is lost! The garden is doomed!

     But Kovrin worked with the same diligence as before and did not notice the bustle. Love only poured fuel on the fire. After each date with Tanya, happy and ecstatic, he would go to his place and with the same passion with which he had just kissed Tanya and confessed his love to her, he would take up a book or his manuscript. What the black monk said about God's chosen ones, eternal truth, the brilliant future of humanity, etc., gave his work a special, extraordinary significance and filled his soul with pride, with the consciousness of his own height. Once or twice a week, in the park or at home, he met with the black monk and talked with him for a long time, but this did not frighten him; rather, it inspired him, since he was firmly convinced that such visions visit only chosen, outstanding people who have devoted themselves to serving an idea.

     One day the monk appeared during lunch and sat by the window in the dining room. Kovrin was delighted and very cleverly started a conversation with Egor Semenych and Tanya about what might interest the monk; the black guest listened and nodded amicably, and Egor Semenych and Tanya also listened and smiled cheerfully, unaware that Kovrin was not speaking to them but to his hallucination.

     Unnoticed, the Uspensky fast approached, and soon the wedding day, which, at Egor Semenych’s persistent wish, was celebrated “with a bang,” that is, with a senseless spree lasting two days. They ate and drank for about three thousand rubles, but due to the poor hired music, the loud toasts, the lackey’s scurrying, the noise, and the crowding, they did not appreciate either the expensive wines or the wonderful snacks ordered from Moscow.



VII



     Once, on one of the long winter nights, Kovrin was lying in bed reading a French novel. Poor Tanya, whose head hurt in the evenings from the unfamiliarity of living in the city, had long since fallen asleep and occasionally uttered some incoherent phrases in a delirium.

     It struck three o’clock. Kovrin put out the candle and lay down; he lay with his eyes closed for a long time but could not sleep because it seemed to him that the bedroom was very hot and Tanya was delirious. At half past four he lit the candle again, and at that moment he saw the black monk sitting in a chair near the bed.

     – Hello, – said the monk and, after a short silence, asked: – What are you thinking about now?

     – About fame, – answered Kovrin. – In the French novel I was just reading, there is a man, a young scientist, who does foolish things and wastes away with longing for fame. That longing is incomprehensible to me.

     – Because you are smart. You treat fame indifferently, like a toy that does not interest you.

     – Yes, that’s true.

     – Fame does not smile on you. What is flattering, amusing, or instructive about having your name carved on a tombstone only to have time erase the inscription along with the gold leaf? And luckily, there are too many of you for weak human memory to hold your names.

     – I understand, – Kovrin agreed. – And why remember them? But let’s talk about something else. For example, happiness. What is happiness?

     When the clock struck five, he was sitting on the bed with his legs dangling over the carpet and said, addressing the monk:

     – In ancient times, one happy man eventually became afraid of his happiness—it was so great!—and to appease the gods, sacrificed his beloved ring. You know that? And I, like Polycrates, am starting to feel a little uneasy about my happiness. It seems strange to me that from morning till night I experience only joy; it fills me entirely and drowns out all other feelings. I don’t know what sadness, grief, or boredom is. Here I am, unable to sleep, I have insomnia, but I am not bored. Seriously, I’m starting to be puzzled.

     – But why? – the monk exclaimed. – Isn’t joy a supernatural feeling? Shouldn’t it be a normal state for a person? The higher a person is in intellectual and moral development, the freer they are, the more pleasure life gives them. Socrates,Diogenes and Marcus Aurelius felt joy, not sorrow. And the apostle says: always rejoice. So rejoice and be happy.

     – What if the gods get angry? – Kovrin joked and laughed. – If they take away my comfort and make me shiver and starve, I hardly think I’ll like it.

     Meanwhile, Tanya woke up and looked at her husband in amazement and horror. He was talking, addressing the chair, gesturing and laughing: his eyes shone and there was something strange in his laughter.

     – Andryusha, who are you talking to? – she asked, grabbing his hand, which he had extended toward the monk. – Andryusha! Who?

     – Huh? Who? – Kovrin was confused. – With him… He’s sitting there, – he said, pointing at the black monk.

     – There’s no one here… no one! Andryusha, you’re sick!

     Tanya hugged her husband and pressed against him as if protecting him from visions, and covered his eyes with her hand.

     – You’re sick! – she cried, trembling all over. – Forgive me, dear, sweet, but I've long noticed that your soul is troubled by something… You’re mentally ill, Andryusha…

     Her trembling affected him too. He looked again at the chair, which was already empty, suddenly felt weakness in his arms and legs, got scared, and started dressing.

     – It’s nothing, Tanya, nothing… – he muttered, trembling. – In fact, I’m a little unwell… it’s time to admit it.

     – I’ve noticed for a long time… and Dad has noticed, – she said, trying to hold back her sobs. – You talk to yourself, smile strangely somehow… don’t sleep. Oh my God, my God, save us! – she said in horror. – But don’t be afraid, Andryusha, don’t be afraid, for God’s sake, don’t be afraid…

     She also started dressing. Only now, looking at her, Kovrin understood the full danger of his situation, understood what the black monk and conversations with him meant. It was now clear to him that he was insane.

     Both, not knowing why, got dressed and went to the hall: she ahead, he behind her. There, awakened by the sobbing, stood Yegor Semenych in a robe and holding a candle, who was visiting them.

     – Don’t be afraid, Andryusha, – Tanya said, trembling as if with a fever, – don’t be afraid… Dad, it will all pass… it will all pass…

     Kovrin could not speak because of excitement. He wanted to say to his father-in-law jokingly:

     – Congratulate me, I think I’ve gone mad, – but moved only his lips and smiled bitterly.

     At nine in the morning, they put a coat and a fur coat on him, wrapped him in a shawl, and took him by carriage to the doctor. He began treatment.



VIII



     Summer came again, and the doctor ordered him to go to the village. Kovrin had already recovered, stopped seeing the black monk, and only needed to strengthen his physical condition. Living at his father-in-law’s in the village, he drank a lot of milk, worked only two hours a day, did not drink wine or smoke.

     On the evening of Ilyin’s day, they held an all-night vigil at the house. When the church clerk handed the censer to the priest, the old huge hall smelled exactly like a cemetery, and Kovrin became bored. He went out to the garden. Not noticing the luxurious flowers, he walked in the garden, sat on a bench, then strolled through the park; reaching the river, he went down and stood in thought, looking at the water. The gloomy pines with shaggy roots, which last year had seen him so young, joyful, and cheerful, now did not whisper but stood still and silent, as if they didn’t recognize him. Indeed, his head was shaved, the long beautiful hair was gone, his gait was sluggish, and his face, compared to last summer, had grown fuller and paler.

     He crossed the benches to the other shore. There, where last year was rye, now lay rows of cut oats. The sun had already set, and a wide red glow blazed on the horizon, heralding windy weather tomorrow. It was quiet. Peering in the direction where the black monk appeared for the first time last year, Kovrin stood for about twenty minutes until the evening glow began to fade…

     When he, sluggish and dissatisfied, returned home, the all-night vigil was already over. Yegor Semenych and Tanya sat on the terrace steps drinking tea. They were talking about something but suddenly fell silent when they saw Kovrin, and he concluded from their faces that their conversation had been about him.

     – It seems to you,It’s time to drink milk, – Tanya said to her husband.

     – No, it’s not time yet… – he replied, sitting on the lowest step. – Drink it yourself. I don’t want to.

     Tanya glanced anxiously at her father and said in a guilty voice:

     – You yourself notice that milk is good for you.

     – Yes, very good! – Kovrin smirked. – Congratulations: since Friday, I’ve gained another pound. – He squeezed his head tightly with his hands and said sadly: – Why, why did you treat me? Bromide drugs, idleness, warm baths, supervision, cowardly fear of every sip, every step – all of this will eventually drive me to idiocy. I went crazy, I had a delusion of grandeur, but at least I was cheerful, energetic, and even happy, I was interesting and original. Now I’ve become more sensible and solid, but I’m just like everyone else: I’m mediocre, life is boring for me… Oh, how cruel you have been to me! I saw hallucinations, but who was bothered by it? I ask: who was bothered by it?

     – God knows what you’re saying! – sighed Egor Semenych. – Even listening to it is boring.

     – Then don’t listen.

     The presence of people, especially Egor Semenych, now irritated Kovrin, he answered him dryly, coldly and even rudely and looked at him only mockingly and with hatred, while Egor Semenych was embarrassed and coughed guiltily, although he felt no guilt. Not understanding why their kind, good-natured relationships had changed so sharply, Tanya clung to her father and looked anxiously into his eyes; she wanted to understand but couldn’t, and the only thing clear to her was that the relations were getting worse every day, that her father had aged significantly lately, and her husband had become irritable, capricious, picky, and uninteresting. She could no longer laugh or sing, ate nothing at dinner, didn’t sleep for whole nights, expecting something terrible, and got so exhausted that one day she lay fainted from lunch till evening. During the all-night service, she thought her father cried, and now, when the three of them sat on the terrace, she made an effort not to think about it.

     – How happy Buddha and Mohammed or Shakespeare must be that kind relatives and doctors did not treat them for ecstasy and inspiration! – said Kovrin. – If Mohammed had taken potassium bromide for nerves, worked only two hours a day and drank milk, then as much of this wonderful man would have remained as of his dog. Doctors and kind relatives will eventually make humanity stupid, mediocrity will be considered genius, and civilization will perish. If you only knew, – Kovrin said with annoyance, – how grateful I am to you!

     He felt strong irritation and, to avoid saying too much, quickly got up and went into the house. It was quiet, and the scent of tobacco and jalap from the garden drifted through the open windows. In the huge dark hall, on the floor and on the piano, moonlight lay in green patches. Kovrin remembered the delights of last summer when the jalap smelled the same and the moon shone through the windows. To bring back last year’s mood, he quickly went to his study, lit a strong cigar and ordered the footman to bring wine. But the cigar made his mouth bitter and unpleasant, and the wine was not as tasty as last year. And what does it mean to lose the habit! After a cigar and two sips of wine, his head spun and palpitations began, so he needed to take potassium bromide.

     Before going to bed, Tanya said to him:

     – Father adores you. You’re angry at him for something, and it’s killing him. Look: he’s aging by the hour, not by the day. I beg you, Andryusha, for God’s sake, for the sake of your late father, for my peace, be kind to him!

     – I can’t and don’t want to.

     – But why? – Tanya asked, beginning to shake all over. – Explain to me, why?

     – Because I don’t like him, that’s all, – Kovrin said casually and shrugged, – but let’s not talk about him: he’s your father.

     – I can’t, I can’t understand! – Tanya said, pressing her temples and staring at one point. – Something incomprehensible, terrible is happening in our house. You’ve changed, you don’t look like yourself anymore… You, smart,An extraordinary person, you get annoyed over trifles, you interfere in quarrels... Such little things trouble you so much that sometimes you just marvel and can’t believe: is this really you? Well, well, don’t be angry, don’t be angry, – she continued, frightened by her own words and kissing his hands. – You are smart, kind, noble. You will be fair to your father. He is so kind!

     – He is not kind, but good-natured. Vaudeville uncles, like your father, with their well-fed good-natured faces, are extraordinarily hospitable and eccentric. They used to delight and amuse me in stories, in vaudevilles, and in real life, but now they disgust me. They are egoists to the core. What I find most repulsive is their satiety and this gastric, purely bovine or boar-like optimism.

     Tanya sat on the bed and laid her head on the pillow.

     – It’s torture, – she said, and from her voice, it was clear she was extremely tired and found it hard to speak. – Not a single quiet minute since winter... This is terrible, my God! I suffer…

     – Yes, of course, I’m Herod, and you and your daddy are Egyptian infants. Of course!

     Tanya found his face ugly and unpleasant. Hatred and a mocking expression did not suit him. Even before, she noticed that something was missing from his face, as if since he got his hair cut, his face had changed. She wanted to say something hurtful to him but immediately caught herself in the feeling of dislike, got scared, and left the bedroom.

IX

     Kovrin received an independent chair. The introductory lecture was scheduled for December 2, and an announcement about this was posted in the university corridor. But on the appointed day, he informed the student inspector by telegram that he would not give lectures due to illness.

     He had blood coming from his throat. He spat blood, but about twice a month it flowed heavily, and then he became extremely weak and would fall into a drowsy state. This illness did not especially frighten him because he knew that his late mother had lived with exactly the same disease for ten years or more; and doctors assured him it was not dangerous and advised only not to worry, to live a proper life, and speak less.

     In January, the lecture again did not take place for the same reason, and by February it was already too late to start the course. It had to be postponed until the next year.

     He no longer lived with Tanya but with another woman who was two years older than him and took care of him like a child. His mood was peaceful, submissive: he willingly obeyed, and when Varvara Nikolaevna – that was his companion’s name – was about to take him to Crimea, he agreed, although he sensed that nothing good would come of this trip.

     They arrived in Sevastopol in the evening and stayed at a hotel to rest and travel to Yalta the next day. The journey had tired them both. Varvara Nikolaevna drank tea, went to bed, and soon fell asleep. But Kovrin did not lie down. Even at home, an hour before leaving for the station, he had received a letter from Tanya and did not dare to open it. Now it lay in his side pocket, and the thought of it disturbed him unpleasantly. Sincerely, deep down, he now considered his marriage to Tanya a mistake, was glad he had finally separated from her, and the memory of this woman, who in the end had turned into walking living relics and in whom, it seems, everything was already dead except for the large, intensely staring, intelligent eyes, stirred only pity and annoyance with himself. The handwriting on the envelope reminded him how unfair and cruel he had been about two years ago, how he took out on innocent people his spiritual emptiness, boredom, loneliness, and dissatisfaction with life. At the same time, he recalled how once he tore his dissertation to pieces and all the articles written during his illness and threw them out the window, and the little scraps, flying in the wind, got caught on trees and flowers; in every line he saw strange, unfounded claims, frivolous ardor, audacity, megalomania, and this made an impression on him as if he were reading a description of his own vices.but when the last notebook was torn apart and flew out the window, for some reason he suddenly felt annoyed and bitter, he went to his wife and said

VOLODYA

One summer Sunday, around five in the evening, Volodya, a seventeen-year-old boy, unattractive, sickly, and shy, was sitting in a gazebo at the Shumikhin's country house and feeling bored. His gloomy thoughts ran in three directions. First, tomorrow, Monday, he had to take a math exam; he knew that if he failed to solve the written problem tomorrow, he would be expelled, since he had been in the sixth grade for two years and had an annual grade of 2 3/4 in algebra. Second, his stay with the Shumikhins, wealthy people aspiring to aristocracy, constantly hurt his pride. It seemed to him that Madame Shumikhina and her nieces looked at him and his maman as poor relatives and hangers-on, that they did not respect maman and laughed at her. Once, he accidentally overheard Madame Shumikhina telling her cousin Anna Fyodorovna on the terrace that his maman was still trying to act young and make herself beautiful, that she never paid her losses and had a fondness for other people's boots and tobacco. Every day Volodya begged maman not to go to the Shumikhins, described to her the humiliating role she played among these people, persuaded her, spoke boldly, but she, frivolous, spoiled, having lived through two states in her life—her own and her husband's—always drawn to high society, did not understand him, and Volodya had to accompany her to the hated country house twice a week.

Third, the young man could not shake off a strange, unpleasant feeling that was completely new to him... It seemed to him that he was in love with the Shumikhins' cousin and guest, Anna Fyodorovna. She was a lively, loud-voiced, and laugh-prone lady, about thirty years old, healthy, sturdy, rosy-cheeked, with rounded shoulders, a round fat chin, and a constant smile on her thin lips. She was neither beautiful nor young—Volodya knew this well—but for some reason, he could not stop thinking about her or looking at her when she, playing croquet, shrugged her round shoulders and moved her smooth back, or after long laughter and running up and down the stairs, would fall into a chair and, squeezing her eyes shut, breathing heavily, pretended her chest felt tight and stuffy. She was married. Her husband, a respectable architect, came to the country house once a week, got plenty of rest, and returned to the city. This strange feeling in Volodya began when he irrationally hated this architect and was glad every time he left for the city.

Now, sitting in the gazebo thinking about tomorrow’s exam and about maman, who was being laughed at, he felt a strong desire to see Nyuta (as the Shumikhins called Anna Fyodorovna), to hear her laughter, the rustle of her dress... This desire was not like the pure, poetic love he knew from novels and dreamed about every evening before going to sleep; it was strange, incomprehensible, he was ashamed of it and afraid of it as something very bad and unclean, something hard to admit even to himself...

“This is not love,” he told himself. “You don’t fall in love with thirty-year-olds and married women... It’s just a little fling... Yes, a fling...”

Thinking of a fling, he remembered his invincible shyness, the lack of a mustache, freckles, narrow eyes, imagined himself next to Nyuta—and this pair seemed impossible to him; then he hurried to imagine himself handsome, brave, witty, dressed in the latest fashion...

In the middle of his daydreams, hunched over and looking at the ground, sitting in the dark corner of the gazebo, light footsteps were heard. Someone was walking slowly along the alley. Soon the footsteps faded, and something white flashed at the entrance.

“Is anyone here?” asked a female voice.

Volodya recognized this voice and lifted his head in fright.

“Who’s there?” Nyuta asked, entering the gazebo. “Ah, it’s you, Volodya? What are you doing here? Thinking? And how can you think, think, think like that... it can drive you crazy!”

Volodya got up and looked at Nyuta awkwardly. She had just returned from the bathhouse. A sheet and a fluffy towel hung over her shoulders,Wet hair stuck to her forehead peeked out from under the white silk scarf on her head. She gave off a moist, cool scent of the bathhouse and almond soap. She was out of breath from walking quickly. The top button of her blouse was undone, so the young man could see both her neck and chest.

     "Why are you silent?" Nyuta asked, glancing at Volodya. "It's rude to be silent when a lady is speaking to you. What a seal you are, Volodya! You just sit there, silent, thinking like some philosopher. You have no life or fire in you at all! Honestly, you're unpleasant... At your age, you should be living, jumping, chatting, courting women, falling in love.

     Volodya looked at the sheet supported by a white, plump hand and thought...

     "Silent!" Nyuta was surprised. "That's even strange... Listen, be a man! At least smile! Ugh, unpleasant philosopher!" she laughed. "And you know, Volodya, why you're such a seal? Because you don't court women. Why don’t you court women? True, there are no young ladies here, but nothing stops you from courting ladies! Why don’t you, for example, court me?"

     Volodya listened and scratched his temple in heavy, tense thought.

     "Only very proud people stay silent and love solitude," Nyuta continued, pulling his hand away from his temple. "You're proud, Volodya. Why do you look askance? Look me straight in the face! Come on now, seal!"

     Volodya decided to speak. Wanting to smile, he twitched his lower lip, blinked, and again reached for his temple.

     "I... I love you!" he said.

     Nyuta raised her eyebrows in surprise and laughed.

     "What am I hearing?!" she sang, like opera singers do when they hear something terrible. "What? What did you say? Repeat, repeat..."

     "I... I love you!" Volodya repeated.

     And without any control of his own will, understanding nothing, he took half a step toward Nyuta and took her hand above the wrist. His eyes blurred, tears welled up, and the whole world turned into one big, hairy towel that smelled like a bathhouse.

     "Bravo, bravo!" he heard cheerful laughter. "Why are you silent? I want you to speak! Well?"

     Seeing he was allowed to keep holding her hand, Volodya looked at Nyuta’s laughing face and awkwardly, uncomfortably took her by the waist with both hands, his palms meeting on her back. He held her waist with both hands, and she, throwing her hands behind her head and showing dimples on her elbows, adjusted her hairstyle under the scarf and spoke in a calm voice:

     "You need to be skillful, kind, charming, Volodya, and you can only become such under the influence of female company. However, you have such an unpleasant... angry face. You need to speak, laugh... Yes, Volodya, don’t be a grump, you’re young and you still have time to philosophize. Now, let me go, I’m leaving. Let me go!"

     She effortlessly freed her waist and, humming something, left the gazebo. Volodya was left alone. He smoothed his hair, smiled, and paced back and forth three times, then sat down on the bench and smiled again. He felt unbearably ashamed, so much so that even he was surprised that human shame could reach such sharpness and power. He smiled from shame, whispered some incoherent words, and gesticulated.

     He was ashamed that he had just been treated like a boy, ashamed of his timidity, and most of all, ashamed that he dared to take a respectable married woman by the waist even though, by age, appearance, and social standing, it seemed to him that he had no right to do so.

     He jumped up, left the gazebo, and, without looking back, walked deep into the garden away from the house.

     "Ah, I wish I could leave here soon!" he thought, grabbing his head. "God, I wish it were soon!"

     The train Volodya was supposed to take with maman left at 8:40. There were about three hours until the train, but he would have happily gone to the station right away without waiting for maman.

     At the eighth hour, he was approaching the house. His whole figure showed determination: whatever will be, will be! He had decided to enter boldly, looking straight ahead,to speak loudly, no matter what.

He passed the terrace, the large hall, the living room, and stopped in the last one to catch his breath. From there, he could hear tea being drunk in the neighboring dining room. M-me Shumikhina, maman, and Nyuta were talking and laughing about something.

Volodya listened closely.

"I assure you!" Nyuta was saying. "I couldn't believe my own eyes! When he started confessing his love to me and even, imagine, took me by the waist, I didn't recognize him. And you know, he has a way about him! When he said he was in love with me, there was something wild on his face, like a Circassian."

"Really!" gasped maman, bursting into a prolonged laugh. "Really! How he reminds me of my father!"

Volodya ran back and rushed out into the fresh air. "And how can they talk about this out loud!" he agonized, flinging his arms and looking at the sky in horror. "They say it out loud, calmly... And maman laughed... maman! My God, why did you give me such a mother? Why?"

But he had to go back inside, no matter what. He paced the alley three times, calmed down a little, and entered the house.

"Why don't you come on time to have tea?" M-me Shumikhina asked strictly.

"My fault, I... I have to go," he mumbled, not raising his eyes. "Maman, it’s already eight o'clock!"

"Go yourself, my dear," maman said languidly, "I’m staying overnight at Lily's. Goodbye, my friend... Let me cross you..."

She made the sign of the cross over her son and said in French, addressing Nyuta:

"He looks a bit like Lermontov, doesn’t he?"

After a hasty goodbye without looking at anyone’s face, Volodya left the dining room. Ten minutes later, he was already walking along the road to the station and was glad about it. Now he felt neither fear nor shame; breathing was easy and free.

Half a verst from the station, he sat on a stone by the road and stared at the sun, which was more than halfway hidden behind the embankment. Lights had already turned on here and there at the station, a faint green light twinkled, but the train was not yet in sight. Volodya enjoyed sitting still, listening to the gradual onset of evening. The twilight of the gazebo, footsteps, the smell of the bathhouse, laughter, and the waist—all this appeared with astonishing clarity in his imagination, and none of it was as scary or significant as before...

"Trifles... She didn’t pull her hand away and laughed when I held her by the waist," he thought, "so she liked it. If she had disliked it, she would have gotten angry..."

And now Volodya felt annoyed that he hadn't been brave enough in the gazebo. He regretted leaving so foolishly, and he was sure that if the situation happened again, he would be braver and see things more simply.

And the situation was easy to repeat. The Shumikhin family often took long walks after dinner. If Volodya walked with Nyuta through the dark garden, there—it would be the chance!

"I’ll come back," he thought, "and leave tomorrow on the morning train... I’ll say I missed the train."

And he returned... M-me Shumikhina, maman, Nyuta, and one of the nieces were sitting on the terrace playing whist. When Volodya lied that he missed the train, they worried that he might miss his exam tomorrow and advised him to get up early. The whole time they played cards, he sat aside, eagerly watching Nyuta and waiting... In his head, a plan was already forming: he would approach Nyuta in the dark, take her hand, then hug her; no need to talk because both would understand everything without words.

But after dinner, the ladies did not go for a walk in the garden and kept playing cards. They played until one in the morning and then went to bed.

"How silly all this is!" Volodya grumbled as he lay down. "But never mind, I’ll wait for tomorrow... Tomorrow again in the gazebo. Nothing..."

He didn’t try to fall asleep but sat in bed hugging his knees and thinking. The thought of the exam repelled him. He had already decided that he would be expelled and that there was nothing terrible about it. On the contrary, it was very good, even very good. Tomorrow he would be free like a bird, would wear a particular outfit, smoke openly, come here, and court Nyuta.whenever; and he would no longer be a gymnasium student, but a "young man." And the rest, what they call career and future, was clear: Volodya would enter the volunteers, then become a telegraph operator, finally work in a pharmacy, where he would advance to pharmacist... there are many positions, after all. An hour or two passed, and he kept sitting and thinking...

At the third hour, when dawn was breaking, the door creaked cautiously and maman entered the room.

"Aren't you asleep?" she asked, yawning. "Sleep, sleep, I'll be just a minute... I just need to take the drops..."

"Why do you need them?"

"Poor Lily has spasms again. Sleep, my child, you have an exam tomorrow..."

She took from the cabinet a bottle with something, went to the window, read the label, and left.

"Marya Leontyevna, those are not the drops!" Volodya heard a female voice a minute later. "That's lily of the valley, but Lily is asking for morphine. Is your son asleep? Ask him to find it..."

It was Nyuta's voice. Volodya felt a chill. He quickly put on his pants, threw a coat over his shoulders, and went to the door.

"Do you understand? Morphine!" Nyuta whispered, explaining. "It should say in Latin. Wake Volodya, he will find it..."

Maman opened the door, and Volodya saw Nyuta. She was wearing the same blouse she had worn to swim. Her hair was uncombed, scattered over her shoulders, her face sleepy and dark from the twilight...

"Look, Volodya is not asleep..." she said. "Volodya, please look, dear, for morphine in the cabinet! Punishment with this Lily... she always has something."

Maman mumbled something, yawned, and left.

"Search then," Nyuta said. "What are you standing for?"

Volodya went to the cabinet, knelt down, and began sorting through bottles and boxes of medicine. His hands trembled, and in his chest and stomach he felt as if cold waves were running through all his insides. The smell of ether, carbolic acid, and various herbs, which he grabbed with trembling hands without any need and which crumbled because of this, made him feel suffocated and dizzy.

"It seems maman has left," he thought. "That's good... good..."

"Soon?" Nyuta asked drawlingly.

"Now... This seems to be morphine..." Volodya said, having read the word "morph..." on one of the labels. "Here you go!"

Nyuta stood in the doorway so that one foot was in the hallway and the other in his room. She was fixing her hair, which was hard to fix because it was so thick and long! — and she gazed absentmindedly at Volodya. In the loose blouse, sleepy, with loose hair, in that faint light coming into the room from the white but not yet sunlit sky, she seemed enchanting, luxurious to Volodya... Enchanted, trembling all over and recalling with pleasure how he had embraced this wonderful body in the gazebo, he handed her the drops and said:

"You are so..."

"What?"

She entered the room.

"What?" she asked, smiling.

He was silent and looked at her, then, like back in the gazebo, took her hand... And she looked at him, smiled, and waited: what would happen next?

"I love you..." he whispered.

She stopped smiling, thought for a moment, and said:

"Wait, I think someone is coming. Oh, these gymnasium students of mine!" she said quietly, going to the door and peeking into the hallway. "No, no one is visible..."

She returned...

Then Volodya felt that the room, Nyuta, the dawn, and himself — all merged into one sensation of sharp, extraordinary, unprecedented happiness, for which one could give their whole life and endure eternal torment, but half a minute later, it all suddenly disappeared. Volodya saw only a full, ugly face distorted with disgust, and he suddenly felt revulsion at what had happened.

"However, I need to leave," Nyuta said, looking at Volodya with disgust. "What an ugly, pathetic... ugh, nasty duckling!"

How ugly her long hair, loose blouse, her steps, her voice now seemed to Volodya!

"Ugly duckling..." he thought after she left. "Indeed, I am ugly... Everything is ugly."

Outside, the sun was already rising, birds were singing loudly; it was possible to hear,how the gardener walked in the garden and how his wheelbarrow creaked... And a little later the mooing of cows and the sounds of a shepherd’s pipe were heard. The sunlight and the sounds spoke of a life somewhere in this world that was pure, graceful, poetic. But where was it? Neither maman nor all the people around Volodya ever spoke about it.

     When the footman woke him for the morning train, he pretended to be asleep...

     “To hell with it all!” he thought.

     He got out of bed at eleven o’clock. Combing his hair in front of the mirror and looking at his ugly, pale face from a sleepless night, he thought:

     “Exactly... Ugly duckling.”

     When maman saw him and was horrified that he wasn’t at the exam, Volodya said:

     - I overslept, maman... But don’t worry, I will provide a medical certificate.

     M-me Shumikhina and Nyuta woke up at one o’clock. Volodya heard M-me Shumikhina waking and opening her window with a clatter, and Nyuta answered her harsh voice with a booming laugh. He saw the door open and a line of nieces and boarders come from the living room to breakfast (maman was among the latter), he saw Nyuta’s washed and laughing face flashing by, and next to her face the black eyebrows and beard of the architect who had just arrived.

     Nyuta wore a Little Russian costume, which didn’t suit her at all and made her clumsy; the architect joked crudely and flatly; the cutlets served for breakfast had way too much onion – so it seemed to Volodya. He also thought that Nyuta deliberately laughed loudly and glanced toward him to show him that she wasn’t bothered by the night’s memory at all and that she didn’t notice the presence of the ugly duckling at the table.

     At four o’clock Volodya was going with maman to the station. Dirty memories, a sleepless night, impending expulsion from gymnasium, pangs of conscience – all this now stirred a heavy, gloomy anger in him. He looked at maman’s thin profile, at her little nose, at the waterproof coat Nyuta had given her, and muttered:

     - Why do you put on powder? It’s not fitting for your age! You try to make yourself beautiful, don’t pay the price, smoke someone else’s tobacco... disgusting! I don’t love you... I don’t love you!

     He insulted her, and she nervously moved her eyes, fluttered her hands, and whispered in horror:

     - What are you saying, my friend? My God, the coachman will hear! Be quiet, or the coachman will hear! He hears everything!

     - I don’t love you... I don’t love you! – he continued, gasping. – You’re immoral, soulless... Don’t you dare wear that waterproof! Hear me? Or I’ll tear it to shreds...

     - Come to your senses, my child! – maman cried. – The coachman will hear!

     - And where is my father’s fortune? Where is your money? You’ve wasted everything! I’m not ashamed of my poverty, but I’m ashamed to have such a mother... When my comrades ask about you, I always blush.

     They had to travel two stations by train to the city. All the time Volodya stood on the platform and trembled all over. He didn’t want to enter the car because his mother, whom he hated, was sitting there. He hated himself, the conductors, the steam from the locomotive, the cold, which he blamed for his shivering... The heavier his heart felt, the more he sensed that somewhere in this world, among some people, there was a pure, noble, warm, elegant life full of love, affection, fun, freedom... He felt it and longed so much that even one passenger, looking intently at his face, asked:

     - Probably your teeth hurt?

     In the city, maman and Volodya lived with Marya Petrovna, a noblewoman who rented a large apartment and sublet it to tenants. Maman rented two rooms: in one, with windows, where her bed stood and two paintings in golden frames hung on the walls, she lived herself, and in the other, adjoining, small and dark, lived Volodya. There was a sofa on which he slept, and besides that sofa, there was no other furniture; the whole room was filled with wicker baskets with dresses, hat boxes, and all kinds of clutter that maman kept for some reason.Volodya prepared the lessons in his mother's room or in the "common room" – that’s what the large room was called, where all the residents gathered during lunch and in the evenings.
Ionych



I



When visitors in the provincial town of S. complained about boredom and the monotony of life, the locals, almost in justification, said that on the contrary, S. was very good, that S. had a library, a theater, a club, there were balls, and finally, there were intelligent, interesting, pleasant families with whom one could make acquaintances. And they pointed to the Turkin family as the most educated and talented.



This family lived on the main street, near the governor’s residence, in their own house. Turkin himself, Ivan Petrovich, a stocky, handsome brunette with sideburns, organized amateur performances for charitable purposes, played old generals himself, and at the same time coughed very amusingly. He knew many anecdotes, charades, sayings, loved to joke and be witty, and he always had such an expression that it was impossible to tell whether he was joking or speaking seriously. His wife, Vera Iosifovna, a slender, pretty lady with pince-nez, wrote stories and novels and gladly read them aloud to their guests. Their daughter, Ekaterina Ivanovna, a young woman, played the piano. In short, each family member had some kind of talent. The Turkins greeted guests warmly and showed off their talents cheerfully, with heartfelt simplicity. Their large stone house was spacious and cool in the summer; half the windows looked out onto an old shady garden where nightingales sang in spring; when guests were sitting in the house, knives clattered in the kitchen, and the yard smelled of fried onions – and this always heralded a plentiful and delicious dinner.



And to Doctor Startsev, Dmitry Ionich, when he was just appointed as the rural doctor and settled in Dyalyzh, nine versts from S., people also said that, as an intelligent person, he needed to get acquainted with the Turkins. One winter day, on the street, he was introduced to Ivan Petrovich; they talked about the weather, theater, cholera, and then an invitation followed. In spring, on the holiday – it was Ascension – after seeing patients, Startsev went to the town to have a little fun and happen to buy something for himself. He walked leisurely (he didn’t have his own horses yet) and kept humming: When I had not yet drunk the tears from the cup of existence...



In the town, he had lunch, strolled in the garden, then somehow the invitation from Ivan Petrovich came to mind, and he decided to visit the Turkins to see what kind of people they were.



“Hello, please,” said Ivan Petrovich, greeting him on the porch. “I am very, very glad to see such a pleasant guest. Come, I’ll introduce you to my better half. I tell him, Verochka,” he continued, introducing the doctor to his wife, “I tell him he has no Roman right to sit in his hospital; he must give his leisure to society. Isn't that right, dear?”



“Sit here,” said Vera Iosifovna, seating the guest beside her. “You can take care of me. My husband is jealous, he’s Othello, but we shall try to behave so he notices nothing.”



“Oh, you little minx, a flirt…” Ivan Petrovich murmured tenderly and kissed her on the forehead. “You came at just the right time,” he said to the guest again, “my better half has written a majority novel and will read it aloud today.”



“Zhanchik,” said Vera Iosifovna to her husband, “dites que l'on nous donne du thé1.”



Startsev was introduced to Ekaterina Ivanovna, an eighteen-year-old girl who looked very much like her mother, just as slender and pretty. Her expression was still childlike, and her waist was thin and delicate; and her virginal, already developed breast, beautiful and healthy, spoke of spring, true spring. Then they drank tea with jam, honey, candies, and very delicious cookies that melted in the mouth. With the onset of evening, guests gradually arrived, and at each one Ivan Petrovich turned his laughing eyes and said:



“Hello, please.”



Then everyone sat in the parlor, with very serious faces, and Vera Iosifovna read her novel. She began like this: “The frost was growing stronger...” The windows were wide open, one could hear knives clattering in the kitchen, and the smell of fried onions drifted in... In soft, deep armchairs, there was calm, the lights flickered so gently in the dusk of the parlor; and now,On a summer evening, when voices and laughter drifted in from the street and the scent of lilac came from the yard, it was hard to understand how the frost was strengthening and how the setting sun illuminated the snowy plain and the traveler walking alone along the road with its cold rays. Vera Iosifovna was reading about how a young, beautiful countess was setting up schools, hospitals, and libraries in her village and how she fell in love with a wandering artist – she was reading about things that never happen in real life, and yet it was pleasant and comfortable to listen, and good, calm thoughts came to mind – there was no desire to get up.

"Not bad..." Ivan Petrovich said quietly.

And one of the guests, listening and lost in thought somewhere very, very far away, said barely audibly:

"Yes... indeed..."

An hour passed, then another. In the city garden nearby, the orchestra played and a choir sang the singers’ songs. When Vera Iosifovna closed her notebook, they were silent for about five minutes, listening to "Luchinushka," sung by the choir, and this song conveyed what was not in the novel but happens in real life.

"Do you publish your works in journals?" Starcev asked Vera Iosifovna.

"No," she replied, "I don’t publish anywhere. I write and hide them in my cupboard. Why publish?" she explained. "After all, we have the means."

And for some reason, everyone sighed.

"Now you, Kotik, play something," Ivan Petrovich said to his daughter.

They lifted the piano lid, opened the sheet music that was already ready. Ekaterina Ivanovna sat down and struck the keys with both hands; and then immediately struck them again with all her might, and again, and again; her shoulders and chest shuddered, she stubbornly hit the same place over and over, and it seemed she wouldn't stop until she drove the key into the piano. The living room filled with thunder; everything thundered: the floor, the ceiling, the furniture... Ekaterina Ivanovna was playing a difficult passage, interesting precisely because of its difficulty, long and monotonous, and Starcev, listening, imagined stones falling from a high mountain, falling and falling, and he wished they would stop falling sooner, and at the same time Ekaterina Ivanovna, pink from tension, strong, energetic, with a curl falling on her forehead, pleased him greatly. After the winter spent in Dyalizh among the sick and peasants, sitting in the living room, looking at this young, graceful, and probably pure creature and listening to these noisy, annoying, but nevertheless cultured sounds – it was so pleasant, so new...

"Well, Kotik, today you played like never before," Ivan Petrovich said with tears in his eyes when his daughter finished and stood up. "Die, Denis, you couldn’t have written better."

Everyone surrounded her, congratulated her, marveled, assured that they hadn’t heard such music in a long time, and she listened silently, slightly smiling, and her whole figure expressed triumph.

"Wonderful! Excellent!"

"Wonderful!" Starcev said, caught up in the general enthusiasm. "Where did you study music?" he asked Ekaterina Ivanovna. "At the conservatory?"

"No, I am only planning to go to the conservatory, but for now, I studied here with Madame Zavlovskaya."

"Did you finish the course at the local gymnasium?"

"Oh no!" Vera Iosifovna answered for her. "We invited teachers to our home; at the gymnasium or institute, you see, there could be bad influences; while a girl is growing up, she should be under the influence of only her mother."

"But still, I will go to the conservatory," Ekaterina Ivanovna said.

"No, Kotik loves her mother. Kotik won’t upset mom and dad."

"No, I will go! I will go!" Ekaterina Ivanovna said, joking and being capricious, and stomped her foot.

And during dinner, Ivan Petrovich was already showing his talents. He told anecdotes laughing only with his eyes, joked, proposed funny problems and solved them himself, and all the while spoke in his unusual language, developed through long practice in wit and obviously a long-standing habit: majority-style, pretty good, twisted you thank you...

But that was not all. When the guests, full and satisfied, crowded in the hall, taking off their coats and canes, footman Pavlusha bustled around them, or,what was his name here, Pava, a boy of about fourteen, crew cut, with full cheeks.

     – Come on, Pava, show us! – said Ivan Petrovich.

     Pava struck a pose, raised his hand, and spoke in a tragic tone:

     – Die, unfortunate one!

     And everyone laughed.

     “Interesting,” thought Startsev as he stepped outside.

     He went into a restaurant and had a beer, then walked back to his place in Dyalyzh. He sang all the way: Your voice for me is both tender and languid…



     After walking nine versts and then going to bed, he did not feel the slightest fatigue; on the contrary, it seemed to him that he would gladly walk another twenty versts.

     “Not bad…” – he recalled, falling asleep, and smiled.



II



     Startsev kept planning to visit the Turkins, but there was a lot of work at the hospital, and he could never find a free moment. More than a year passed like this in work and solitude; but then a letter arrived from the city in a blue envelope…

     Vera Iosifovna had suffered from migraines for a long time, but recently, when Kotik threatened daily to leave for the conservatory, the attacks became more frequent. All the city doctors had visited the Turkins; finally, it was the zemstvo doctor’s turn. Vera Iosifovna wrote him a touching letter, asking him to come and ease her suffering. Startsev came, and after that, he began visiting the Turkins often, very often… He really helped Vera Iosifovna a little, and she already told all the guests that he was an extraordinary, amazing doctor. But he no longer visited the Turkins just for her migraines…

     A festive day. Ekaterina Ivanovna had finished her long, tiring exercises on the piano. Then they sat for a long time in the dining room drinking tea, and Ivan Petrovich was telling something funny. But then there was a bell; someone had to go to the hallway to meet a guest; Startsev took advantage of the moment of confusion and whispered to Ekaterina Ivanovna, very nervous:

     – For God’s sake, I beg you, don’t torment me, let’s go to the garden!

     She shrugged, as if puzzled and not understanding what he wanted from her, but she stood up and went.

     – You play the piano for three, four hours, – he said, following her, – then you sit with your mother, and there’s no chance to talk to you. Give me at least fifteen minutes, I beg you.

     Autumn was approaching, and the old garden was quiet, sad, and dark leaves lay on the alleys. It was already getting dark early.

     – I haven’t seen you for a whole week, – Startsev continued, – and if you knew what a suffering it is! Let’s sit down. Listen to me.

     They both had a favorite spot in the garden: a bench under an old wide maple. They sat on that bench now.

     – What do you want? – asked Ekaterina Ivanovna dryly, in a businesslike tone.

     – I haven’t seen you for a whole week, I haven’t heard you for so long. I passionately want, I crave your voice. Speak.

     She fascinated him with her freshness, the naive expression of her eyes and cheeks. Even in how the dress fit her, he saw something unusually sweet, touching in its simplicity and naive grace. And at the same time, despite this naivety, she seemed very smart and mature for her age. With her, he could talk about literature, art, anything; he could complain to her about life, about people, although during serious conversations, it sometimes happened that she suddenly laughed at an inappropriate moment or ran into the house. She, like most girls from S–ia, read a lot (in general, in S., very few people read, and the local library said that if it weren’t for the girls and the young Jews, they might as well close the library); this endlessly pleased Startsev, who asked her with excitement every time what she had read recently, and listened with enchantment when she told him.

     – What did you read this week while we didn’t see each other? – he asked now. – Please tell me.

     – I read Pisemsky.

     – What exactly?

     – “The Thousand Souls,” – Kotik answered. – And how funny Pisemsky’s name sounded: Alexey Feofilaktych!

     – Where are you going? – Startsev was alarmed when she suddenly stood up and headed to the house. – I must talk to you,I have to explain… Stay with me for at least five minutes! I beg you!

She stopped, as if wanting to say something, then awkwardly slipped a note into his hand and ran into the house, where she sat down at the piano again.

“Tonight, at eleven o’clock, – read Startsev, – be at the cemetery near the Demetti monument.”

“Well, that’s really not smart, – he thought, coming to his senses. – What’s the cemetery got to do with it? For what?”

It was clear: Kotik was joking. Who, really, would seriously think of setting up a meeting at night, far outside the city, at a cemetery, when it could easily be arranged on the street, in the city garden? And is it fitting for him, a zemstvo doctor, a smart, respectable person, to sigh, receive notes, wander around cemeteries, do foolish things that even gymnasium students now laugh at? Where would this romance lead? What will his comrades say when they find out? This was Startsev’s thought as he wandered around the club near the tables, and at half past ten he suddenly decided to go to the cemetery.

He already had his own pair of horses and coachman Panteleimon in a velvet vest. The moon was shining. It was quiet, warm, but warm in an autumn way. In the suburbs, near the slaughterhouses, dogs were howling. Startsev left the horses at the edge of the city, in one of the alleys, and went to the cemetery on foot. “Everyone has their own oddities, – he thought. – Kotik is strange too and – who knows? – maybe she’s not joking, she might come,” – and he surrendered to this weak, empty hope, and it intoxicated him.

He walked half a verst through the field. The cemetery appeared in the distance as a dark stripe, like a forest or a large garden. A white stone fence appeared, a gate… In the moonlight, the gate bore the inscription: “The hour is coming in…” Startsev entered the gate, and the first thing he saw was white crosses and monuments on both sides of a wide alley and black shadows from them and the poplars; all around, far off, one could see white and black, and the sleepy trees bent their branches over the white. It seemed that it was lighter here than in the field; maple leaves, resembling paws, stood out sharply against the yellow sand of the alleys and on the slabs, and the inscriptions on the monuments were clear. At first, Startsev was struck by what he now saw for the first time in his life and what, probably, he would never see again: a world unlike any other – a world where the moonlight is so good and soft, as if here was its cradle, where there is no life, no yes, no, but in every dark poplar, in every grave, the presence of a mystery is felt, promising a quiet, beautiful, eternal life. From the slabs and fading flowers, along with the autumn scent of leaves, came a sense of forgiveness, sadness, and peace.

Silence was everywhere; the stars looked down from the sky in deep reverence, and Startsev’s footsteps sounded so sharp and out of place. And only when the church began to strike the hour and he imagined himself dead, buried here forever, did it seem to him that someone was looking at him, and for a moment he thought it was not calm and silence, but the dull sorrow of nonexistence, depressed despair…

The Demetti monument was shaped like a chapel, with an angel on top; once an Italian opera troupe passed through S., one of the singers died, she was buried and this monument was erected. No one in the city remembered her anymore, but the lamp over the entrance reflected the moonlight and seemed to be burning.

There was no one around. And who would come here at midnight? But Startsev was waiting, and, as if the moonlight warmed the passion inside him, he waited passionately, imagining kisses, embraces. He sat near the monument for about half an hour, then walked along the side alleys, hat in hand, waiting and thinking about how many women and girls were buried here, who had been beautiful, charming, who loved, burned with passion at night, surrendering to caresses. How unkind mother nature jokes on people, how painful it is to realize this! Startsev thought so, and at the same time he wanted to shout that he wanted, that he was waiting for love at all costs; before him the pieces were no longer marble, but beautiful bodies, he saw forms that shyly hid in the shadows of trees, felt warmth, and this yearning became oppressive…

And as if the curtain fell, the moon went under the clouds,and suddenly everything went dark around. Startsev barely found the gates, – it was already as dark as an autumn night, – then wandered for about an hour and a half, searching for the alley where he had left his horses.

     – I'm tired, I can barely stand, – he said to Panteleimon.

     And, sitting with pleasure in the carriage, he thought:

     "Oh, I shouldn’t gain weight!"



III



     The next evening he went to the Turkins to make a proposal. But it turned out to be inconvenient, as Ekaterina Ivanovna was having her hair done by the hairdresser in her room. She was getting ready for a dance evening at the club.

     He had to sit for a long time again in the dining room and drink tea. Ivan Petrovich, seeing that the guest was pensive and bored, took notes out of his vest pocket and read a funny letter from the German manager about how all the locks on the estate had spoilt and the shyness had collapsed.

     "And they must be giving quite a dowry," Startsev thought, absentmindedly listening.

     After a sleepless night, he was in a state of daze, as if he had been drugged with something sweet and soporific; his soul was foggy but joyful, warm, and at the same time, in his head, some cold, heavy piece reasoned:

     "Stop before it's too late! Is she the right match for you? She’s spoiled, capricious, sleeps until two, and you are the priest’s son, a zemstvo doctor..."

     "Well then? – he thought. – So be it."

     "Moreover, if you marry her, – the piece continued, – her family will force you to quit the zemstvo service and live in the city."

     "Well then? – he thought. – If in the city, then in the city. They’ll give a dowry, we’ll set up a household..."

     Finally, Ekaterina Ivanovna entered in a ball gown, décolletage, pretty, neat, and Startsev admired her and was so delighted that he couldn’t utter a single word, only looked at her and laughed.

     She began to say goodbye, and he – there was no reason for him to stay any longer – stood up, saying that it was time for him to go home: the patients were waiting.

     – Nothing to be done, – said Ivan Petrovich, – go on, and by the way, give Kotik a ride to the club.

     It was drizzling outside, very dark, and only by Panteleimon’s hoarse cough could one guess where the horses were. They put up the cover on the carriage.

     – I walk on the carpet, you walk while you lie, – said Ivan Petrovich, seating his daughter in the carriage, – he walks while he lies… Go! Please say goodbye!

     They set off.

     – I was at the cemetery yesterday, – Startsev began. – How unkind and merciless of you...

     – You were at the cemetery?

     – Yes, I was there and waited for you until almost two o’clock. I suffered...

     – And suffer, if you don’t understand jokes.

     Ekaterina Ivanovna, pleased that she had so cleverly teased the lover and that he loved her so much, laughed out loud and suddenly screamed in fright, because at that very moment the horses sharply turned into the club gates and the carriage tilted. Startsev wrapped his arms around Ekaterina Ivanovna’s waist; she, frightened, pressed against him, and he couldn’t help but passionately kiss her on the lips, on the chin, and hugged her tighter.

     – Enough, – she said dryly.

     And in a moment she was no longer in the carriage, and the city policeman near the illuminated club entrance shouted in a horrible voice at Panteleimon:

     – What are you standing for, crow? Move on!

     Startsev went home but soon returned. Dressed in someone else’s tailcoat and white stiff tie, which somehow always stood up and wanted to slip off the collar, he sat in the club’s lounge at midnight and spoke to Ekaterina Ivanovna passionately:

     – Oh, how little those know who have never loved! It seems to me no one has yet accurately described love, and it is hardly possible to describe this gentle, joyful, tormenting feeling, and whoever has experienced it even once will not try to convey it in words. What are introductions and descriptions for? What useless eloquence? My love is limitless… Please, I beg you, – Startsev finally said, – be my wife!

     – Dmitry Ionich, – said Ekaterina Ivanovna with a very serious expression, after thinking. – Dmitry Ionich, I am very grateful to you for the honor, I respect you, but… – she stood up and continued standing, – sorry, but I cannot be your wife. Let’s speak seriously.Dmitry Ionich, you know, what I love most in life is art
