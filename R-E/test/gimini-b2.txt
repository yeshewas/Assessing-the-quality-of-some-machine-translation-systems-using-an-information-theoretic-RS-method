Having learned from Kovrin that not only had the romance blossomed, but that there would even be a wedding, Yegor Semyonych paced back and forth for a long time, trying to hide his agitation. His hands began to tremble, his neck swelled and turned crimson, he ordered the racing droshky to be harnessed and drove off somewhere. Tanya, who saw him whip the horse and pull his cap down deeply, almost over his ears, understood his mood, locked herself in her room, and cried all day.

     Peaches and plums were already ripe in the greenhouses; packing and sending this delicate and capricious cargo to Moscow required a lot of attention, labor, and trouble. Thanks to the very hot and dry summer, it was necessary to water every tree, which took a lot of time and manpower, and caterpillars appeared in great numbers, which the workers and even Yegor Semyonych and Tanya, to Kovrin's great disgust, crushed directly with their fingers. Despite all this, it was already necessary to take orders for fruits and trees for the autumn and conduct extensive correspondence. And at the busiest time, when it seemed no one had a free minute, field work began, which took more than half of the workers from the garden; Yegor Semyonych, deeply tanned, exhausted, and angry, galloped from the garden to the field and shouted that he was being torn apart and that he would put a bullet in his head.

     And then there was the fuss with the dowry, to which the Pesotskys attached no small importance; from the clinking of scissors, the clatter of sewing machines, the fumes of irons, and the whims of the modiste, a nervous, easily offended lady, everyone in the house felt dizzy. And as if on purpose, guests arrived every day, whom they had to entertain, feed, and even let stay overnight. But all this hard labor passed unnoticed, as if in a fog. Tanya felt as if love and happiness had caught her off guard, although from the age of fourteen she had been sure for some reason that Kovrin would marry her. She was amazed, perplexed, did not believe herself… Then suddenly such joy would surge that she wanted to fly up to the clouds and pray to God there, and then suddenly she would remember that in August she would have to part with her native home and leave her father, or, God knows from where, the thought would come that she was insignificant, petty, and unworthy of such a great man as Kovrin – and she would go to her room, lock herself in, and cry bitterly for several hours. When there were guests, suddenly it would seem to her that Kovrin was extraordinarily handsome and that all women were in love with him and envied her, and her soul would fill with delight and pride, as if she had conquered the whole world, but as soon as he smiled kindly at some young lady, she would tremble with jealousy, go to her room – and again tears. These new sensations completely took hold of her, she helped her father mechanically and noticed neither the peaches, nor the caterpillars, nor the workers, nor how quickly time flew by.

     Almost the same thing was happening to Yegor Semyonych. He worked from morning till night, always rushing somewhere, losing his temper, getting irritated, but all this was in some kind of magical half-sleep. It was as if two people were already inside him: one was the real Yegor Semyonych, who, listening to the gardener Ivan Karlych reporting on the disorders, was indignant and in despair clutched his head, and the other, not real, as if half-drunk, who suddenly interrupted a business conversation mid-sentence, touched the gardener on the shoulder, and began to mumble:

     "Say what you will, but blood means a lot. His mother was an amazing, most noble, most intelligent woman. It was a pleasure to look at her kind, clear, pure face, like an angel. She painted beautifully, wrote poetry, spoke five foreign languages, sang… Poor thing, may she rest in peace, she died of consumption."

     The unreal Yegor Semyonych sighed and, after a pause, continued:

     "When he was a boy and grew up with me, he had the same angelic face, clear and kind. His gaze, his movements, and his speech are tender and graceful, like his mother's. And his mind? He always amazed us with his mind. And besides, it's no wonder he's a master! No wonder! And wait, Ivan Karlych, what will he be like in ten years! You won't be able to reach him!"

     But then the real Yegor Semyonych, catching himself, made a terrible face,grabbed his head and shouted:

     – Devils! They've defiled it, ruined it, made it disgusting! The garden is lost! The garden is dead!

     But Kovrin worked with his former diligence and did not notice the commotion. Love only added fuel to the fire. After each date with Tanya, he, happy, ecstatic, would go to his room and with the same passion with which he had just kissed Tanya and declared his love, he would pick up a book or his manuscript. What the black monk said about God's chosen ones, eternal truth, the brilliant future of humanity, and so on, gave his work a special, extraordinary significance and filled his soul with pride, with a sense of his own greatness. Once or twice a week, in the park or in the house, he would meet the black monk and talk with him for a long time, but this did not frighten him; on the contrary, it delighted him, as he was already firmly convinced that such visions only visited chosen, outstanding people who had dedicated themselves to serving an idea.

     One day the monk appeared during dinner and sat by the window in the dining room. Kovrin was delighted and very cleverly started a conversation with Yegor Semyonych and Tanya about what might be interesting to the monk; the black guest listened and nodded his head amiably, and Yegor Semyonych and Tanya also listened and smiled cheerfully, not suspecting that Kovrin was talking not to them, but to his hallucination.

     The Dormition Fast imperceptibly approached, and soon after it, the wedding day, which, at Yegor Semyonych's insistent request, was celebrated "with a bang," that is, with a senseless revelry that lasted two days. They ate and drank about three thousand rubles' worth, but from the bad hired music, loud toasts, and lackey's scurrying, from the noise and crowding, they did not appreciate the taste of either the expensive wines or the amazing appetizers ordered from Moscow.
Anton Chekhov

     VOLODYA

     On one summer Sunday, around five in the evening, Volodya, a seventeen-year-old youth, unattractive, sickly, and timid, sat in the gazebo at the Shumikhins' dacha and was bored. His gloomy thoughts flowed in three directions. Firstly, tomorrow, Monday, he had to take a mathematics exam; he knew that if he failed to solve the written problem tomorrow, he would be expelled, as he had been in the sixth grade for two years and had an annual algebra grade of 2 3/4. Secondly, his stay with the Shumikhins, wealthy people who pretended to be aristocratic, caused constant pain to his self-esteem. It seemed to him that m-me Shumikhina and her nieces looked at him and his maman as poor relatives and hangers-on, that they did not respect maman and laughed at her. Once, he accidentally overheard m-me Shumikhina telling her cousin Anna Fyodorovna on the terrace that his maman continued to try to look younger and beautify herself, that she never paid her gambling debts and had a fondness for other people's boots and other people's tobacco. Every day Volodya begged maman not to go to the Shumikhins, described to her what an insulting role she played with these people, persuaded, argued, spoke insolently, but she, frivolous, spoiled, having lived through two fortunes in her lifetime - her own and her husband's, always gravitating towards high society, did not understand him, and Volodya had to escort her to the hated dacha twice a week.

     Thirdly, the young man could not for a minute get rid of a strange, unpleasant feeling that was completely new to him... It seemed to him that he was in love with Shumikhina's cousin and guest, Anna Fyodorovna. She was a lively, loud, and giggling lady, about thirty years old, healthy, strong, rosy, with round shoulders, a round, plump chin, and a constant smile on her thin lips. She was not beautiful and not young - Volodya knew this perfectly well, but for some reason, he could not help but think about her, could not help but look at her when she, playing croquet, shrugged her round shoulders and moved her smooth back, or after a long laugh and running up and down the stairs, fell into an armchair and, closing her eyes, breathing heavily, pretended that her chest was tight and stuffy. She was married. Her husband, a respectable architect, came to the dacha once a week, slept soundly, and returned to the city. Volodya's strange feeling began with him causelessly hating this architect and rejoicing every time he left for the city.

     Now, sitting in the gazebo and thinking about tomorrow's exam and about maman, who was being laughed at, he felt a strong desire to see Nyuta (as the Shumikhins called Anna Fyodorovna), to hear her laugh, the rustle of her dress... This desire was not like the pure, poetic love that he knew from novels and about which he dreamed every evening before going to bed; it was strange, incomprehensible, he was ashamed of it and feared it, like something very bad and impure, something difficult to admit to himself...

     "This is not love," he told himself. "You don't fall in love with thirty-year-olds and married women... This is just a little fling... Yes, a fling..."

     Thinking about the fling, he recalled his invincible timidity, his lack of a mustache, his freckles, his narrow eyes, imagined himself next to Nyuta - and this pair seemed impossible to him; then he hastened to imagine himself handsome, brave, witty, dressed in the latest fashion...

     In the midst of his dreams, as he sat hunched over, looking at the ground, in a dark corner of the gazebo, light footsteps were heard. Someone was walking slowly along the alley. Soon the footsteps died down and something white flashed at the entrance.

     "Is anyone here?" a woman's voice asked.

     Volodya recognized the voice and fearfully raised his head.

     "Who's there?" Nyuta asked, entering the gazebo. "Oh, it's you, Volodya? What are you doing here? Thinking? And how can one keep thinking, thinking, thinking... one could go mad like that!"

     Volodya stood up and looked at Nyuta in confusion. She had just returned from the bathhouse. A sheet and a fluffy towel hung over her shoulders,and from under the white silk headscarf, wet hair, stuck to her forehead, peeked out. A damp, cool scent of a bathhouse and almond soap emanated from her. She was out of breath from walking fast. The top button of her blouse was undone, so the young man could see both her neck and chest.

     - Why are you silent? - Nyuta asked, looking at Volodya. - It's impolite to be silent when a lady is speaking to you. What a seal you are, Volodya! You just sit, silent, thinking like some philosopher. There's no life or fire in you at all! You're annoying, really... At your age, you should be living, jumping, chatting, courting women, falling in love.

     Volodya looked at the sheet, which was supported by a white, plump hand, and thought...

     - Silent! - Nyuta was surprised. - That's even strange... Listen, be a man! Well, at least smile! Phooey, annoying philosopher! - she laughed. And you know, Volodya, why are you such a seal? Because you don't court women. Why don't you court them? True, there are no young ladies here, but nothing prevents you from courting ladies! Why don't you, for example, court me?

     Volodya listened and, in deep, intense thought, scratched his temple.

     - Only very proud people are silent and love solitude, - Nyuta continued, pulling his hand away from his temple. - You are proud, Volodya. Why are you looking from under your brows? Please look me straight in the face! Come on, seal!

     Volodya decided to speak. Wanting to smile, he twitched his lower lip, blinked his eyes, and again pulled his hand to his temple.

     - I... I love you! - he uttered.

     Nyuta raised her eyebrows in surprise and laughed.

     - What do I hear?! - she sang, as opera singers do when they hear something terrible. - How? What did you say? Repeat, repeat...

     - I... I love you! - Volodya repeated.

     And without any will of his own, understanding nothing and comprehending nothing, he took half a step towards Nyuta and took her hand above the wrist. His eyes blurred and tears welled up, the whole world turned into one large, shaggy towel that smelled of a bathhouse.

     - Bravo, bravo! - he heard cheerful laughter. - Why are you silent? I want you to speak! Well?

     Seeing that she didn't stop him from holding her hand, Volodya looked at Nyuta's laughing face and awkwardly, uncomfortably took her by the waist with both hands, with his hands meeting on her back. He held her by the waist with both hands, and she, throwing her hands behind her head and showing dimples on her elbows, adjusted her hairstyle under the scarf and said in a calm voice:

     - You need to be agile, courteous, sweet, Volodya, and you can only become that under the influence of female society. However, what an unpleasant... angry face you have. You need to talk, laugh... Yes, Volodya, don't be a grouch, you are young and will have time to philosophize later. Well, let me go, I'm leaving. Let me go!

     She effortlessly freed her waist and, humming something, left the gazebo. Volodya remained alone. He smoothed his hair, smiled, and walked back and forth three times, then sat on the bench and smiled again. He was unbearably ashamed, so much so that he even wondered if human shame could reach such intensity and strength. From shame, he smiled, whispered some incoherent words, and gesticulated.

     He was ashamed that he had just been treated like a boy, ashamed of his timidity, and most importantly, that he had dared to take a respectable married woman by the waist, although neither by age, nor by his external qualities, nor by social status, he, as it seemed to him, had no right to do so.

     He jumped up, left the gazebo, and, without looking back, walked deep into the garden, away from the house.

     "Oh, to leave here as soon as possible! - he thought, clutching his head. God, as soon as possible!"

     The train, on which Volodya and maman were to travel, departed at eight forty. There were about three hours left until the train, but he would have gladly gone to the station right away, without waiting for maman.

     At eight o'clock, he approached the house. His whole figure expressed determination: come what may! He decided to enter boldly, to look straight,speak loudly, no matter what.

He walked past the terrace, the large hall, the living room, and stopped in the latter to catch his breath. From there, he could hear tea being drunk in the neighboring dining room. M-me Shumikhina, maman, and Nyuta were talking and laughing about something.

Volodya listened.

"I assure you!" said Nyuta. "I couldn't believe my own eyes! When he started declaring his love for me and even, imagine, took me by the waist, I didn't recognize him. And you know, he has a manner! When he said he was in love with me, there was something savage in his face, like a Circassian."

"No, really!" gasped maman, bursting into a prolonged laugh. "No, really! How he reminds me of his father!"

Volodya ran back and rushed out into the fresh air. "And how can they talk about it out loud!" he tormented himself, wringing his hands and looking at the sky with horror. "They talk out loud, coolly... And maman laughed... maman! My God, why did you give me such a mother? Why?"

But he had to go back into the house, no matter what. He walked along the alley three times, calmed down a little, and entered the house.

"Why aren't you coming to drink tea on time?" asked m-me Shumikhina strictly.

"My fault, I... I have to go," he mumbled, not looking up. "Maman, it's already eight o'clock!"

"Go by yourself, my dear," said maman languidly, "I'm staying overnight at Lily's. Goodbye, my friend... Let me cross you..."

She crossed her son and said in French, addressing Nyuta:

"He's a little like Lermontov... Isn't he?"

Having said goodbye somehow and without looking at anyone's face, Volodya left the dining room. Ten minutes later, he was already walking along the road to the station and was glad of it. Now he was neither scared nor ashamed; he breathed easily and freely.

Half a verst from the station, he sat on a stone by the road and began to look at the sun, which was more than halfway hidden behind the embankment. At the station, lights were already lit here and there, a murky green light flickered, but the train was not yet visible. Volodya enjoyed sitting, not moving, and listening to how evening gradually approached. The twilight of the arbor, the footsteps, the smell of the bathhouse, the laughter, and the waist - all this appeared in his imagination with striking clarity, and all this was no longer as frightening and significant as before...

"Trifles... She didn't pull her hand away and laughed when I held her by the waist," he thought, "so she liked it. If she found it repulsive, she would have gotten angry..."

And now Volodya was annoyed that there, in the arbor, he hadn't been bold enough. He regretted that he was leaving so foolishly, and he was already sure that if that situation were to repeat itself, he would be bolder and would look at things more simply.

And it's not difficult for the situation to repeat itself. The Shumikhins take long walks after dinner. If Volodya goes for a walk with Nyuta in the dark garden, then - there's the opportunity!

"I'll go back," he thought, "and leave tomorrow on the morning train... I'll say I missed the train."

And he returned... M-me Shumikhina, maman, Nyuta, and one of the nieces were sitting on the terrace and playing whist. When Volodya lied to them that he had missed the train, they became worried that he might miss his exam tomorrow and advised him to get up early. All the time they were playing cards, he sat aside, eagerly looking at Nyuta and waiting... A plan was already ready in his head: he would approach Nyuta in the dark, take her hand, then embrace her; there was no need to say anything, as everything would be clear to both of them without words.

But after dinner, the ladies did not go for a walk in the garden and continued to play cards. They played until one in the morning and then went to bed.

"How foolish all this is!" Volodya grumbled, getting into bed. "But never mind, I'll wait for tomorrow... Tomorrow again in the arbor. Never mind..."

He didn't try to sleep, but sat in bed, hugging his knees with his hands, and thought. The thought of the exam was repulsive to him. He had already decided that he would be expelled and that there was nothing terrible in this expulsion. On the contrary, everything was very good, even very good. Tomorrow he would be free as a bird, put on civilian clothes, openly smoke, come here, and court Nyuta,anytime; and he would no longer be a schoolboy, but a "young man." And the rest, what is called a career and a future, was so clear: Volodya would enlist as a volunteer, as a telegraph operator, finally, in a pharmacy, where he would rise to the rank of pharmacist... aren't there many positions? An hour or two passed, and he still sat and thought...

     Around two o'clock, when it was already dawning, the door creaked softly and maman entered the room.

     "Are you awake?" she asked, yawning. "Sleep, sleep, I'll just be a minute... I'll just get the drops..."

     "What do you need them for?"

     "Poor Lily is having spasms again. Sleep, my child, you have an exam tomorrow..."

     She took a vial of something from the cabinet, went to the window, read the label, and left.

     "Maria Leontievna, these aren't the right drops!" Volodya heard a woman's voice a minute later. "These are lily of the valley, and Lily is asking for morphine. Is your son asleep? Ask him to find it..."

     It was Nyuta's voice. Volodya felt a chill. He quickly put on his trousers, threw his greatcoat over his shoulders, and went to the door.

     "Do you understand? Morphine!" Nyuta explained in a whisper. "It should be written in Latin. Wake Volodya, he'll find it..."

     Maman opened the door, and Volodya saw Nyuta. She was in the same blouse she wore for swimming. Her hair was uncombed, scattered over her shoulders, her face sleepy, dark from the twilight...

     "Volodya isn't sleeping..." she said. "Volodya, please, darling, look for the morphine in the cabinet! What a nuisance with this Lily... She always has something."

     Maman mumbled something, yawned, and left.

     "Look for it then," Nyuta said. "Why are you standing there?"

     Volodya went to the cabinet, knelt down, and began sifting through the vials and boxes of medicines. His hands trembled, and in his chest and stomach, there was a sensation as if cold waves were running through all his insides. From the smell of ether, carbolic acid, and various herbs, which he grabbed without any need with trembling hands and which scattered from this, he felt suffocated and dizzy.

     "It seems maman has left," he thought. "That's good... good..."

     "Soon?" Nyuta asked languidly.

     "Just a moment... This seems to be morphine..." Volodya said, reading the word "morph..." on one of the labels. "Here you go!"

     Nyuta stood in the doorway so that one of her legs was in the corridor and the other in his room. She was adjusting her hair, which was difficult to adjust because it was so thick and long! - and gazed distractedly at Volodya. In her spacious blouse, sleepy, with her hair down, in the meager light that entered the room from the white, but not yet sunlit sky, she seemed charming, luxurious to Volodya... Enchanted, trembling all over and recalling with pleasure how he had embraced that wonderful body in the gazebo, he handed her the drops and said:

     "How you are..."

     "What?"

     She entered the room.

     "What?" she asked, smiling.

     He was silent and looked at her, then, as he had in the gazebo, took her hand... And she looked at him, smiled, and waited: what would happen next?

     "I love you..." he whispered.

     She stopped smiling, thought, and said:

     "Wait, I think someone's coming. Oh, these schoolboys! she said in a low voice, walking to the door and looking into the corridor. "No, no one's there..."

     She returned...

     Then it seemed to Volodya that the room, Nyuta, the dawn, and he himself - everything merged into one sensation of sharp, extraordinary, unprecedented happiness, for which one could give one's whole life and go to eternal torment, but half a minute passed, and all this suddenly disappeared. Volodya saw only a full, unattractive face, distorted by an expression of disgust, and he himself suddenly felt revulsion for what had happened.

     "However, I need to leave," Nyuta said, looking at Volodya with disdain. "How ugly, pathetic... ugh, an ugly duckling!"

     How ugly her long hair, her spacious blouse, her steps, her voice seemed to Volodya now!

     "Ugly duckling... - he thought after she left. - Indeed, I am ugly... Everything is ugly."

     The sun was already rising in the yard, birds were singing loudly; one could hear,"""how the gardener walked in the garden and how his wheelbarrow creaked... A little later, the lowing of cows and the sounds of a shepherd's pipe were heard. The sunlight and sounds spoke of a pure, elegant, poetic life somewhere in this world. But where was it? Neither maman nor all the people around him ever spoke to Volodya about it.

     When the footman woke him for the morning train, he pretended to be asleep...

     "To hell with it all!" he thought.

     He got out of bed at eleven o'clock. Combing his hair in front of the mirror and looking at his ugly, pale face from a sleepless night, he thought:

     "Exactly... The Ugly Duckling."

     When maman saw him and was horrified that he wasn't at the exam, Volodya said:

     - I overslept, maman... But don't worry, I'll provide a medical certificate.

     M-me Shumikhina and Nyuta woke up at one o'clock. Volodya heard m-me Shumikhina, having woken up, open her window with a clang, and Nyuta answer her coarse voice with a booming laugh. He saw the door open and a string of nieces and hangers-on (maman was among the latter) trail out of the living room for breakfast, how Nyuta's washed, laughing face flashed by, and next to her face, the black eyebrows and beard of the recently arrived architect.

     Nyuta was in a Little Russian costume, which did not suit her at all and made her clumsy; the architect made vulgar and flat jokes; there was too much onion in the cutlets served for breakfast - so it seemed to Volodya. It also seemed to him that Nyuta deliberately laughed loudly and glanced in his direction, to make him understand that the memory of the night did not bother her at all and that she did not notice the presence of the ugly duckling at the table.

     At four o'clock, Volodya was going to the station with maman. Dirty memories, a sleepless night, the impending expulsion from the gymnasium, pangs of conscience - all this now aroused in him a heavy, gloomy malice. He looked at maman's thin profile, at her small nose, at the waterproof coat Nyuta had given her, and mumbled:

     - Why do you powder your face? It's not appropriate at your age! You put on makeup, don't pay your losses, smoke other people's tobacco... disgusting! I don't love you... I don't love you!

     He insulted her, and she nervously moved her eyes, clasped her hands, and whispered in horror:

     - What are you saying, my dear? My God, the coachman will hear! Be quiet, or the coachman will hear! He hears everything!

     - I don't love you... I don't love you! - he continued, gasping for breath. - You are immoral, heartless... Don't dare to wear that waterproof! Do you hear? Or I'll tear it to shreds...

     - Come to your senses, my child! - maman cried. - The coachman will hear!

     - And where is my father's fortune? Where is your money? You've squandered everything! I'm not ashamed of my poverty, but I'm ashamed that I have such a mother... When my comrades ask about you, I always blush.

     They had to travel two stations by train to the city. All the time Volodya stood on the platform, trembling all over. He didn't want to go into the carriage, as his mother, whom he hated, was sitting there. He hated himself, the conductors, the smoke from the locomotive, the cold, to which he attributed his trembling... And the heavier his heart became, the more strongly he felt that somewhere in this world, among some people, there was a pure, noble, warm, elegant life, full of love, caresses, joy, expanse... He felt this and longed so much that even one passenger, looking intently into his face, asked:

     - Probably your teeth hurt?

     In the city, maman and Volodya lived with Marya Petrovna, a noblewoman who rented a large apartment and sublet it to tenants. Maman rented two rooms: in one, with windows, where her bed stood and two paintings in golden frames hung on the walls, she lived herself, and in the other, adjoining, small and dark, Volodya lived. There was a sofa on which he slept, and besides this sofa, there was no other furniture; the whole room was occupied by wicker baskets with clothes, hatboxes, and all sorts of junk that maman kept for some reason.Volodya did his homework in his mother's room or in the "common room" - that's what they called the large room where all the residents gathered for dinner and in the evenings.


VII



     One long winter night, Kovrin was lying in bed reading a French novel. Poor Tanya, who suffered from headaches in the evenings due to her unaccustomed city life, had long been asleep and occasionally muttered some incoherent phrases in her delirium.

     It struck three o'clock. Kovrin put out the candle and lay down; he lay for a long time with his eyes closed, but could not fall asleep because, as it seemed to him, the bedroom was very hot and Tanya was delirious. At half past four, he lit the candle again and at that moment saw the black monk, who was sitting in an armchair near the bed.

     – Hello, – said the monk and, after a short pause, asked: – What are you thinking about now?

     – About glory, – Kovrin replied. – The French novel I was just reading depicts a man, a young scholar, who does foolish things and wastes away from longing for glory. This longing is incomprehensible to me.

     – Because you are intelligent. You are indifferent to glory, like a toy that doesn't interest you.

     – Yes, that's true.

     – Fame does not smile upon you. What is flattering, or amusing, or instructive in having your name carved on a tombstone and then time erasing that inscription along with the gilding? And, fortunately, there are too many of you for weak human memory to retain your names.

     – I understand, – Kovrin agreed. – And why remember them? But let's talk about something else. For example, about happiness. What is happiness?

     When the clock struck five, he was sitting on the bed, with his feet dangling on the carpet, and said, addressing the monk:

     – In ancient times, a happy man eventually became afraid of his happiness – it was so great! – and, to appease the gods, he sacrificed his favorite ring to them. You know? And like Polycrates, my happiness is starting to worry me a little. It seems strange to me that from morning till night I experience only joy; it fills my whole being and drowns out all other feelings. I don't know what sadness, sorrow, or boredom is. Here I am not sleeping, I have insomnia, but I am not bored. Seriously, I'm starting to be puzzled.

     – But why? – the monk wondered. – Is joy a supernatural feeling? Shouldn't it be a normal human state? The higher a person is in intellectual and moral development, the freer they are, the more pleasure life brings them. Socrates,Diogenes and Marcus Aurelius experienced joy, not sorrow. And the apostle says: rejoice always. So rejoice and be happy.

     "But what if the gods get angry?" Kovrin joked and laughed. "If they take away my comfort and make me shiver and starve, I hardly think I'll like that."

     Meanwhile, Tanya woke up and looked at her husband with astonishment and horror. He was talking, addressing the armchair, gesticulating and laughing: his eyes sparkled and there was something strange in his laughter.

     "Andryusha, who are you talking to?" she asked, grabbing his hand, which he had extended towards the monk. "Andryusha! To whom?"

     "Huh? To whom?" Kovrin was confused. "To him... He's sitting right there," he said, pointing to the black monk.

     "There's no one here... no one! Andryusha, you're sick!"

     Tanya hugged her husband and pressed herself against him, as if protecting him from visions, and covered his eyes with her hand.

     "You're sick!" she sobbed, trembling all over. "Forgive me, my dear, but I've long noticed that your soul is troubled by something... You are mentally ill, Andryusha..."

     Her trembling communicated itself to him. He glanced once more at the armchair, which was now empty, suddenly felt weakness in his arms and legs, got scared, and began to get dressed.

     "It's nothing, Tanya, nothing..." he mumbled, trembling. "Indeed, I'm a little unwell... it's time to admit it."

     "I've noticed it for a long time... and Papa noticed it," she said, trying to hold back her sobs. "You talk to yourself, you smile strangely... you don't sleep. Oh, my God, my God, save us!" she said in horror. "But don't be afraid, Andryusha, for God's sake, don't be afraid..."

     She also started to get dressed. Only now, looking at her, did Kovrin understand the full danger of his situation, understand what the black monk and conversations with him meant. It was clear to him now that he was insane.

     Both, not knowing why, got dressed and went into the hall: she in front, he behind her. There, awakened by the sobs, Yegor Semyonych, who was visiting them, stood in a dressing gown with a candle in his hands.

     "Don't be afraid, Andryusha," Tanya said, trembling as if with a fever, "don't be afraid... Papa, it will all pass... it will all pass..."

     Kovrin was too agitated to speak. He wanted to say to his father-in-law in a jocular tone:

     "Congratulations, I think I've gone mad," but he only moved his lips and smiled bitterly.

     At nine in the morning, they put a coat and a fur coat on him, wrapped him in a shawl, and took him in a carriage to the doctor. He began treatment.

VIII

     Summer came again, and the doctor ordered him to go to the village. Kovrin had already recovered, stopped seeing the black monk, and only needed to regain his physical strength. Living with his father-in-law in the village, he drank a lot of milk, worked only two hours a day, did not drink wine, and did not smoke.

     On the eve of St. Elijah's Day, an all-night vigil was held in the house. When the deacon handed the censer to the priest, the old, enormous hall smelled exactly like a cemetery, and Kovrin became bored. He went out into the garden. Without noticing the luxurious flowers, he walked around the garden, sat on a bench, then walked through the park; reaching the river, he went down and stood there in thought, looking at the water. The gloomy pines with shaggy roots, which last year had seen him here so young, joyful, and cheerful, now did not whisper, but stood motionless and mute, as if they did not recognize him. And indeed, his head was shorn, his long beautiful hair was gone, his gait was sluggish, his face, compared to last summer, had grown fuller and paler.

     He crossed to the other bank on the logs. Where rye had been last year, now mown oats lay in rows. The sun had already set, and a wide red glow blazed on the horizon, foretelling windy weather for tomorrow. It was quiet. Gazing in the direction where the black monk had first appeared last year, Kovrin stood for about twenty minutes until the evening twilight began to fade...

     When he, sluggish and dissatisfied, returned home, the all-night vigil was already over. Yegor Semyonych and Tanya were sitting on the steps of the terrace, drinking tea. They were talking about something, but, seeing Kovrin, they suddenly fell silent, and he concluded from their faces that their conversation had been about him.

     "You, it seems,""It's time to drink milk," Tanya said to her husband.

"No, it's not time..." he replied, sitting on the lowest step. "You drink it. I don't want any."

Tanya exchanged an anxious glance with her father and said in a guilty voice,

"You yourself notice that milk is good for you."

"Yes, very good!" Kovrin sneered. "Congratulations: after Friday, I've gained another pound." He tightly clasped his head with his hands and said with anguish, "Why, why did you treat me? Bromine preparations, idleness, warm baths, supervision, cowardly fear for every sip, for every step – all this will eventually drive me to idiocy. I was going mad, I had megalomania, but at least I was cheerful, vigorous, and even happy, I was interesting and original. Now I've become more reasonable and solid, but I'm like everyone else: I'm a mediocrity, I'm bored with life... Oh, how cruelly you treated me! I saw hallucinations, but who did that bother? I ask: who did that bother?"

"God knows what you're saying!" sighed Yegor Semyonich. "It's even boring to listen."

"Then don't listen."

The presence of people, especially Yegor Semyonich, now irritated Kovrin; he answered him dryly, coldly, and even rudely, and looked at him only with mockery and hatred, while Yegor Semyonich was embarrassed and coughed guiltily, although he felt no guilt himself. Not understanding why their kind, good-natured relationship had changed so sharply, Tanya clung to her father and anxiously looked into his eyes; she wanted to understand but couldn't, and it was clear to her only that their relationship was getting worse and worse every day, that her father had aged greatly recently, and her husband had become irritable, capricious, fault-finding, and uninteresting. She could no longer laugh and sing, ate nothing at dinner, didn't sleep for whole nights, expecting something terrible, and was so exhausted that she once lay in a faint from dinner until evening. During the all-night vigil, it seemed to her that her father was crying, and now, as the three of them sat on the terrace, she made an effort not to think about it.

"How happy Buddha and Mohammed or Shakespeare are that kind relatives and doctors didn't treat them for ecstasy and inspiration!" said Kovrin. "If Mohammed had taken potassium bromide for his nerves, worked only two hours a day, and drunk milk, then as little would remain of that remarkable man as of his dog. Doctors and kind relatives will eventually make humanity dull, mediocrity will be considered genius, and civilization will perish. If you only knew," Kovrin said with annoyance, "how grateful I am to you!"

He felt a strong irritation and, to avoid saying too much, quickly got up and went into the house. It was quiet, and the scent of tobacco and jalap drifted from the garden through the open windows. In the huge dark hall, moonlight lay in green patches on the floor and on the piano. Kovrin recalled the raptures of last summer, when it also smelled of jalap and the moon shone in the windows. To regain last year's mood, he quickly went to his study, lit a strong cigar, and ordered the footman to bring wine. But the cigar made his mouth bitter and unpleasant, and the wine didn't taste the same as last year. And what it means to get out of the habit! From the cigar and two sips of wine, he became dizzy and his heart began to pound, so he had to take potassium bromide.

Before going to bed, Tanya told him,

"Father adores you. You're angry with him for something, and it's killing him. Look: he's aging by the hour, not by the day. I beg you, Andryusha, for God's sake, for the sake of your deceased father, for the sake of my peace, be kind to him!"

"I can't and I won't."

"But why?" Tanya asked, beginning to tremble all over. "Explain to me, why?"

"Because I don't like him, that's all," Kovrin said carelessly and shrugged, "but let's not talk about him: he's your father."

"I can't, I can't understand!" Tanya said, pressing her temples and staring at one spot. "Something incomprehensible, terrible is happening in our house. You've changed, you're not yourself... You, intelligent,"an extraordinary person, you get annoyed over trifles, you meddle in squabbles... Such small things bother you that sometimes one simply wonders and doesn't believe: is that really you? Now, now, don't be angry, don't be angry, – she continued, frightened by her own words and kissing his hands. – You are smart, kind, noble. You will be fair to father. He is so kind!

     – He is not kind, but good-natured. Vaudeville uncles, like your father, with well-fed, good-natured faces, extraordinarily hospitable and eccentric, once touched and amused me in stories, and in vaudevilles, and in life, but now they are repulsive to me. They are egoists to the core. Most repulsive to me is their satiety and this gastric, purely bovine or boar-like optimism.

     Tanya sat on the bed and laid her head on the pillow.

     – This is torture, – she uttered, and from her voice it was clear that she was already extremely tired and that it was difficult for her to speak. – Not a single peaceful minute since winter... This is terrible, my God! I am suffering...

     His face seemed ugly and unpleasant to Tanya. Hatred and a mocking expression did not suit him. And even before, she had noticed that something was missing from his face, as if since he had cut his hair, his face had also changed. She wanted to say something offensive to him, but immediately she caught herself feeling hostile, got scared, and left the bedroom.



IX

But on the appointed day, he informed the student inspector by telegram that he would not be giving lectures due to illness.

     He was spitting blood. He would spit blood, but it happened once or twice a month that it flowed profusely, and then he would become extremely weak and fall into a drowsy state. This illness did not particularly frighten him, as he knew that his late mother had lived with exactly the same illness for ten years, even longer; and doctors assured him that it was not dangerous, and only advised him not to worry, to lead a regular life, and to talk less.

His mood was peaceful, submissive: he willingly obeyed, and when Varvara Nikolaevna – as his companion was called – decided to take him to Crimea, he agreed, although he had a premonition that nothing good would come of this trip.

The handwriting on the envelope reminded him of how, two years ago, he had been unfair and cruel, how he had vented his spiritual emptiness, boredom, loneliness, and dissatisfaction with life on innocent people. I



     When visitors to the provincial town of S. complained about boredom and the monotony of life, the local residents, as if justifying themselves, would say that, on the contrary, life in S. was very good, that S. had a library, a theater, a club, balls were held, and finally, there were intelligent, interesting, pleasant families with whom one could make acquaintances. And they pointed to the Turkin family as the most educated and talented.

     This family lived on the main street, next to the governor, in their own house. Turkin himself, Ivan Petrovich, a stout, handsome brunette with sideburns, organized amateur theatricals for charity, played old generals himself, and coughed very comically while doing so. He knew many anecdotes, charades, proverbs, loved to joke and quip, and always had such an expression that it was impossible to tell if he was joking or speaking seriously. His wife, Vera Iosifovna, a slender, pretty lady in a pince-nez, wrote stories and novels and gladly read them aloud to her guests. Their daughter, Ekaterina Ivanovna, a young girl, played the piano. In short, every member of the family had some talent of their own. The Turkins welcomed guests warmly and displayed their talents cheerfully, with heartfelt simplicity. Their large stone house was spacious and cool in summer, half the windows overlooked an old shady garden where nightingales sang in spring; when guests were in the house, knives clattered in the kitchen, and the smell of fried onions wafted from the yard – and this always heralded an abundant and delicious dinner.

     And Doctor Startsev, Dmitry Ionych, when he had just been appointed a zemstvo doctor and settled in Dyalizh, nine versts from S., was also told that, as an intelligent person, he absolutely had to get acquainted with the Turkins. One winter, he was introduced to Ivan Petrovich on the street; they talked about the weather, the theater, cholera, and an invitation followed. In spring, on a holiday – it was Ascension Day – after seeing patients, Startsev went to town to amuse himself a little and incidentally buy a few things. He walked slowly (he didn't have his own horses yet), and all the while he hummed: When I had not yet drunk tears from the cup of being…



     In town, he had dinner, walked in the garden, then somehow Ivan Petrovich's invitation came to mind, and he decided to visit the Turkins, to see what kind of people they were.

     "Hello, please," said Ivan Petrovich, meeting him on the porch. "Very, very glad to see such a pleasant guest. Come, I'll introduce you to my better half. I tell him, Verochka," he continued, introducing the doctor to his wife, "I tell him that he has no Roman right to sit in his hospital, he must devote his leisure to society. Isn't that right, my dear?"

     "Sit here," said Vera Iosifovna, seating the guest next to her. "You can flirt with me. My husband is jealous, he's an Othello, but we'll try to behave so that he notices nothing."

     "Oh, you little chick, you spoiled girl…" Ivan Petrovich murmured tenderly and kissed her on the forehead. "You've come at a very opportune moment," he turned to the guest again, "my better half has written a major novel and will read it aloud today."

     "Zhanchik," said Vera Iosifovna to her husband, "dites que l'on nous donne du the1."

     Startsev was introduced to Ekaterina Ivanovna, an eighteen-year-old girl, very much like her mother, just as slender and pretty. Her expression was still childlike and her waist thin, delicate; and her virginal, already developed bosom, beautiful, healthy, spoke of spring, true spring. Then they drank tea with jam, honey, sweets, and very delicious cookies that melted in the mouth. As evening approached, guests gradually arrived, and to each of them Ivan Petrovich turned his laughing eyes and said:

     "Hello, please."

     Then everyone sat in the living room, with very serious faces, and Vera Iosifovna read her novel. She began: "The frost grew stronger…" The windows were wide open, one could hear knives clattering in the kitchen, and the smell of fried onions wafted in… It was comfortable in the soft, deep armchairs, the lights flickered so gently in the twilight of the living room; and now,in a summer evening, when voices and laughter drifted from the street and the scent of lilac wafted from the yard, it was hard to understand how the frost could have grown stronger and how the setting sun could have illuminated with its cold rays a snowy plain and a traveler walking alone on the road; Vera Iosifovna was reading about how a young, beautiful countess established schools, hospitals, and libraries in her village and how she fell in love with a wandering artist – she was reading about things that never happen in life, and yet it was pleasant and comfortable to listen, and such good, peaceful thoughts came to mind – she didn't want to get up.

     "Not bad…" Ivan Petrovich said quietly.

     And one of the guests, listening and drifting far, far away in thought, said barely audibly:

     "Yes… indeed…"

     An hour passed, then another. In the nearby city garden, an orchestra played and a choir of singers sang. When Vera Iosifovna closed her notebook, they were silent for about five minutes, listening to "Luchinushka," which the choir was singing, and this song conveyed what was not in the novel and what happens in life.

     "Do you publish your works in magazines?" Startsev asked Vera Iosifovna.

     "No," she replied, "I don't publish anywhere. I write it and hide it in my cupboard. Why publish?" she explained. "After all, we have the means."

     And for some reason, everyone sighed.

     "Now, Kotik, play something," Ivan Petrovich said to his daughter.

     They lifted the piano lid, opened the sheet music that was already prepared. Ekaterina Ivanovna sat down and struck the keys with both hands; and then immediately struck again with all her might, and again, and again; her shoulders and chest shook, she stubbornly struck the same spot, and it seemed that she would not stop until she drove the keys into the piano. The living room filled with thunder; everything thundered: the floor, the ceiling, the furniture… Ekaterina Ivanovna played a difficult passage, interesting precisely because of its difficulty, long and monotonous, and Startsev, listening, imagined stones falling from a high mountain, falling and falling, and he wished they would stop falling sooner, and at the same time, Ekaterina Ivanovna, flushed with effort, strong, energetic, with a lock of hair fallen on her forehead, pleased him very much. After a winter spent in Dyalizh, among the sick and peasants, to sit in the living room, to look at this young, elegant, and probably pure being, and to listen to these noisy, annoying, but still cultured sounds – it was so pleasant, so new…

     "Well, Kotik, today you played like never before," Ivan Petrovich said with tears in his eyes, when his daughter finished and stood up. "Die, Denis, you won't write better."

     "No, I'm only just planning to go to the conservatory, but for now I've been studying here, with Madame Zavlovskaya."

Laughing only with his eyes, he told anecdotes, made witty remarks, posed funny riddles and solved them himself, and all the while spoke in his unusual language, developed through long exercises in wit and, apparently, long since become a habit for him: bolshinsky, not bad, thank you for twisting me…

II

     Startsev kept meaning to visit the Turkins, but there was a lot of work at the hospital, and he couldn't find a free hour. More than a year passed this way, in toil and solitude; but then a letter in a blue envelope arrived from the city...

     Vera Iosifovna had long suffered from migraines, but lately, with Kotik threatening every day to leave for the conservatory, the attacks had become more frequent. All the city doctors had visited the Turkins; finally, it was the zemstvo doctor's turn. Vera Iosifovna wrote him a touching letter, asking him to come and alleviate her suffering. Startsev came, and after that, he began to visit the Turkins often, very often... He did indeed help Vera Iosifovna a little, and she already told all guests that he was an extraordinary, amazing doctor. But he no longer visited the Turkins for her migraines...

     A holiday. Ekaterina Ivanovna finished her long, tedious exercises on the piano. Then they sat in the dining room for a long time, drinking tea, and Ivan Petrovich told something funny. But then the doorbell rang; they had to go to the front hall to meet some guest; Startsev took advantage of the moment of confusion and said to Ekaterina Ivanovna in a whisper, greatly agitated:

     "For God's sake, I beg you, don't torment me, let's go to the garden!"

     She shrugged her shoulders, as if bewildered and not understanding what he wanted from her, but she stood up and went.

Even in the way her dress sat on her, he saw something extraordinarily sweet, touching in its simplicity and naive grace. Like almost all young women from S., she read a lot (in general, very little was read in S., and at the local library they even said that if it weren't for the girls and young Jews, they might as well close the library); this infinitely pleased Startsev, he would ask her with excitement each time what she had read in recent days, and, enchanted, listened when she told him.

And was it fitting for him, a zemstvo doctor, an intelligent, respectable man, to sigh, receive notes, wander through cemeteries, do silly things that even high school students now laughed at? Starcev thought so, and at the same time, he wanted to shout that he wanted, that he was waiting for love at all costs; before him, no longer pieces of marble, but beautiful bodies, he saw forms that shyly hid in the shadows of the trees, felt warmth, and this languor became burdensome...

     The next evening he went to the Turkins' to propose. But it turned out to be inconvenient, as Ekaterina Ivanovna was having her hair done by a hairdresser in her room. She was going to the club for a dance evening.

     "And I was at the cemetery yesterday," Startsev began. "How ungenerous and merciless of you…"

Startsev embraced Ekaterina Ivanovna around the waist; she, frightened, pressed against him, and he couldn't resist and passionately kissed her on the lips, on the chin, and hugged her tighter.
