VI

Learning from Kovrin that not only had the romance progressed, but that there would even be a wedding, Egor Semenyich walked back and forth for a long time, trying to hide his agitation. His hands began to tremble, his neck swelled and turned crimson, he ordered the racing sleigh to be harnessed and drove off somewhere. Tanya, seeing how he lashed the horse and how deeply, almost over his ears, he pulled down his cap, understood his mood, locked herself in her room and cried all day.

In the greenhouses, peaches and plums were already ripening; packing and shipping this delicate and finicky cargo to Moscow required much attention, labor, and effort. Due to the fact that the summer was very hot and dry, it was necessary to water every tree, which took much time and labor, and caterpillars appeared in great numbers, which the workers and even Egor Semenyich and Tanya, to Kovrin's great disgust, crushed with their bare fingers. Despite all this, it was already necessary to accept orders for autumn fruits and trees and conduct extensive correspondence. And at the busiest time, when it seemed no one had a free minute, field work began, which took more than half the garden workers; Egor Semenyich, deeply tanned, exhausted, and angry, rode back and forth between the garden and the field, shouting that he was being torn to pieces and that he would put a bullet in his head.

And then there was the fuss with the dowry, which the Pesotskys attached considerable importance to; from the clicking of scissors, the clatter of sewing machines, the smell of hot irons, and from the whims of the dressmaker, a nervous, touchy lady, everyone in the house had headaches. And as if on purpose, guests arrived every day, who had to be entertained, fed, and even put up for the night. But all this hard labor passed unnoticed, as if in a fog. Tanya felt as though love and happiness had caught her by surprise, although since she was fourteen she had been somehow certain that Kovrin would marry precisely her. She was amazed, bewildered, could not believe it… Suddenly such joy would surge over her that she wanted to fly up into the clouds and pray to God there, but then suddenly she would remember that in August she would have to part with her native home and leave her father, or from God knows where, the thought would come that she was insignificant, small, and unworthy of such a great man as Kovrin – and she would go to her room, lock the door, and cry bitterly for several hours. When there were guests, suddenly it would seem to her that Kovrin was extraordinarily handsome and that all women were in love with him and envied her, and her soul would fill with rapture and pride, as if she had conquered the whole world, but the moment he smiled kindly at some young lady, she would already tremble with jealousy, go to her room – and tears again. These new sensations took complete possession of her; she helped her father mechanically and did not notice either the peaches, or the caterpillars, or the workers, or how quickly time was passing.

Almost the same thing was happening to Egor Semenyich. He worked from morning to night, always hurrying somewhere, losing his temper, getting irritated, but all of this in some kind of magical half-sleep. It was as if two people were already sitting inside him: one was the real Egor Semenyich, who, listening to the gardener Ivan Karlych reporting to him about the disorders, was indignant and in despair clutched his head, and the other, not real, as if half-drunk, who suddenly interrupted the business conversation mid-sentence, touched the gardener on the shoulder and began to mutter:

– Say what you will, but blood means a great deal. His mother was a remarkable woman, most noble, most intelligent. It was a pleasure to look at her kind, clear, pure face, like an angel's. She drew beautifully, wrote poetry, spoke five foreign languages, sang… Poor thing, may she rest in peace, she died of consumption.

The not-real Egor Semenyich sighed and, after a pause, continued:

– When he was a boy and growing up with me, he had such an angelic face, clear and kind. His gaze, his movements, his speech are all tender and graceful, like his mother's. And his mind? He always amazed us with his intelligence. And indeed, it's no wonder he's a master's degree holder! No wonder! But wait, Ivan Karlych, what will he be like in ten years! You won't be able to reach him!

But then the real Egor Semenyich, catching himself, made a terrible face,He grabbed his head and shouted:

     – Devils! They've desecrated it, defiled it, frozen it! The garden is ruined! The garden is lost!

     But Kovrín worked with the same diligence as before and did not notice the commotion. Love only added fuel to the fire. After each meeting with Tanya, he, happy and enthusiastic, would go to his room and with the same passion with which he had just kissed Tanya and declared his love to her, would take up a book or his manuscript. What the black monk had said about God's chosen ones, eternal truth, about the brilliant future of mankind, and so forth, gave his work a special, extraordinary significance and filled his soul with pride, with a consciousness of his own superiority. Once or twice a week, in the park or in the house, he would meet the black monk and converse with him at length, but this did not frighten him; on the contrary, it delighted him, for he was already firmly convinced that such visions visited only the chosen, outstanding people who had devoted themselves to the service of an idea.

     Once the monk appeared during dinner and sat in the dining room by the window. Kovrín was delighted and very skillfully started a conversation with Yegor Semyonich and Tanya about things that might interest the monk; the black guest listened and nodded his head affably, and Yegor Semyonich and Tanya also listened and smiled cheerfully, not suspecting that Kovrín was speaking not to them, but to his hallucination.

     Imperceptibly the Assumption fast approached, and soon after it came the wedding day, which, at Yegor Semyonich's insistent wish, was celebrated "with a bang," that is, with a senseless revelry that lasted two days. They ate and drank about three thousand rubles' worth, but from the poor hired music, loud toasts, and servants running about, from the noise and crowding, they could not appreciate the taste of the expensive wines or the wonderful appetizers ordered from Moscow.

VII

One night during a long winter evening, Kovrín lay in bed reading a French novel. Poor Tanya, whose head ached in the evenings from the unaccustomed life in the city, had long since fallen asleep and occasionally uttered some incoherent phrases in her delirium.

     Three o'clock struck. Kovrín extinguished the candle and lay down; he lay for a long time with his eyes closed, but could not fall asleep because, as it seemed to him, the bedroom was very hot and Tanya was delirious. At half past four he lit the candle again, and at that moment he saw the black monk sitting in an armchair near the bed.

     – Greetings, – said the monk, and after a brief silence asked: – What are you thinking about now?

     – About fame, – answered Kovrín. – In the French novel I was just reading, there is depicted a young scholar who does foolish things and wastes away from longing for fame. I do not understand this longing.

     – Because you are intelligent. You regard fame with indifference, as a toy that does not interest you.

     – Yes, that is true.

     – Fame does not smile upon you. What is flattering, or amusing, or instructive in the fact that your name will be carved on a tombstone and then time will erase this inscription along with the gilding? And besides, fortunately, there are too many of you for weak human memory to retain your names.

     – I understand, – Kovrín agreed. – And why should they remember them? But let us talk about something else. For example, about happiness. What is happiness?

     When the clock struck five, he was sitting on the bed, his legs dangling over the carpet, and speaking to the monk:

     – In ancient times a happy man in the end became frightened of his happiness – it was so great! – and, to appease the gods, sacrificed his favorite ring to them. Do you know? And I, like Polycrates, am beginning to be a little troubled by my happiness. It seems strange to me that from morning to night I experience only joy, it fills me completely and drowns out all other feelings. I do not know what sadness, sorrow, or boredom is. Here I am not sleeping, I have insomnia, but I am not bored. Seriously speaking: I am beginning to wonder.

     – But why? – the monk was astonished. – Is joy a supernatural feeling? Should it not be the normal state of man? The higher a man is in mental and moral development, the freer he is, the greater pleasure life brings him. Socrates,Diogenes and Marcus Aurelius experienced joy, not sorrow. And the apostle says: rejoice always. Rejoice then and be happy.

     – But what if the gods become angry? – Kovrin joked and laughed. – If they take away my comfort and force me to freeze and starve, then I hardly think I would enjoy that.

     Tanya meanwhile woke up and looked at her husband with amazement and horror. He was speaking, addressing the armchair, gesticulating and laughing: his eyes were shining and there was something strange in his laughter.

     – Andryusha, who are you talking to? – she asked, grabbing his hand, which he had extended toward the monk. – Andryusha! To whom?

     – What? To whom? – Kovrin became confused. – To him… He is sitting there, – he said, pointing at the black monk.

     – There is no one here… no one! Andryusha, you are ill!

     Tanya embraced her husband and pressed herself against him, as if protecting him from visions, and covered his eyes with her hand.

     – You are ill! – she sobbed, trembling all over. – Forgive me, my dear, my darling, but I have long noticed that your soul is troubled by something… You are mentally ill, Andryusha…

     Her trembling communicated itself to him. He glanced once more at the armchair, which was now empty, suddenly felt weakness in his arms and legs, became frightened and began to dress.

     – It is nothing, Tanya, nothing… – he muttered, trembling. – In truth I am somewhat unwell… it is time I admitted it.

     – I have long noticed… and papa noticed, – she said, trying to restrain her sobs. – You talk to yourself, smile in such a strange way… you do not sleep. Oh, my God, my God, save us! – she said in horror. – But do not be afraid, Andryusha, do not be afraid, for God's sake, do not be afraid…

     She too began to dress. Only now, looking at her, Kovrin understood the full danger of his situation, understood what the black monk and conversations with him meant. For him it was now clear that he was mad.

     Both, not knowing why themselves, dressed and went to the hall: she ahead, he behind her. There, awakened by the sobs, in his dressing gown and holding a candle in his hand, stood Egor Semyonich, who was visiting them.

     – Do not be afraid, Andryusha, – Tanya said, trembling as if in fever, – do not be afraid… Papa, it will all pass… it will all pass…

     Kovrin could not speak from agitation. He wanted to say to his father-in-law in a joking tone:

     – Congratulate me, I seem to have gone mad, – but only moved his lips and smiled bitterly.

     At nine o'clock in the morning they put a coat and fur coat on him, wrapped him in a shawl and took him by carriage to the doctor. He began treatment.

VIII

Summer came again, and the doctor ordered him to go to the country. Kovrin had already recovered, stopped seeing the black monk, and all that remained was to restore his physical strength. Living at his father-in-law's in the country, he drank much milk, worked only two hours a day, did not drink wine and did not smoke.

     Around Ilyin Day in the evening, an all-night vigil was held in the house. When the deacon handed the censer to the priest, the old vast hall smelled exactly like a cemetery, and Kovrin became bored. He went out into the garden. Not noticing the luxurious flowers, he walked through the garden, sat on a bench, then strolled through the park; reaching the river, he went down and stood there in thought, looking at the water. The gloomy pines with shaggy roots, which last year saw him here so young, joyful and vigorous, now did not whisper, but stood motionless and silent, as if they did not recognize him. And indeed, his head was closely cropped, the long beautiful hair was gone, his gait was listless, his face, compared to last summer, had become fuller and paler.

     Crossing by the planks to the other shore. There, where rye had been last year, now lay mown oats in rows. The sun had already set, and on the horizon burned a wide red glow, which promised windy weather for tomorrow. It was quiet. Looking in the direction where the black monk had first appeared last year, Kovrin stood for about twenty minutes until the evening glow began to fade…

     When he, listless and dissatisfied, returned home, the all-night vigil was already over. Egor Semyonich and Tanya were sitting on the terrace steps and drinking tea. They were talking about something, but, seeing Kovrin, suddenly fell silent, and he concluded from their faces that the conversation had been about him.

     – It seems to you,It's time to drink milk already, – Tanya said to her husband.

     – No, it's not time… – he replied, sitting down on the lowest step. – Drink it yourself. I don't want to.

     Tanya exchanged an anxious glance with her father and said in a guilty voice:

     – You yourself notice that milk is good for you.

     – Yes, very good! – Kovrin smirked. – I congratulate you: since Friday I've gained another pound. – He pressed his head tightly with his hands and spoke with anguish: – Why, why did you treat me? Bromide preparations, idleness, warm baths, supervision, cowardly fear for every sip, for every step – all this will eventually drive me to idiocy. I was going mad, I had delusions of grandeur, but in return I was cheerful, vigorous and even happy, I was interesting and original. Now I've become more reasonable and respectable, but in return I'm like everyone else: I'm mediocre, I'm bored with life… Oh, how cruelly you've treated me! I saw hallucinations, but who did it bother? I ask: who did it bother?

     – God knows what you're saying! – sighed Egor Semyonich. – It's even boring to listen to.

     – Then don't listen.

     The presence of people, especially Egor Semyonich, now irritated Kovrin; he answered him dryly, coldly and even rudely and looked at him only mockingly and with hatred, while Egor Semyonich became confused and guiltily coughed, though he felt no guilt whatsoever. Not understanding why their kind, good-natured relations had changed so sharply, Tanya clung to her father and looked anxiously into his eyes; she wanted to understand but could not, and it was clear to her only that the relations were getting worse with each day, that her father had aged greatly of late, and her husband had become irritable, capricious, fault-finding and uninteresting. She could no longer laugh or sing, ate nothing at dinner, did not sleep whole nights, waiting for something terrible, and was so tormented that once she lay in a faint from afternoon until evening. During the all-night vigil it seemed to her that her father was crying, and now, when the three of them sat on the terrace, she made an effort not to think about it.

     – How happy Buddha and Mohammed or Shakespeare were that kind relatives and doctors did not treat them for ecstasy and inspiration! – said Kovrin. – If Mohammed had taken potassium bromide for his nerves, worked only two hours a day and drunk milk, then after this remarkable man there would have remained as little as after his dog. Doctors and kind relatives will eventually make humanity stupid, mediocrity will be considered genius and civilization will perish. If you only knew, – said Kovrin with annoyance, – how grateful I am to you!

     He felt a strong irritation and, to avoid saying too much, quickly got up and went into the house. It was quiet, and the aroma of tobacco and jalap wafted in through the open windows from the garden. In the enormous dark hall, moonlight lay on the floor and on the piano in green patches. Kovrin recalled the raptures of last summer, when it smelled the same of jalap and the moon shone in the windows. To recapture last year's mood, he quickly went to his study, lit a strong cigar and ordered the footman to bring wine. But the cigar tasted bitter and unpleasant in his mouth, and the wine did not taste the same as last year. And what does it mean to lose the habit! From the cigar and two sips of wine his head began to spin and his heart to race, so that he had to take potassium bromide.

     Before going to bed, Tanya said to him:

     – Father adores you. You're angry with him for something, and it's killing him. Look: he's aging not by days but by hours. I beg you, Andryusha, for God's sake, for the sake of your late father, for my peace of mind, be kind to him!

     – I can't and I won't.

     – But why? – asked Tanya, beginning to tremble all over. – Explain to me, why?

     – Because I don't like him, that's all, – Kovrin said carelessly and shrugged his shoulders, – but let's not talk about him: he's your father.

     – I can't, I can't understand! – said Tanya, pressing her temples and staring at one point. – Something incomprehensible, terrible is happening in our house. You've changed, you're not yourself… You, intelligent,an extraordinary man, you get irritated over trifles, you meddle in petty squabbles… Such insignificant things trouble you that sometimes you simply marvel and cannot believe: is that really you? Well, well, don't be angry, don't be angry, – she continued, frightened by her own words and kissing his hands. – You are intelligent, kind, noble. You will be fair to father. He is such a good man!

     – He is not good, but good-natured. Vaudeville uncles, like your father, with their well-fed good-natured faces, extraordinarily hospitable and eccentric, once touched my heart and made me laugh both in stories and in vaudevilles and in life, but now they repel me. They are egoists to the core. What repels me most is their satiation and this gastric, purely bovine or porcine optimism.

     Tanya sat on the bed and laid her head on the pillow.

     – This is torture, – she said, and from her voice it was evident that she was already extremely exhausted and that it was difficult for her to speak. – Since winter itself, not a single peaceful moment… This is terrible, my God! I am suffering…

     – Yes, of course, I am Herod, and you and your papa are Egyptian infants. Of course!

     His face seemed to Tanya ugly and unpleasant. Hatred and a mocking expression did not suit him. And even before she had noticed that something was already missing from his face, as if since he had cut his hair, his face too had changed. She wanted to say something hurtful to him, but immediately she caught herself in this feeling of aversion, became frightened and left the bedroom.

IX

Kovrin received an independent professorship. The inaugural lecture was scheduled for December second and an announcement about this was posted in the university corridor. But on the appointed day he notified the student inspector by telegram that he would not give lectures due to illness.

     He was coughing up blood. He spat blood, but it happened about twice a month that it flowed abundantly, and then he became extremely weak and fell into a drowsy state. This illness did not particularly frighten him, as he knew that his late mother had lived with exactly the same illness for ten years, even longer; and the doctors assured him that it was not dangerous, and advised only not to worry, to lead a regular life and to speak less.

     In January the lecture did not take place again for the same reason, and in February it was already too late to begin the course. It had to be postponed until next year.

     He was now living not with Tanya, but with another woman who was two years older than him and cared for him like a child. His mood was peaceful, submissive: he willingly obeyed, and when Varvara Nikolaevna – that was what his companion was called – decided to take him to the Crimea, he agreed, although he had a premonition that nothing good would come of this journey.

     They arrived in Sevastopol in the evening and stopped at a hotel to rest and travel to Yalta the next day. Both were exhausted from the journey. Varvara Nikolaevna drank some tea, lay down to sleep and soon fell asleep. But Kovrin did not go to bed. Still at home, an hour before leaving for the station, he had received a letter from Tanya and did not dare to open it, and now it lay in his side pocket, and the thought of it troubled him unpleasantly. Sincerely, in the depths of his soul, his marriage to Tanya he now considered a mistake, was glad that he had finally parted with her, and the memory of this woman, who in the end had turned into a walking living relic, and in whom, it seemed, everything was already dead except for large, intently gazing, intelligent eyes, the memory of her aroused in him only pity and annoyance at himself. The handwriting on the envelope reminded him how two years ago he had been unjust and cruel, how he had vented on innocent people his spiritual emptiness, boredom, loneliness and dissatisfaction with life. By the way, he recalled how once he had torn his dissertation into small pieces and all the articles written during his illness, and how he had thrown them out the window, and the pieces, flying in the wind, caught on trees and flowers; in every line he saw strange, unfounded pretensions, frivolous audacity, impudence, megalomania, and this made such an impression on him as if he were reading a description of his own vices;but when the last notebook was torn and flew out the window, for some reason he suddenly felt annoyed and bitter, he went to his wife and said harsh words to her

    VOLODYA

    One summer Sunday, at about five in the evening, Volodya, a seventeen-year-old youth, plain, sickly and timid, sat in the arbor at the Shumihins' dacha and was bored. His cheerless thoughts flowed in three directions. First, the next day, Monday, he had to take an exam in mathematics; he knew that if he failed to solve the written problem tomorrow, he would be expelled, for he had been in the sixth grade for two years and had an annual mark of 2 3/4 in algebra. Second, his stay at the Shumihins', people who were wealthy and pretended to aristocracy, constantly pained his pride. It seemed to him that Mme Shumihin and her nieces looked upon him and his maman as poor relations and parasites, that they did not respect maman and laughed at her. Once he accidentally overheard Mme Shumihin say on the terrace to her cousin Anna Feodorovna that his maman kept trying to look young and doll herself up, that she never paid her gambling losses and had a weakness for other people's shoes and tobacco. Every day Volodya begged maman not to go to the Shumihins', described to her what an offensive role she played among these people, tried to convince her, said harsh things, but she, frivolous, spoiled, having squandered two fortunes in her lifetime — her own and her husband's — always gravitating toward high society, did not understand him, and Volodya had to accompany her to the hateful dacha two or three times a week.

    Third, the youth could not for a moment shake off a strange, unpleasant feeling that was completely new to him... It seemed to him that he was in love with the Shumihins' cousin and guest, Anna Feodorovna. She was a lively, loud-voiced, giggly little lady, about thirty, healthy, sturdy, rosy, with round shoulders, a round, plump chin, and a constant smile on her thin lips. She was not pretty and not young — Volodya knew that perfectly well — but for some reason he could not help thinking about her, could not help looking at her when she, playing croquet, shrugged her round shoulders and moved her smooth back, or when, after a long fit of laughter and running up and down the stairs, she fell into an armchair and, closing her eyes, breathing heavily, pretended that her chest was tight and stifling. She was married. Her husband, a solid architect, came to the dacha once a week, slept very well, and went back to town. Volodya's strange feeling began with him hating this architect for no reason and rejoicing every time the man left for town.

    Now, sitting in the arbor and thinking about tomorrow's exam and about maman, who was laughed at, he felt a strong desire to see Nyuta (as the Shumihins called Anna Feodorovna), to hear her laugh, the rustle of her dress... This desire was unlike that pure, poetic love which he knew from novels and which he dreamed about every evening before falling asleep; it was strange, incomprehensible, he was ashamed of it and afraid of it, as of something very bad and unclean, something it was hard to admit even to himself...

    "This is not love," he told himself. "People don't fall in love with thirty-year-old married women... This is just a little intrigue... Yes, an intrigue..."

    Thinking about the intrigue, he remembered his invincible timidity, his lack of a mustache, his freckles, his narrow eyes, pictured himself in his imagination next to Nyuta — and this pair seemed impossible to him; then he hastened to imagine himself handsome, bold, witty, dressed in the very latest fashion...

    At the height of his daydreams, when he, hunched over and staring at the ground, sat in the dark corner of the arbor, light footsteps were heard. Someone was walking unhurriedly along the path. Soon the footsteps died down and something white flashed at the entrance.

    "Is anyone here?" asked a woman's voice.

    Volodya recognized the voice and raised his head in fright.

    "Who's here?" Nyuta asked, entering the arbor. "Ah, it's you, Volodya? What are you doing here? Thinking? And how can you just keep thinking, thinking, thinking... you might go mad that way!"

    Volodya stood up and looked at Nyuta in confusion. She had just returned from the bathhouse. Over her shoulders hung a sheet and a fluffy towel,And from under the white silk scarf on her head, wet hair peeked out, plastered to her forehead. She exuded a damp, cool scent of the bathhouse and almond soap. She was out of breath from walking quickly. The top button of her blouse was undone, so the young man could see both her neck and chest.

"Why are you silent?" Nyuta asked, looking Volodya over. "It's impolite to be silent when a lady is speaking to you. What a seal you are, though, Volodya! You just sit there, silent, thinking like some kind of philosopher. There's no life or fire in you at all! You're really insufferable... At your age, you should be living, jumping, chatting, courting women, falling in love."

Volodya looked at the sheet, held up by a white, plump hand, and thought...

"Silent!" Nyuta marveled. "That's even strange... Listen, be a man! Well, at least smile! Ugh, you insufferable philosopher!" she laughed. "And you know, Volodya, why you're such a seal? Because you don't court women. Why don't you court them? It's true, there are no young ladies here, but nothing stops you from courting married women! Why don't you court me, for example?"

Volodya listened and, in heavy, tense thought, scratched his temple.

"Only very proud people are silent and love solitude," Nyuta continued, pulling his hand away from his temple. "You're a proud man, Volodya. Why do you scowl? Look me straight in the face! Come on, you seal!"

Volodya decided to speak. Wanting to smile, he twitched his lower lip, blinked his eyes, and again reached his hand to his temple.

"I... I love you!" he said.

Nyuta raised her eyebrows in surprise and laughed.

"What do I hear?!" she sang, as opera singers sing when they hear something terrible. "What? What did you say? Repeat it, repeat it..."

"I... I love you!" Volodya repeated.

And without any will of his own, understanding or comprehending nothing, he took a half-step towards Nyuta and took her by the hand above the wrist. His eyes grew dim and tears appeared, the whole world turned into one large, fuzzy towel that smelled of the bathhouse.

"Bravo, bravo!" he heard cheerful laughter. "Why are you silent? I want you to talk! Well?"

Seeing that he was not being stopped from holding her hand, Volodya looked at Nyuta's laughing face and clumsily, awkwardly took her by the waist with both hands, with his hands meeting on her back. He held her by the waist with both hands, while she, throwing her hands behind her head and showing the dimples on her elbows, adjusted her hairstyle under the scarf and said in a calm voice:

"You need to be agile, courteous, charming, Volodya, and one can only become that under the influence of female company. However, what an unpleasant... angry face you have. You need to talk, laugh... Yes, Volodya, don't be a sourpuss, you're young and you'll have plenty of time to philosophize later. Well, let me go, I'm leaving. Let go!"

She freed her waist without effort and, humming something, left the gazebo. Volodya was left alone. He smoothed his hair, smiled, and paced back and forth a few times, then sat down on the bench and smiled again. He was unbearably ashamed, so much so that he was even surprised that human shame could reach such intensity and strength. From shame, he smiled, whispered some incoherent words, and gesticulated.

He was ashamed that he had just been treated like a boy, ashamed of his timidity, and most of all, that he had dared to take a respectable married woman by the waist, although, as it seemed to him, by age, appearance, or social standing, he had no right to do so.

He jumped up, left the gazebo, and without looking back, went deep into the garden, away from the house.

"Oh, if only I could get out of here quickly!" he thought, clutching his head. "God, if only I could get out quickly!"

The train on which Volodya and maman were to leave departed at eight-forty. There were about three hours left until the train, but he would have gladly gone to the station right then, without waiting for maman.

At around seven o'clock, he approached the house. His whole figure expressed determination: come what may! He decided to enter boldly, to look straight ahead,Speak loudly, no matter what.

He walked across the terrace, the large hall, and the drawing room, stopping in the last one to catch his breath. From there, he could hear them drinking tea in the adjacent dining room. Madame Shumikhina, maman, and Nyuta were talking and laughing about something.

Volodya listened.

"I assure you!" Nyuta was saying. "I couldn't believe my eyes! When he started confessing his love to me and even, imagine, took me by the waist, I didn't recognize him. And you know, he has a certain manner! When he said he was in love with me, there was something wild in his face, like a Circassian."

"Really!" gasped maman, breaking into a drawn-out laugh. "Really! How he reminds me of his father!"

Volodya ran back and dashed out into the fresh air. "How can they talk about this out loud!" he agonized, throwing up his hands and looking at the sky in horror. "They talk about it aloud, cold-bloodedly... And maman laughed... maman! My God, why did you give me such a mother? Why?"

But he had to go back into the house, no matter what. He walked along the alley three times, calmed down a bit, and entered the house.

"Why aren't you coming in time for tea?" asked Madame Shumikhina sternly.

"I'm sorry, I... I have to go," he muttered without raising his eyes. "Maman, it's already eight o'clock!"

"Go on your own, my dear," said maman languidly, "I'm staying overnight with Lily. Goodbye, my friend... Let me cross you..."

She crossed her son and said in French, turning to Nyuta:

"He looks a bit like Lermontov... Doesn't he?"

Having barely said his goodbyes without looking at anyone's face, Volodya left the dining room. Ten minutes later, he was already walking along the road to the station and was glad of it. Now he felt neither fear nor shame; he breathed easily and freely.

Half a mile from the station, he sat down on a stone by the roadside and began to look at the sun, which was more than half hidden behind the embankment. At the station, lights were already flickering here and there, one dim green light flashing, but the train was not yet in sight. Volodya found it pleasant to sit still and listen as evening gradually set in. The twilight of the gazebo, the footsteps, the smell of the bathhouse, the laughter and the waist—all of this appeared in his imagination with astonishing clarity, and none of it seemed as frightening or significant as before...

"Nonsense... She didn't pull her hand away and laughed when I held her by the waist," he thought, "so she must have liked it. If she had found it unpleasant, she would have gotten angry..."

And now Volodya felt annoyed that back there in the gazebo, he hadn't been bold enough. He regretted leaving so foolishly, and he was now confident that if that situation were to happen again, he would be braver and take things more simply.

And it wouldn't be hard for the situation to repeat itself. At the Shumikhins', they often took long walks after supper. If Volodya went for a walk with Nyuta through the dark garden, then—there was his chance!

"I'll go back," he thought, "and leave tomorrow on the morning train... I'll say I missed the train."

And he went back... Madame Shumikhina, maman, Nyuta, and one of the nieces were sitting on the terrace playing bridge. When Volodya lied to them that he had missed the train, they worried that he might be late for his exam tomorrow and advised him to get up early. All the while they played cards, he sat to the side, greedily watching Nyuta and waiting... In his head, a plan was already forming: he would approach Nyuta in the dark, take her hand, then embrace her; there was no need to say anything, since everything would be clear to both of them without words.

But after supper, the ladies did not go for a walk in the garden and continued playing cards. They played until one in the morning and then went to bed.

"How stupid all this is!" Volodya fumed as he lay down in bed. "But never mind, I'll wait for tomorrow... Tomorrow, again in the gazebo. Never mind..."

He didn't try to fall asleep but sat up in bed, hugging his knees, and thought. The thought of the exam was repulsive to him. He had already decided that he would be expelled and that there was nothing terrible about that expulsion. On the contrary, everything was very good, even excellent. Tomorrow he would be free as a bird, would put on civilian clothes, would smoke openly, come here, and court Nyuta.whenever; and he will no longer be a schoolboy, but a "young man." And as for the rest, the so-called career and future, it's so clear: Volodya will enlist as a volunteer, become a telegraphist, or finally work in a pharmacy, where he'll work his way up to a pharmacist's assistant... aren't there plenty of jobs? An hour or two passed, but he still sat there thinking...

Around two in the morning, when it was already getting light, the door creaked cautiously and maman entered the room.

"Aren't you asleep?" she asked, yawning. "Sleep, sleep, I'm just a moment... I'll just get some drops..."

"What do you need them for?"

"Poor Lili is having spasms again. Sleep, my child, you have an exam tomorrow..."

She took a vial of something from the cupboard, went to the window, read the label, and left.

"Marya Leontyevna, those aren't the right drops!" Volodya heard a woman's voice a minute later. "That's lily of the valley, but Lili asked for morphine. Is your son asleep? Ask him to find it..."

It was Nyuta's voice. Volodya turned cold. He quickly pulled on his trousers, threw his overcoat over his shoulders, and walked to the door.

"Do you understand? Morphine!" Nyuta explained in a whisper. "It should be written in Latin. Wake Volodya, he'll find it..."

Maman opened the door, and Volodya saw Nyuta. She was wearing the same blouse she wore when she went swimming. Her hair was uncombed, scattered over her shoulders, her face sleepy, dusky in the twilight...

"Volodya isn't asleep..." she said. "Volodya, darling, look for the morphine in the cupboard! What a trial this Lili is... She always has something."

Maman muttered something, yawned, and left.

"Look for it, will you," said Nyuta. "Why are you standing there?"

Volodya went to the cupboard, knelt down, and began sorting through the vials and medicine boxes. His hands were trembling, and in his chest and stomach, he felt as if cold waves were running through his insides. The smell of ether, carbolic acid, and various herbs—which he grabbed for no reason with his trembling hands and which scattered because of that—made him feel suffocated and dizzy.

"It seems maman has left," he thought. "That's good... good..."

"Will it be soon?" Nyuta asked, drawling.

"Right away... This, I think, is morphine..." said Volodya, reading the word "morph..." on one of the labels. "Here you are!"

Nyuta stood in the doorway so that one foot was in the corridor and the other in his room. She was tidying her hair, which was hard to fix because it was so thick and long!—and looked at Volodya absently. In her loose blouse, sleepy, with her hair down, in the scant light coming into the room from the white, still sunless sky, she seemed charming, magnificent to Volodya... Enchanted, trembling all over, and recalling with delight how he had embraced that wonderful body in the gazebo, he handed her the drops and said:

"How you..."

"What?"

She entered the room.

"What?" she asked, smiling.

He was silent and looked at her, then, just like in the gazebo, took her hand... And she looked at him, smiled, and waited: what would happen next?

"I love you..." he whispered.

She stopped smiling, thought, and said:

"Wait, I think someone's coming. Oh, these schoolboys of mine!" she said under her breath, going to the door and peeking into the corridor. "No, no one's there..."

She returned...

Then it seemed to Volodya that the room, Nyuta, the dawn, and he himself—all merged into one feeling of acute, extraordinary, unprecedented happiness, for which one could give up one's whole life and go to eternal torment, but half a minute passed, and everything suddenly vanished. Volodya saw only a full, unattractive face, distorted with an expression of disgust, and he himself suddenly felt revulsion for what had happened.

"However, I need to go," said Nyuta, looking at Volodya with disgust. "How ugly, pathetic you look... ugh, ugly duckling!"

How ugly her long hair, her loose blouse, her steps, her voice now seemed to Volodya!..

"Ugly duckling..." he thought after she left. "I really am ugly... Everything is ugly."

Outside, the sun was already rising, birds were singing loudly; there was heard,as the gardener paced in the orchard and his wheelbarrow creaked... And a little later, the lowing of cows and the sound of a shepherd's pipe were heard. The sunlight and the sounds seemed to say that somewhere in this world there existed a life that was pure, elegant, and poetic. But where was it? Neither Maman nor any of the people surrounding him had ever spoken to Volodya of such a life.

When the footman woke him for the morning train, he pretended to be asleep...

"To hell with it all!" he thought.

He got out of bed around eleven o'clock. Combing his hair in front of the mirror and looking at his plain, pale face, haggard from a sleepless night, he thought:

"Absolutely right... An ugly duckling."

When Maman saw him and was horrified that he wasn't at his exam, Volodya said:

"I overslept, Maman... But don't worry, I'll get a medical certificate."

Madame Shumikhina and Nyuta woke up around one o'clock. Volodya heard how, upon waking, Madame Shumikhina noisily opened her window, and how Nyuta responded to her gruff voice with a peal of laughter. He saw the door open and a procession of nieces and dependent hangers-on (among the latter was his Maman) shuffle through to breakfast, saw Nyuta's washed, laughing face flash by, and next to her face, the black eyebrows and beard of the newly arrived architect.

Nyuta was wearing a Little Russian costume, which did not suit her at all and made her look clumsy; the architect made vulgar, insipid jokes; the cutlets served for breakfast had far too much onion – or so it seemed to Volodya. It also seemed to him that Nyuta laughed on purpose, loudly, and kept glancing his way, as if to make it clear that the memory of the night didn't bother her in the slightest, and that she didn't notice the ugly duckling's presence at the table.

Around four o'clock, Volodya was riding with his Maman to the station. Filthy memories, a sleepless night, impending expulsion from the gymnasium, pangs of conscience – all of this now stirred in him a heavy, sullen rage. He looked at his Maman's thin profile, her little nose, the waterproof coat Nyuta had given her, and muttered:

"Why do you powder your face? It's unbecoming at your age! You doll yourself up, you don't pay your gambling debts, you smoke other people's tobacco... it's disgusting! I don't love you... I don't love you!"

He insulted her, and she, frightened, would dart her little eyes about, wring her hands, and whisper in terror:

"What are you saying, my dear? My God, the coachman will hear! Be quiet, or the coachman will hear! He can hear everything!"

"I don't love you... I don't love you!" he continued, gasping. "You are immoral, heartless... Don't you dare wear that waterproof coat! Do you hear? Or I'll tear it to shreds..."

"Come to your senses, my child!" Maman wept. "The coachman will hear!"

"And where is my father's fortune? Where is your money? You've squandered it all! I'm not ashamed of being poor, but I am ashamed that I have such a mother... When my comrades ask about you, I always blush."

On the train, they had to travel two stations to get to the city. Volodya stood on the platform the entire time, trembling all over. He didn't want to go inside the carriage because his mother, whom he hated, was sitting there. He hated himself, the conductors, the steam from the locomotive, the cold, to which he attributed his trembling... And the heavier his heart became, the more strongly he felt that somewhere in this world, with some people, there was a life that was pure, noble, warm, elegant, full of love, caresses, merriment, and freedom... He felt this and yearned so intensely that even a fellow passenger, staring intently at his face, asked:

"Probably have a toothache?"

In the city, Maman and Volodya lived with Marya Petrovna, a lady of the gentry who rented a large apartment and sublet rooms from it. Maman rented two rooms: in one, with windows, where her bed stood and two paintings in gold frames hung on the walls, she lived herself; and in the other, adjacent, small, and dark, Volodya lived. There was a couch there on which he slept, and besides this couch, there was no other furniture; the entire room was filled with wicker baskets of clothes, hat boxes, and all sorts of junk that Maman kept for some reason.Volodya prepared his lessons in his mother's room or in the "common room" — that was the name for the large room where all the tenants gathered during dinner and in the evenings.
Ionych

I

When visitors to the provincial town of S. complained about the boredom and monotony of life, the local inhabitants, as if in justification, would say that, on the contrary, things were very good in S., that there was a library, a theatre, a club, there were balls, and finally, there were clever, interesting, agreeable families with whom one could make acquaintances. And they pointed to the Turkin family as the most educated and talented.

This family lived on the main street, near the governor, in their own house. Turkin himself, Ivan Petrovich, a stout, handsome brunet with side-whiskers, staged amateur performances for charitable purposes, playing old generals himself and coughing very comically in the process. He knew many anecdotes, charades, and sayings, loved to joke and make witty remarks, and he always had such an expression that it was impossible to tell whether he was joking or speaking seriously. His wife, Vera Iosifovna, a thin, pretty lady in pince-nez, wrote novels and stories and willingly read them aloud to her guests. Their daughter, Yekaterina Ivanovna, a young girl, played the piano. In short, each member of the family had some talent of their own. The Turkins received guests hospitably and cheerfully displayed their talents with heartfelt simplicity. Their large stone house was spacious and cool in summer, half of the windows looking out onto an old shady garden where nightingales sang in spring; when guests were sitting in the house, knives were clattering in the kitchen and the smell of fried onions drifted through the yard – and this always heralded a plentiful and tasty supper.

And Doctor Startsev, Dmitry Ionych, when he was just appointed as a district doctor and settled in Dyalizh, nine versts from S., was also told that, as an intellectual person, he absolutely had to get acquainted with the Turkins. One winter he was introduced to Ivan Petrovich on the street; they talked about the weather, about the theatre, about cholera, and an invitation followed. In spring, on a holiday – it was Ascension Day – after seeing his patients, Startsev went to town to relax a little and also buy a few things. He walked, without hurrying (he did not have his own horses yet), and kept humming: "When I have not yet drunk tears from the cup of being..."

In town he had dinner, took a walk in the garden, and then the invitation from Ivan Petrovich somehow came to mind on its own, and he decided to go to the Turkins' to see what kind of people they were.

"Welcome, welcome," said Ivan Petrovich, meeting him on the porch. "Very, very glad to see such a pleasant guest. Come along, I'll introduce you to my better half. I tell him, Verochka," he continued, presenting the doctor to his wife, "I tell him that he has no Roman right whatsoever to sit in his hospital; he must devote his leisure time to society. Isn't that true, my dear?"

"Sit here," said Vera Iosifovna, seating the guest beside her. "You may court me. My husband is jealous, he's Othello, but we shall try to behave in such a way that he notices nothing."

"Oh, you little chick, you spoilt thing..." Ivan Petrovich murmured tenderly and kissed her on the forehead. "You have come very opportunely," he addressed the guest again, "my better half has written a humongous novel and will read it aloud today."

"Zhanchik," Vera Iosifovna said to her husband, "dites que l'on nous donne du thé."

Startsev was introduced to Yekaterina Ivanovna, an eighteen-year-old girl, very much like her mother, just as thin and pretty. Her expression was still childlike and her waist thin, delicate; and her virginal, already developed bust, beautiful and healthy, spoke of spring, of real spring. Then they drank tea with jam, honey, sweets, and very tasty biscuits that melted in the mouth. With the onset of evening, guests gradually gathered, and Ivan Petrovich turned his laughing eyes to each of them and said:

"Welcome, welcome."

Then everyone sat in the drawing-room with very serious faces, and Vera Iosifovna read her novel. She began like this: "The frost grew stronger..." The windows were wide open; the clatter of knives in the kitchen could be heard, and the smell of fried onions drifted in... It was comfortable in the soft, deep armchairs; the lights flickered so kindly in the twilight of the drawing-room; and now,On a summer evening, when voices and laughter drifted in from the street and the scent of lilac wafted from the courtyard, it was hard to understand how the frost could be strengthening and how the setting sun could illumine the snowy plain and the traveler walking alone along the road with its cold rays; Vera Iosifovna was reading about how a young, beautiful countess set up schools, hospitals, and libraries in her village and how she fell in love with a wandering artist – reading about things that never happen in life, and yet it was pleasant and comfortable to listen, and such good, peaceful thoughts came into one's head – one did not want to get up.

"Not bad..." Ivan Petrovich said quietly.

And one of the guests, listening and letting his thoughts drift somewhere very, very far away, said barely audibly:

"Yes... indeed..."

An hour passed, then another. In the neighboring town garden, an orchestra was playing and a choir of singers was singing. When Vera Iosifovna closed her notebook, they were silent for about five minutes and listened to "Luchinushka," which the choir was singing, and this song conveyed what was not in the novel and what happens in life.

"Do you publish your works in magazines?" Startsev asked Vera Iosifovna.

"No," she replied, "I don't publish anywhere. I write and hide it in my cupboard. Why publish?" she explained. "After all, we have the means."

And everyone somehow sighed.

"And now, Kotik, play something," Ivan Petrovich said to his daughter.

They raised the lid of the piano and opened the music that was already lying ready. Ekaterina Ivanovna sat down and struck the keys with both hands; and then immediately struck again with all her might, and again, and again; her shoulders and chest trembled, she stubbornly kept hitting the same spot, and it seemed she would not stop until she drove the keys inside the piano. The drawing-room was filled with thunder; everything thundered: the floor, the ceiling, the furniture... Ekaterina Ivanovna played a difficult passage, interesting precisely for its difficulty, long and monotonous, and Startsev, listening, pictured to himself stones falling from a high mountain, falling and falling, and he wanted them to stop falling as soon as possible, and at the same time Ekaterina Ivanovna, flushed from the strain, strong, energetic, with a lock of hair fallen onto her forehead, greatly pleased him. After the winter spent in Dyalizh, among sick people and peasants, to sit in a drawing-room, look at this young, elegant, and probably pure creature, and listen to these noisy, tiresome, but still cultured sounds – was so pleasant, so new...

"Well, Kotik, today you played like never before," said Ivan Petrovich with tears in his eyes, when his daughter finished and stood up. "Die, Denis, you couldn't write better."

Everyone surrounded her, congratulated her, marveled, assured her that they had not heard such music for a long time, while she listened silently, barely smiling, and triumph was written all over her figure.

"Wonderful! Excellent!"

"Wonderful!" said Startsev as well, yielding to the general enthusiasm. "Where did you study music?" he asked Ekaterina Ivanovna. "At the conservatory?"

"No, I'm only planning to go to the conservatory, but for now I studied here, with Madame Zavlovskaya."

"Did you complete the course at the local gymnasium?"

"Oh no!" Vera Iosifovna answered for her. "We invited teachers to the house; in a gymnasium or institute, you must agree, there could be bad influences; while a girl is growing up, she should be under the influence of her mother alone."

"Still, I will go to the conservatory," said Ekaterina Ivanovna.

"No, Kotik loves her mama. Kotik won't upset papa and mama."

"No, I'll go! I'll go!" said Ekaterina Ivanovna, jokingly and capriciously, and stamped her foot.

And at supper, Ivan Petrovich showed off his talents again. Laughing only with his eyes, he told anecdotes, made witty remarks, proposed funny problems and solved them himself, and spoke all the time in his extraordinary language, developed by long exercises in wit and, evidently, long since become a habit: mostial, not bad, I humbly thank you...

But that was not all. When the guests, full and satisfied, crowded in the hallway, sorting out their coats and canes, the footman Pavlusha bustled around them, or,Here, his name was Pava, a boy of about fourteen, with a crew cut and chubby cheeks.

"Ay, come on, Pava, do your thing!" Ivan Petrovich said to him.

Pava struck a pose, raised his hand, and said in a tragic tone:

"Die, wretched woman!"

And everyone burst out laughing.

"Interesting," thought Startsev as he stepped out into the street.

He stopped by the restaurant for some beer, then walked home to Dyalizh. All the way, he hummed: "Your voice for me, so tender and languid..."

After walking nine versts and then going to bed, he felt not the slightest fatigue; on the contrary, it seemed to him he would gladly have walked another twenty versts.

"Not bad..." he recalled as he fell asleep, and chuckled.

II

Startsev kept meaning to visit the Turkins, but there was too much work at the hospital, and he just couldn't find a free hour. Over a year passed like this, in labor and solitude; then a letter came from town in a blue envelope...

Vera Iosifovna had long suffered from migraines, but lately, when Kotik frightened her daily by threatening to leave for the conservatory, the attacks became more and more frequent. All the town doctors had been to the Turkins; finally, it was the district doctor's turn. Vera Iosifovna wrote him a touching letter, asking him to come and ease her suffering. Startsev came, and after that he began visiting the Turkins often, very often... He did help Vera Iosifovna a little, and she already told all the guests that he was an extraordinary, amazing doctor. But he went to the Turkins not for her migraine anymore...

A holiday. Ekaterina Ivanovna finished her long, tiresome piano exercises. Then they sat for a long time in the dining room drinking tea, and Ivan Petrovich told something funny. But then the doorbell rang; someone had to go to the hall to greet a guest; Startsev seized the moment of confusion and said to Ekaterina Ivanovna in a whisper, greatly agitated:

"For God's sake, I beg you, don't torment me, let's go into the garden!"

She shrugged her shoulders, as if perplexed and not understanding what he wanted from her, but she stood up and went.

"You play the piano for three, four hours at a time," he said, following her, "then you sit with your mother, and there's no way to talk to you. Give me at least a quarter of an hour, I beg you."

Autumn was approaching, and in the old garden it was quiet, sad, and dark leaves lay on the paths. It was already getting dark early.

"I haven't seen you for a whole week," Startsev continued, "and if you only knew what torment that is! Let's sit down. Listen to me."

They both had a favorite spot in the garden: a bench under an old, wide maple tree. And now they sat down on that bench.

"What do you want?" asked Ekaterina Ivanovna dryly, in a businesslike tone.

"I haven't seen you for a whole week, I haven't heard you for so long. I passionately want, I thirst for your voice. Speak."

She enchanted him with her freshness, the naive expression in her eyes and cheeks. Even in the way her dress sat on her, he saw something extraordinarily lovely, touching in its simplicity and naive grace. And at the same time, despite this naivete, she seemed to him very intelligent and developed beyond her years. With her he could talk about literature, about art, about anything, he could complain to her about life, about people, though during a serious conversation, it sometimes happened that she would suddenly start laughing inappropriately or run into the house. She, like almost all the C. girls, read a lot (generally in S. they read very little, and at the local library they even said that if it weren't for the girls and the young Jews, they might as well close the library); this pleased Startsev immensely, he would ask her with excitement every time what she had read in the last few days, and, charmed, he would listen when she told him.

"What did you read this week while we didn't see each other?" he asked now. "Tell me, I beg you."

"I read Pisemsky."

"What exactly?"

"A Thousand Souls," answered Kotik. "And what a funny name Pisemsky had: Alexei Feofilaktich!"

"Where are you going?" Startsev was horrified when she suddenly got up and walked toward the house. "I need to talk to you,"I must explain myself… Stay with me for at least five minutes! I implore you!

She stopped, as if wanting to say something, then awkwardly thrust a note into his hand and ran into the house, where she sat down at the piano again.

"Today, at eleven o'clock in the evening," read Startsev, "be at the cemetery near the Demetti monument."

"Well, that's hardly clever," he thought, coming to his senses. "What does the cemetery have to do with it? Why?"

It was clear: Kotik was fooling around. Who, after all, would seriously think of arranging a rendezvous at night, far outside the city, in a cemetery, when it could easily be arranged on a street or in the town garden? And was it becoming for him, a district doctor, an intelligent, respectable man, to sigh, receive little notes, traipse around cemeteries, and do foolish things that even schoolboys now laugh at? Where would this romance lead? What would his colleagues say when they found out? So thought Startsev, wandering near the tables in the club, yet at half past ten he suddenly up and drove to the cemetery.

He already had his own pair of horses and the coachman Panteleimon in a velvet vest. The moon was shining. It was quiet, warm, but warm in an autumnal way. In the suburb, near the slaughterhouses, dogs were howling. Startsev left his horses at the edge of town, in one of the side streets, and went to the cemetery on foot. "Everyone has their oddities," he thought. "Kotik is also strange and — who knows? — perhaps she isn't joking, she'll come," and he gave himself over to this weak, empty hope, and it intoxicated him.

He walked about half a verst across a field. The cemetery loomed in the distance as a dark strip, like a forest or a large garden. A white stone fence came into view, then gates… In the moonlight, one could read on the gates: "The hour is coming when…" Startsev entered through the wicket gate, and the first thing he saw were white crosses and monuments on both sides of the wide avenue, and the black shadows from them and from the poplars; and all around, far into the distance, white and black were visible, and the sleepy trees bent their branches over the white. It seemed brighter here than in the field; the maple leaves, resembling paws, stood out sharply against the yellow sand of the avenues and the flagstones, and the inscriptions on the monuments were clear. At first, Startsev was struck by what he was seeing now for the first time in his life and what, probably, would never happen again: a world unlike any other — a world where the moonlight is so beautiful and soft, as if here is its cradle, where there is no life, none at all, but in every dark poplar, in every grave, one senses the presence of a mystery promising a quiet, beautiful, eternal life. From the slabs and the withered flowers, along with the autumn scent of leaves, there emanates forgiveness, sadness, and peace.

All around was silence; the stars looked down from the sky in deep humility, and Startsev's footsteps rang out so sharply and incongruously. And only when the church clock began to strike and he imagined himself dead, buried here forever, did it seem to him that someone was watching him, and for a moment he thought that this was not peace or stillness, but the dull anguish of non-existence, suppressed despair…

The Demetti monument was in the form of a chapel, with an angel on top; once, an Italian opera company had passed through S., one of the singers died, she was buried, and this monument was erected. No one in the town remembered her anymore, but the icon lamp above the entrance reflected the moonlight and seemed to be burning.

There was no one. And who would come here at midnight? But Startsev waited, and as if the moonlight stoked his passion, he waited passionately and pictured kisses and embraces in his imagination. He sat near the monument for about half an hour, then walked along the side avenues, hat in hand, waiting and thinking about how many women and girls were buried here in these graves — women who were beautiful, charming, who loved, burned with passion at night, surrendering to caresses. How unkindly mother nature mocks a person, how painful it is to realize this! Startsev thought so, and at the same time he wanted to shout that he wanted, that he was waiting for love at any cost; before him, it was no longer pieces of marble that gleamed white, but beautiful bodies, he saw forms that shyly hid in the shadow of the trees, felt warmth, and this longing became oppressive…

And as if a curtain fell, the moon slipped behind the clouds,And suddenly everything went dark around. Startsev barely found the gate – it was already as dark as an autumn night – and then wandered for about an hour and a half, looking for the lane where he had left his horses.

     "I'm tired, I can barely stand," he said to Pantelaimon.

     And as he sat down in the carriage with pleasure, he thought:

     "Oh, I shouldn't put on weight!"

III

     The next evening he went to the Turkins to propose. But this turned out to be awkward, as Yekaterina Ivanovna was having her hair done by a hairdresser in her room. She was getting ready to go to the club for a dance evening.

     He had to sit in the dining room again for a long time and drink tea. Ivan Petrovich, seeing that the guest was thoughtful and bored, took some notes out of his waistcoat pocket and read a funny letter from the German steward about how all the locks on the estate had broken and the shyness had crumbled away.

     "They must be giving quite a dowry," thought Startsev, listening absentmindedly.

     After a sleepless night, he was in a state of stupefaction, as if he had been drugged with something sweet and soporific; his soul was hazy, but joyful and warm, and at the same time a sort of cold, heavy little piece in his head reasoned:

     "Stop, while it's not too late! Is she a match for you? She's spoiled, capricious, sleeps until two o'clock, and you're a sexton's son, a district doctor..."

     "Well, what of it?" he thought. "So be it."

     "Moreover, if you marry her," the little piece continued, "her relatives will force you to give up your district service and live in town."

     "Well, what of it?" he thought. "Town it is, then town. They'll give a dowry, we'll set up a household..."

     Finally, Yekaterina Ivanovna came in wearing a ball gown, low-cut, pretty, clean, and Startsev admired her and was filled with such delight that he couldn't utter a single word, but only looked at her and laughed.

     She began to say goodbye, and he – there was no longer any reason for him to stay – got up, saying it was time for him to go home: patients were waiting.

     "Nothing to be done," said Ivan Petrovich, "go ahead, you can give Kotik a ride to the club on the way."

     Outside, a light rain was drizzling, it was very dark, and only by Pantelaimon's hoarse cough could one guess where the horses were. They raised the carriage top.

     "I walk on the rug, you walk while you lie," said Ivan Petrovich, seating his daughter in the carriage, "he walks while he lies... Giddy up! Goodbye, please!"

     They set off.

     "I was at the cemetery yesterday," Startsev began. "How ungenerous and unmerciful of you..."

     "Were you at the cemetery?"

     "Yes, I was there and waited for you almost until two o'clock. I suffered..."

     "Then suffer, if you don't understand jokes."

     Yekaterina Ivanovna, pleased that she had played such a clever trick on the man in love and that she was so deeply loved, burst out laughing and suddenly cried out in fright, because at that very moment the horses were turning sharply into the club gates and the carriage tilted. Startsev put his arm around Yekaterina Ivanovna's waist; frightened, she pressed against him, and he couldn't restrain himself and passionately kissed her on the lips, on the chin, and held her tighter.

     "Enough," she said dryly.

     And a moment later she was no longer in the carriage, and a policeman near the illuminated entrance of the club was shouting in a disgusting voice at Pantelaimon:

     "What are you stopping for, you crow? Move on!"

     Startsev went home, but soon returned. Dressed in someone else's tailcoat and a stiff white tie that kept sticking up and seemed about to slip off his collar, he sat at midnight in the club drawing-room and spoke to Yekaterina Ivanovna with enthusiasm:

     "Oh, how little they know who have never loved! It seems to me that no one has yet accurately described love, and it is hardly possible to describe this tender, joyful, tormenting feeling, and whoever has experienced it even once will not try to put it into words. What need for prefaces, descriptions? What need for useless eloquence? My love is boundless... I beg, I implore you," Startsev finally uttered, "be my wife!"

     "Dmitry Ionych," said Yekaterina Ivanovna with a very serious expression, after thinking. "Dmitry Ionych, I am very grateful to you for the honor, I respect you, but..." – she stood up and continued standing – "but, excuse me, I cannot be your wife. Let's talk seriously."Dmitry Ionych, you know, more than anything in life I love art.
