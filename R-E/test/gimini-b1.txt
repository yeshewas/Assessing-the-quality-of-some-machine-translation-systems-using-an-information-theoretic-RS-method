CHAPTER THREE

Meanwhile, Chichikov, in a very contented frame of mind, sat in his chaise, which had long been rolling along the high road. From the preceding chapter it is already clear what constituted the chief object of his taste and inclinations, and therefore it is no wonder that he was soon entirely immersed in it, body and soul. The calculations, estimates, and reflections that flitted across his face were evidently very pleasant, for every minute they left behind traces of a satisfied smirk. Occupied with them, he paid no attention whatever to how his coachman, pleased with the reception given him by Manilov’s house-serfs, was making very sensible remarks to the dapple-gray trace-horse harnessed on the right side. This dapple-gray horse was highly cunning and only made a show of pulling, whereas the bay shaft-horse and the skewbald trace-horse on the left, called the Assessor because he had been acquired from some assessor, worked with all their hearts, so that even in their eyes one could see the pleasure they derived from it.

"Trifle away, trifle away! I’ll out-trifle you!" said Selifan, rising a little and lashing the sluggard with his whip. "You mind your business, you German pantaloon! The bay is a respectable horse, he does his duty, I’ll gladly give him an extra measure, because he is a respectable horse, and the Assessor is a good horse too... Well, well! What are you shaking your ears for? You fool, listen when you’re spoken to! I won’t teach you anything bad, you ignoramus. Look where he’s crawling!" Here he lashed him with the whip again, adding: "Oh, you barbarian! You cursed Bonaparte!" Then he shouted at all three: "Hey, my beauties!" and flicked all three, no longer by way of punishment, but to show that he was pleased with them. Having afforded them this pleasure, he again addressed his speech to the dapple-gray: "You think you can hide your conduct. No, live according to the truth if you want people to show you respect. Now, at the landowner's where we just were, those were good people. I’m glad to have a chat when a man is good; with a good man we are always close friends, bosom buddies; whether to drink tea or have a bite—gladly, if he’s a good man. Everyone shows respect to a good man. Now, our master, everyone respects him because, do you hear, he filled a government service, he’s a Collegiate Councillor..."

Reflecting in this manner, Selifan at last wandered into the most remote abstractions. If Chichikov had listened, he would have learned many details relating personally to himself; but his thoughts were so occupied with their own subject that only a loud clap of thunder forced him to rouse himself and look around. The whole sky was completely covered with clouds, and the dusty post road was sprinkled with drops of rain. At last a second clap of thunder rumbled louder and closer, and the rain suddenly poured down as if from a bucket. At first, taking a slanting direction, it beat against one side of the chaise's hood, then against the other; then, changing its mode of attack and becoming perfectly straight, it drummed directly upon the top of the hood; the spray at last began to reach his face. This forced him to draw the leather curtains with their two little round windows intended for viewing the roadside scenery, and to order Selifan to drive faster.

Selifan, interrupted also in the very middle of his speech, discerned that, indeed, there was no time to lose; he instantly dragged out from under the box-seat some rubbish of gray cloth, put his arms into the sleeves, snatched up the reins, and shouted at his troika, which was barely moving its legs, for it felt a pleasant relaxation from the instructive speeches. But Selifan could by no means remember whether he had passed two turnings or three. Puzzling over and recalling the road somewhat, he guessed that there had been many turnings, all of which he had let pass by. Since a Russian in decisive moments always finds what to do without entering into long deliberations, he turned to the right onto the first crossroad, shouted: "Hey, you honored friends!" and set off at a gallop, thinking little of where the chosen road might lead.

The rain, however, seemed to have settled in for a long time. The dust lying on the road was quickly kneaded into mud, and every minute it became heavier for the horses to drag the chaise. Chichikov was already beginning to grow highly anxious at not seeing Sobakevich’s village for so long. By his calculations, it ought to have arrived long ago. He peered out on all sides, but the darkness was so thick you could gouge your eye out.

"Selifan!" he said at last, leaning out of the chaise.

"What is it, master?" Selifan replied.

"Look out, is the village visible?"

"No, master, it’s nowhere to be seen!" After this, Selifan, waving his whip, struck up a song—not exactly a song, but something so long drawn out that there was no end to it. Everything went into it: all the encouraging and prompting cries with which horses are treated all over Russia from one end to the other; adjectives of every kind without further distinction, whatever first happened to come to his tongue. In this way it reached the point where he finally began to call them secretaries.

Meanwhile, Chichikov began to notice that the chaise was rocking from side to side and dealing him most violent jolts; this made him feel that they had turned off the road and were probably dragging along a ploughed field. Selifan seemed to perceive it himself, but did not say a word.

"What, you scoundrel, what road are you driving on?" said Chichikov.

"Well, master, what’s to be done, the weather being what it is; you can’t see the whip, it’s such pitch darkness!" Saying this, he tilted the chaise so much that Chichikov was obliged to hold on with both hands. Only then did he notice that Selifan had taken a drop too much.

"Hold hard, hold hard, you’ll overturn us!" Chichikov shouted to him.

"No, master, how could I overturn us," said Selifan. "It’s a bad thing to overturn, I know that myself; I won’t overturn by any means." Then he began slightly turning the chaise; he turned it and turned it, and at last upset it completely onto its side. Chichikov splashed into the mud with both hands and feet. Selifan stopped the horses, however; besides, they would have stopped of themselves, for they were utterly exhausted. Such an unforeseen event completely amazed him. Getting down from the box, he stood before the chaise, propped his hands on his hips while his master wallowed in the mud, struggling to get out, and said after some reflection: "Look at that, it did capsize!"

"You are as drunk as a cobbler!" said Chichikov.

"No, master, how could I be drunk! I know that it’s a bad thing to be drunk. I had a talk with a friend, because one can have a talk with a good man, there’s no harm in that; and we had a bite together. A snack is no offense; one can have a snack with a good man."

"And what did I tell you the last time you got drunk? Eh? Have you forgotten?" said Chichikov.

"No, your honor, how could I forget? I know my business already. I know it’s not good to be drunk. I had a talk with a good man, because..."

"When I have you soundly flogged, then you’ll know how to talk with a good man!"

"As your mercy pleases," replied Selifan, consenting to everything, "if it’s to be a flogging, then a flogging; I’m not at all against it. Why not be flogged if it’s for cause? That’s the master's will. It’s necessary to flog, because the muzhik plays the fool, order must be observed. If it’s for cause, then do flog; why not flog?"

To such reasoning the master was completely at a loss for an answer. But at that moment it seemed as if fate itself resolved to take pity on him. A dog's barking was heard in the distance. Delighted, Chichikov gave orders to urge on the horses. A Russian driver has a good scent instead of eyes; because of this it happens that, shutting his eyes, he sometimes bowls along at full speed and always arrives somewhere or other. Selifan, without seeing a wink, directed the horses so straight toward the village that he stopped only when the shafts of the chaise struck against a fence and when there was absolutely nowhere else to go.

Chichikov only noticed through the thick veil of pouring rain something resembling a roof. He sent Selifan to hunt for the gates, which would undoubtedly have taken a long time had there not been in Russia, instead of porters, dashing dogs who announced his arrival so loudly that he put his fingers to his ears. A light glimmered in one little window and reached the fence in a misty stream, showing our travelers the gate. Selifan began to knock, and soon, a small gate opening, some figure covered in a peasant's smock poked out, and master and servant heard a hoarse woman's voice:

"Who is knocking? What are you raising a racket for?"

"Travelers, mother, let us in to spend the night," Chichikov uttered.

"Look how nimble-footed he is," said the old woman, "arriving at such a time! This is no inn: a lady landowner lives here."

"What’s to be done, mother: you see, we’ve lost our way. We can’t spend the night in the steppe in such weather."

"Yes, the weather is dark, bad weather," Selifan added.

"Hold your tongue, you fool," said Chichikov.

"But who are you?" said the old woman.

"A nobleman, mother."

The word "nobleman" seemed to make the old woman reflect a little.

"Wait, I’ll tell the mistress," she pronounced, and in about two minutes returned with a lantern in her hand.

The gates were unlocked. A light glimmered in another window too. The chaise, having driven into the yard, stopped before a small house which, owing to the darkness, was difficult to make out. Only one half of it was illuminated by the light coming from the windows; a puddle was also visible before the house, upon which the same light fell directly. The rain drummed loudly on the wooden roof and ran down in gurgling streams into a barrel placed beneath. Meanwhile, the dogs broke out into all possible voices: one, throwing back its head, howled so long and with such effort as if he received God knows what salary for it; another barked in a hurried manner, like a parish clerk; between them rang out, like a post-horn, the restless treble of probably a young puppy; and all this, finally, was capped by a bass—perhaps an old fellow endowed with a sturdy canine nature, because he hoarsed like a choral double-bass when a concert is in full swing, where the tenors stand on tiptoe from a powerful desire to bring out a high note, and everything that exists strains upward, throwing back its head, while he alone, having thrust his unshaven chin into his necktie, squatting and sinking almost to the ground, lets out from there his note, from which the window-panes shake and rattle. By the dog's barking alone, composed of such musicians, one could suppose that the village was a decent one; but our drenched and chilled hero thought of nothing but a bed.

The chaise had scarcely come to a complete standstill when he jumped onto the porch, stumbled, and nearly fell. Another woman came out onto the porch, younger than the first but very like her. She conducted him into a room. Chichikov cast two passing glances around: the room was hung with old striped wallpaper; pictures with some sort of birds; between the windows, old-fashioned small mirrors with dark frames in the form of curled leaves; behind every mirror was tucked either a letter, or an old pack of cards, or a stocking; a wall clock with flowers painted on the dial... he was unable to notice anything more. He felt that his eyes were sticking together as though someone had smeared them with honey.

A minute later the mistress entered—a woman of advanced years, in some sort of nightcap put on in a hurry, with flannel around her neck, one of those mothers, small-scale landowners, who weep over failures of the crops, losses, and hold their heads somewhat to one side, while meanwhile they amass a little money by degrees in motley linen bags distributed among the drawers of their chests. In one bag they select all the silver rubles, in another the half-rubles, in a third the quarter-rubles, although to all appearances it seems as if there were nothing in the chest but linen, and night-jackets, and hanks of thread, and an unpicked mantle destined afterwards to turn into a dress if the old one should somehow get burnt during the baking of festive cakes with all kinds of fried dainties, or wear away of itself. But the dress will not burn and will not wear away of itself: the old woman is careful, and the mantle is fated to lie for a long time in an unpicked state, and then to pass by will to a niece of a second-cousin, along with all the other trumpery.

Chichikov apologized for having disturbed her by an unexpected arrival.

"Never mind, never mind," said the mistress. "At what a time God has brought you! Such a turmoil and blizzard... You ought to have a bite of something after the road, but the time is night, it's impossible to prepare anything."

The mistress’s words were interrupted by a strange hissing, so that the guest was rather frightened; the noise resembled the whole room becoming filled with snakes; but, looking up, he was reassured, for he perceived that the wall clock had taken a fancy to strike. The hissing was immediately followed by a wheezing, and at last, straining with all its might, it struck two o'clock with a sound as if someone were banging a stick against a broken pot, after which the pendulum went on ticking quietly again to the right and to the left.

Chichikov thanked the mistress, saying that he required nothing, that she should not trouble herself about anything, that except for a bed he demanded nothing, and was curious only to know into what parts he had driven and whether it was far from here to the estate of the landowner Sobakevich, to which the old woman said that she had never even heard such a name and that there was no such landowner at all.

"At least you know Manilov?" said Chichikov.

"And who is Manilov?"

"A landowner, mother."

"No, I haven’t heard, there is no such landowner."

"What ones are there then?"

"Bobrov, Svinin, Kanapatiev, Kharpakin, Trepakin, Pleshakov."

"Are they rich people or not?"

"No, father, there are no overly rich ones. One has twenty souls, another thirty, but as for those with a hundred—there are none such."

Chichikov perceived that he had driven into quite a remote wilderness.

"Is it far at least to the town?"

"About sixty versts it will be. How sorry I am that there is nothing for you to eat! Wouldn't you like some tea, my good sir?"

"Thank you, mother. Nothing is needed but a bed."

"True, after such a road one has great need of rest. Dispose yourself right here, father, on this sofa. Hey, Fetinya, bring a feather bed, pillows, and a sheet. What weather God has sent: such thunder—I had a candle burning before the icon all night. Oh, my father, why, your whole back and side are in mud like a boar's! Where did you manage to get so soiled?"

"Thanks be to God that I only got soiled; I must be thankful that I didn't break my ribs entirely."

"Saints above, what horrors! Don't you need something to rub your back with?"

"Thank you, thank you. Don't trouble yourself, but only order your girl to dry and clean my clothes."

"Do you hear, Fetinya!" said the mistress, turning to the woman who had come out onto the porch with a candle, and who had already managed to drag in a feather bed and, having beaten it up with both hands on both sides, let loose a whole flood of feathers throughout the room. "Take his coat together with his undergarments, and first dry them before the fire, as we used to do for the late master, and afterwards rub them and beat them out thoroughly."

"Very good, mistress!" said Fetinya, spreading a sheet over the feather bed and laying the pillows.

"Well, there’s your bed ready for you," said the mistress. "Good-bye, father, I wish you a good night. Do you need anything else? Perhaps, my father, you are accustomed to having someone scratch your heels at night? My late husband could never fall asleep without that."

But the guest declined the scratching of his heels as well. The mistress went out, and he immediately hastened to undress, delivering to Fetinya all the armor he stripped off, both upper and lower; and Fetinya, having also wished him a good night on her part, carried off these wet accoutrements.

Left alone, he looked not without pleasure at his bed, which reached almost to the ceiling. Fetinya was evidently a mistress of the art of beating up feather beds. When, having placed a chair, he climbed into the bed, it sank under him almost to the very floor, and the feathers, displaced by him from their borders, flew into all corners of the room. Extinguishing the candle, he covered himself with the chintz quilt and, curling himself up like a pretzel beneath it, fell asleep that very minute.

He woke up the next day at a rather late hour in the morning. The sun shone through the window directly into his eyes, and the flies, which yesterday had been sleeping quietly on the walls and ceiling, all turned their attention to him: one sat on his lip, another on his ear, a third strove to settle on his very eye; as for the one that had the imprudence to alight near his nostril, he drew it into his nose in his sleep, which made him sneeze violently—a circumstance that was the cause of his awakening. Casting a glance around the room, he now noticed that the pictures were not *all* birds: among them hung a portrait of Kutuzov, and some old man painted in oils with red cuffs on his uniform, such as were worn in the time of Paul Petrovich. The clock again emitted a hissing and struck ten; a woman’s face peeped in at the door and instantly vanished, for Chichikov, wishing to sleep better, had stripped off absolutely everything.

The face that peeped in seemed to him somewhat familiar. He began to recollect who it might be, and at last remembered that it was the mistress. He put on his shirt; his clothes, already dried and cleaned, lay beside him. Having dressed, he went up to the mirror and sneezed again so loudly that a turkey-cock who had come up to the window at that moment—the window being very close to the ground—suddenly spluttered something out to him very quickly in his strange language, probably "I wish you good health," to which Chichikov called him a fool.

Approaching the window, he began to examine the views before him: the window looked out almost into a poultry-yard; at least, the narrow little yard before him was entirely filled with birds and all kinds of domestic creatures. There was no number to the turkeys and hens; among them walked a cock with measured steps, shaking his comb and turning his head to one side as if listening to something; a pig with her family appeared there too; there too, scratching up a heap of rubbish, she ate a chicken in passing and, without noticing it, went on gobbling up watermelon rinds in her regular way. This small yard, or poultry-run, was enclosed by a board fence, beyond which stretched extensive kitchen-gardens with cabbage, onions, potatoes, beets, and other domestic vegetables. Apple trees and other fruit trees were scattered here and there about the garden, covered with nets for protection against magpies and sparrows, the latter of which moved from place to place in whole slanting clouds. For this same reason several scarecrows were erected on long poles, with outstretched arms; upon one of them was placed the nightcap of the mistress herself. Beyond the kitchen-gardens followed the peasants’ huts, which, although they were built at random and not enclosed in regular streets, yet, according to the observation made by Chichikov, showed the prosperity of the inhabitants, for they were properly maintained: the decayed shingles on the roofs were everywhere replaced by new ones; the gates were nowhere askew; and in the covered peasant barns facing him, he noticed in one a spare, almost new cart standing, and in another even two.

"Why, she has no small village," he said, and resolved there and then to have a chat and get more closely acquainted with the mistress. He peeped through the chink of the door through which she had poked her head, and seeing her sitting at the tea-table, went in to her with a cheerful and kindly air.

"Good morning, father. How did you sleep?" said the mistress, rising from her place. She was better dressed than yesterday—in a dark dress and no longer in a nightcap, but there was still something tied around her neck in the same way.

"Well, well," said Chichikov, seating himself in an armchair. "How are you, mother?"

"Bad, my father."

"How so?"

"Sleeplessness. My loins ache all the time, and my leg, above the ankle, just throbs so."

"It will pass, it will pass, mother. You mustn't mind that."

"God grant it may pass. I rubbed it with lard and moistened it with turpentine too. And what will you have with your tea? There is fruit liqueur in the flask."

"Not bad, mother, we’ll have a drop of fruit liqueur too."

The reader, I think, has already noticed that Chichikov, in spite of his kindly air, spoke, however, with greater freedom than with Manilov, and did not stand on ceremony at all. It must be said that if we in Russia have not yet caught up with foreigners in some other respects, we have far outstripped them in the art of address. It is impossible to count all the shades and refinements of our address. A Frenchman or a German will never discern or understand all its peculiarities and differences; he will speak in almost the same voice and the same language to a millionaire and to a petty tobacco dealer, although, of course, in his soul he will cringe sufficiently before the first. With us it is not so: we have such wiseacres who will speak to a landowner possessing two hundred souls quite differently than to one who has three hundred, and to him who has three hundred they will speak again not as to him who has five hundred, and to him who has five hundred again not as to him who has eight hundred—in a word, though you ascend to a million, they will still find shades.

Let us suppose, for instance, that there exists a government office, not here, but in a kingdom far away, and in the office, let us suppose, there exists a director of the office. I beg you to look at him when he sits among his subordinates—why, simply from fear you won't utter a word! Pride and nobility, and what does his face not express? Just take a brush and paint: Prometheus, a perfect Prometheus! He looks like an eagle, he walks smoothly, measuredly. That very same eagle, the moment he leaves the room and approaches the private office of his chief, scurries along like a partridge with papers under his arm so fast it’s painful to watch. In society and at an evening party, if everyone is of a low rank, Prometheus will remain Prometheus, but if there is anyone slightly above him, such a transformation takes place in Prometheus as Ovid himself could not invent: a fly, less even than a fly, he is annihilated into a grain of sand! "Why, that is not Ivan Petrovich," you say, looking at him. "Ivan Petrovich is taller, while this one is both short and thin; that one speaks loudly, in a bass voice, and never laughs, while this one, devil knows what, squeaks like a bird and does nothing but laugh." You go closer, you look—it is exactly Ivan Petrovich! "Aha," you think to yourself...

But, however, let us return to our characters. Chichikov, as we have already seen, resolved not to stand on ceremony at all, and therefore, taking a cup of tea in his hand and pouring some fruit liqueur into it, began the conversation thus:

"You have a nice little village, mother. How many souls are in it?"

"The souls in it, my father, are ninety short of a hundred," said the mistress, "but the trouble is, times are bad; why, last year there was such a failure of the crops, God preserve us."

"However, the peasants look sturdy, the huts are strong. And allow me to learn your surname. I was so distracted... arriving at night time..."

"Korobochka, widow of a Collegiate Secretary."

"I thank you most humbly. And your name and patronymic?"

"Nastasya Petrovna."

"Nastasya Petrovna? A good name, Nastasya Petrovna. I have a maternal aunt, my mother's sister, Nastasya Petrovna."

"And what is your name?" asked the landlady. "Why, you, I take it, are an assessor?"

"No, mother," replied Chichikov, smiling, "no, not an assessor, but just traveling on our own little affairs."

"Ah, so you are a buyer! How I regret, really, that I sold my honey to the merchants so cheap; why, you, father, would surely have bought it from me."

"But I wouldn't have bought honey."

"What else then? Hemp, perhaps? But then, I have very little hemp now: half a pood in all."

"No, mother, a commodity of another kind: tell me, have any of your peasants died?"

"Oh, father, eighteen of them!" said the old woman, fetching a sigh. "And such fine people died, all workmen. After that, true, some have been born, but what use are they: they're all such small fry; and the assessor drove up—pay the tax, says he, on each soul. The people are dead, but pay as if they were alive. Last week my blacksmith was burnt, such a skillful blacksmith, he knew locksmith's work too."

"Did you have a fire, mother?"

"God preserved us from such a calamity, a fire would be even worse; he caught fire himself, my father. Something inside him somehow started burning, he had drunk too much; only a blue flame came from him, he wasted away entirely, wasted away and turned black as a coal, and he was such an extremely skillful blacksmith! And now I have nothing to go out in: there is no one to shoe the horses."

"The will of God is in all things, mother!" said Chichikov, fetching a sigh; "against the wisdom of God nothing can be said... Cede them to me, Nastasya Petrovna?"

"Whom, father?"

"Why, all those who have died."

"But how cede them?"

"Why, just simply. Or, if you like, sell them. I will give you money for them."

"But how? Really, I can't grasp the sense of it. Do you mean you want to dig them up out of the earth?"

Chichikov saw that the old woman had gone far astray and that it was absolutely necessary to explain to her what the matter was. In a few words he explained to her that the transfer or purchase would figure only on paper and the souls would be written down as if living.

"But what do you want them for?" said the old woman, goggling her eyes at him.

"That is my business."

"But they are dead."

"Well, who says they are alive? That is just why it is a loss to you, because they are dead: you pay for them, but now I will rid you of the trouble and the payment. Do you understand? And not only rid you of it, but besides that I will give you fifteen rubles. Well, is it clear now?"

"Really, I don't know," the landlady uttered with pauses. "Why, I have never sold dead people before."

"Naturally! It would rather resemble a miracle if you had sold them to anyone. Or do you think there is really some use in them?"

"No, I don't think that. What use can there be in them? There is no use at all. The only thing that perplexes me is that they are already dead."

"Well, the woman seems thick-headed!" Chichikov thought to himself.

"Listen, mother. Just reflect well: why, you are being ruined, you pay the tax for him as for a living man..."

"Oh, my father, don't even speak of it!" the landlady caught him up. "Only week before last I paid more than a hundred and fifty. And greased the assessor’s palm too."

"Well, you see, mother. And now take into consideration only that you won't have to grease the assessor’s palm anymore, because now I pay for them; I, and not you; I take upon myself all the obligations. I will even execute the deed at my own expense, do you understand that?"

The old woman grew thoughtful. She saw that the business did, indeed, look advantageous, only it was too new and unprecedented; and therefore she began to be highly afraid lest this buyer should somehow take her in; he had come, too, God knows from where, and at night time besides.

"Well, mother, shall we strike hands on it then?" said Chichikov.

"Really, father, it has never yet happened to me to sell the deceased. The living ones I cede—why, only year before last I let the priest have two girls, at a hundred rubles each, and he thanked me very much, they turned out such fine workers: they weave napkins themselves."

"Well, but it's not a matter of the living; God be with them. I am asking for the dead ones."

"Really, I’m afraid at first, lest I should somehow suffer a loss. Perhaps, father, you are deceiving me, and they... they are worth more somehow."

"Listen, mother... eh, what people you are! What can they be worth? Consider: why, they are dust. Do you understand? They are simply dust. Take any worthless, last thing, for instance even a simple rag, and even a rag has a price: it will at least be bought for a paper factory, but this is needed for nothing. Well, tell me yourself, what is it needed for?"

"That's true, indeed. It's completely needed for nothing; but then, the only thing that stops me is that they are already dead."

"Oh, what a blockhead of a woman!" Chichikov said to himself, already beginning to lose patience. "Go and manage her! She’s thrown me into a sweat, the cursed old crone!"

Here he, pulling a handkerchief out of his pocket, began to wipe the sweat that had actually broken out on his forehead. However, Chichikov was angry in vain: many a respectable and even statesmanlike man turns out in reality a perfect Korobochka. Once he has knocked something into his head, you can't overcome him by anything; no matter how many arguments you present to him, clear as day, everything bounces off him just as a rubber ball bounces off a wall. Having wiped away the sweat, Chichikov resolved to try whether she could be brought into the path from some other side.

"You, mother," he said, "either do not wish to understand my words, or you speak so on purpose, just to have something to say... I am giving you money: fifteen rubles in banknotes. Do you understand? Why, that is money. You won't find it in the street. Come, confess, for how much did you sell your honey?"

"At twelve rubles a pood."

"You have taken a little sin upon your soul, mother. You didn't sell it for twelve."

"By God, I did."

"Well, you see? But then, that is honey. You gathered it, perhaps, for about a year, with cares, with effort, trouble; you went about, stifled the bees, fed them in the cellar all winter; but dead souls are a business not of this world. Here you on your part have applied no effort; it was the will of God that they should leave this world, inflicting a loss upon your estate. There you received twelve rubles for labor, for effort, but here you take money for nothing, gratis, and not twelve, but fifteen, and not in silver, but all in blue banknotes."

After such powerful persuasions Chichikov had scarcely any doubt that the old woman would at last give way.

"Really," replied the landlady, "mine is such an inexperienced widow's business! I'd better wait a little while, maybe merchants will come along, and I'll see about the prices."

"Shame, shame, mother! Simply a shame! Come, what are you saying, think for yourself! Who on earth is going to buy them? Come, what use can anyone make of them?"

"Well, maybe in the household they'll come in handy somehow by chance..." the old woman objected, and did not finish her speech, opening her mouth and looking at him almost with fear, wishing to know what he would say to that.

"Dead people in the household! Look where you’ve wandered to! To scare the sparrows at night in your kitchen-garden, or what?"

"The power of the cross be with us! What horrors you say!" uttered the old woman, crossing herself.

"Where else did you mean to place them? But, anyway, the bones and the graves—all that remains to you; the transfer is only on paper. Well, then? How is it to be? Answer at least."

The old woman again grew thoughtful.

"What are you thinking about, Nastasya Petrovna?"

"Really, I still can't make up my mind what to do; I’d better sell you some hemp."

### CHAPTER FOUR

Having driven up to the tavern, Chichikov ordered a stop for two reasons. On the one hand, to give the horses a rest, and on the other hand, to have a bite of something himself and refresh his strength. The author must confess that he highly envies the appetite and stomach of people of this sort. For him, all the grand gentlemen living in Petersburg and Moscow signify absolutely nothing—those who spend their time contemplating what they might eat tomorrow and what kind of dinner to devise for the day after, and who sit down to this dinner only after first sending a pill into their mouths; who swallow oysters, sea-spiders, and other monstrosities, and then set off for Carlsbad or the Caucasus. No, these gentlemen have never aroused his envy. But the gentlemen of the middle sort, who at one station demand ham, at a second a sucking pig, at a third a slice of sturgeon or some baked sausage with onions, and then sit down to the table at any time you please, as if nothing had happened, and the sterlet soup with burbot and milt hisses and grumbles between their teeth, washed down with a *rasstegai* or a *kulyebyaka* with catfish tail, so that from the outside one is seized with an appetite—these gentlemen do, indeed, enjoy a enviable gift from heaven! More than one grand gentleman would sacrifice this very minute half his peasant souls and half his estates, mortgaged and otherwise, just to have such a stomach as a gentleman of the middle sort possesses; but the trouble is that for no amount of money, nor even for estates, with or without improvements, can one acquire such a stomach as happens to belong to a gentleman of the middle sort.

The wooden, darkened tavern received Chichikov under its narrow, hospitable overhang supported by carved wooden posts that resembled ancient church candlesticks. The tavern was something like a Russian log hut, only on a somewhat larger scale. The carved ornamental cornices of fresh wood around the windows and under the roof contrasted sharply and vividly against its dark walls; on the shutters were painted pitchers with flowers.

Climbing up the narrow wooden stairs to the broad entry hall above, he encountered a door opening with a creak and a fat old woman in motley calicoes, who mumbled: "This way, please!" In the room he came upon all those old friends that encounter everyone in small wooden taverns, of which no small number are built along the roads, namely: a frost-covered samovar, smoothly scraped pine walls, a triangular cupboard with teapots and cups in the corner, gilded porcelain eggs hanging before the icons on blue and red ribbons, a cat that had recently littered, a mirror that showed four eyes instead of two and a sort of pancake instead of a face; and finally, bundles of fragrant herbs and cloves stuck by the icons, dried to such a degree that anyone who wished to smell them did nothing but sneeze and nothing more.

"Have you any sucking pig?" Chichikov turned with this question to the woman standing there.

"Yes, we have."

"With horseradish and sour cream?"

"With horseradish and sour cream."

"Bring it here!"

Our hero, according to his custom, immediately entered into conversation with her and inquired whether she kept the tavern herself or if there was a master, and how much income the tavern brought in, and whether her sons lived with them, and whether the eldest son was a bachelor or a married man, and what kind of wife he had taken, whether with a large dowry or not, and whether his father-in-law was satisfied, and whether he wasn't angry at having received few gifts at the wedding—in short, he did not omit a thing. It goes without saying that he was curious to learn what landowners were to be found in their neighborhood, and learned that there were all sorts of landowners: Plotin, Pochitaev, Mylnoy, Cheprakov the colonel, Sobakevich. "Ah! You know Sobakevich?" he asked, and there and then heard that the old woman knew not only Sobakevich but Manilov as well, and that Manilov was a bit more delicate than Sobakevich: he would order a chicken to be boiled at once, and ask for veal too; if there was sheep's liver, he would ask for sheep's liver too, and only taste a little of everything, whereas Sobakevich would ask for just one thing, but then he'd eat it all up, and even demand a second helping for the same price.

While he was conversing in this manner, eating the sucking pig, of which only the last piece now remained, there was heard the rattle of wheels of an approaching carriage. From afar there crawled up another wretched carriage, empty, dragged by some long-haired team of four with torn collars and rope harnesses. He was a man of tall stature, thin in the face, or what is called worn out, with reddish moustaches. Since the conversation which the travelers carried on among themselves was not very interesting for the reader, we shall do better if we say something about Nozdrev himself, who, it may be, is destined to play a part by no means the least in our poem.

Nozdrev's face is probably already somewhat familiar to the reader. Everyone has had occasion to meet not a few of such people. They are called dashing young fellows, pass even in childhood and at school for good comrades, and with all that are very painfully thrashed. In their faces is always seen something open, direct, daring. They make acquaintance quickly, and before you have time to look around, they are already saying "thou" to you. Friendship they form, it seems, forever: but it almost always happens that the one who has become friends fights with them that very evening at a friendly carousal. They are always talkers, revelers, daredevils, noticeable people. Nozdrev at thirty-five was completely the same as he had been at eighteen and twenty: a lover of a spree. His marriage had not changed him at all, the more so as his wife soon departed for the next world, leaving two youngsters, who were decidedly not needed by him. A pretty nurse, however, looked after the children. At home he could by no means sit for more than a day. His sharp nose scented from several tens of versts away where there was a fair with all kinds of gatherings and balls; he was there in the twinkling of an eye, argued and raised a rumpus at the green table, for he had, like all such, a little passion for cards. At cards, as we have already seen from the first chapter, he played not altogether sinlessly and cleanly, knowing many different shifts and other subtleties, and therefore the game very frequently ended in another game: either they thrashed him with boots, or else applied a shifting to his thick and very good sideburns, so that he sometimes returned home with only one sideburn, and that a rather thin one. But his healthy and full cheeks were so well created and contained so much vegetative force that his sideburns soon grew out anew, even better than the old ones. And what is most strange, such as can happen perhaps only in Russia alone, within a short time he would already meet again those friends who had drubbed him, and meet them as if nothing had happened, and he, as the saying is, thought nothing of it, and they thought nothing of it.
