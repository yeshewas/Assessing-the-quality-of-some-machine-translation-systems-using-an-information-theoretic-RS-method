VI

Learning from Kovrin that not only had the romance progressed, but that there would even be a wedding, Egor Semenyich walked back and forth for a long time, trying to hide his agitation. His hands began to tremble, his neck swelled and turned crimson, he ordered the racing sleigh to be harnessed and drove off somewhere. Tanya, seeing how he lashed the horse and how deeply, almost over his ears, he pulled down his cap, understood his mood, locked herself in her room and cried all day.

In the greenhouses, peaches and plums were already ripening; packing and shipping this delicate and temperamental fruit to Moscow required much attention, labor, and effort. Due to the fact that the summer was very hot and dry, it was necessary to water every tree, which took much time and labor, and caterpillars appeared in great numbers, which the workers and even Egor Semenyich and Tanya, to Kovrin's great disgust, crushed with their bare fingers. Despite all this, it was already necessary to accept orders for autumn fruits and trees and conduct extensive correspondence. And at the busiest time, when it seemed no one had a free minute, field work began, which took more than half the garden workers away; Egor Semenyich, deeply tanned, exhausted, and angry, rode back and forth between the garden and the field, shouting that he was being torn to pieces and that he would put a bullet in his head.

And then there was the fuss with the dowry, which the Pesotskys attached considerable importance to; from the clicking of scissors, the clatter of sewing machines, the smell of hot irons, and from the whims of the dressmaker, a nervous, touchy lady, everyone in the house had headaches. And as if on purpose, guests arrived every day, who had to be entertained, fed, and even put up for the night. But all this hard labor passed unnoticed, as if in a fog. Tanya felt as though love and happiness had caught her unawares, although since she was fourteen she had been somehow convinced that Kovrin would marry precisely her. She was amazed, bewildered, could not believe it… Suddenly such joy would surge over her that she wanted to fly up into the clouds and pray to God there, but then suddenly she would remember that in August she would have to part with her native home and leave her father, or from God knows where, the thought would come that she was insignificant, small, and unworthy of such a great man as Kovrin – and she would go to her room, lock the door, and cry bitterly for several hours. When there were guests, suddenly it would seem to her that Kovrin was extraordinarily handsome and that all women were in love with him and envied her, and her soul would fill with rapture and pride, as if she had conquered the whole world, but the moment he smiled kindly at some young lady, she would already tremble with jealousy, go to her room – and tears again. These new sensations took complete possession of her; she helped her father mechanically and did not notice either the peaches, or the caterpillars, or the workers, or how quickly time was passing.

Almost the same thing was happening with Egor Semenyich. He worked from morning to night, always hurrying somewhere, losing his temper, getting irritated, but all of this in some kind of magical half-sleep. It was as if two people were sitting inside him: one was the real Egor Semenyich, who, listening to the gardener Ivan Karlych reporting to him about the disorders, was indignant and in despair clutched his head, and the other, not real, as if half-drunk, who suddenly interrupted the business conversation mid-sentence, touched the gardener on the shoulder and began to mutter:

– Say what you will, but blood means a great deal. His mother was a remarkable woman, most noble, most intelligent. It was a pleasure to look at her kind, clear, pure face, like an angel's. She drew beautifully, wrote poetry, spoke five foreign languages, sang… Poor thing, may her soul rest in peace, she died of consumption.

The not real Egor Semenyich sighed and, after a pause, continued:

– When he was a boy and growing up with me, he had such an angelic face, clear and kind. His gaze, his movements, his speech are all tender and graceful, like his mother's. And his mind? He always amazed us with his intelligence. And indeed, it's no wonder he's a master's degree holder! No wonder! But wait, Ivan Karlych, what will he be like in ten years! You won't be able to reach him!

But then the real Egor Semenyich, catching himself, made a terrible face,He grabbed his head and shouted:

     – Devils! They've desecrated it, defiled it, frozen it! The garden is ruined! The garden is lost!

     But Kovrín worked with the same diligence as before and did not notice the commotion. Love only added fuel to the fire. After each meeting with Tanya, he, happy and enthusiastic, would go to his room and with the same passion with which he had just kissed Tanya and declared his love to her, would take up a book or his manuscript. What the black monk said about God's chosen ones, eternal truth, about the brilliant future of mankind, and so forth, gave his work a special, extraordinary significance and filled his soul with pride, with a sense of his own superiority. Once or twice a week, in the park or in the house, he would meet the black monk and converse with him at length, but this did not frighten him; on the contrary, it delighted him, for he was already firmly convinced that such visions visited only the chosen, outstanding people who had devoted themselves to serving an idea.

     Once the monk appeared during dinner and sat in the dining room by the window. Kovrín was delighted and very skillfully started a conversation with Yegor Semyonich and Tanya about things that might interest the monk; the black guest listened and nodded his head affably, and Yegor Semyonich and Tanya also listened and smiled cheerfully, not suspecting that Kovrín was speaking not to them, but to his hallucination.

     Imperceptibly the Assumption fast approached, and soon after it came the wedding day, which, at Yegor Semyonich's insistent wish, was celebrated "with a bang," that is, with a senseless revelry that lasted two days. They ate and drank three thousand rubles' worth, but from the poor hired music, loud toasts, and servants running about, from the noise and crowding, they could not appreciate the taste of the expensive wines or the wonderful appetizers ordered from Moscow.

VII

One winter night, during one of the long winter nights, Kovrín lay in bed reading a French novel. Poor Tanya, whose head ached in the evenings from the unaccustomed life in the city, had long since fallen asleep and occasionally muttered some incoherent phrases in her delirium.

     Three o'clock struck. Kovrín extinguished the candle and lay down; he lay for a long time with his eyes closed, but could not fall asleep because, as it seemed to him, the bedroom was very hot and Tanya was delirious. At half past four he lit the candle again, and at that moment he saw the black monk, who was sitting in an armchair near the bed.

     – Hello, – said the monk, and after a brief silence, asked: – What are you thinking about now?

     – About fame, – answered Kovrín. – In the French novel I was just reading, there is depicted a young scholar who does foolish things and wastes away from longing for fame. I do not understand this longing.

     – Because you are intelligent. You regard fame with indifference, as a toy that does not interest you.

     – Yes, that is true.

     – Fame does not smile upon you. What is flattering, or amusing, or instructive in the fact that your name will be carved on a tombstone and then time will erase this inscription along with the gilding? And besides, fortunately, there are too many of you for weak human memory to retain your names.

     – I understand, – Kovrín agreed. – And why should they remember them? But let us talk about something else. For example, about happiness. What is happiness?

     When the clock struck five, he was sitting on the bed, his legs dangling over the carpet, and speaking to the monk:

     – In ancient times, a happy man in the end became frightened of his happiness – it was so great! – and, to appease the gods, sacrificed his favorite ring to them. Do you know? And I, like Polycrates, am beginning to be a little troubled by my happiness. It seems strange to me that from morning to night I experience only joy, it fills me completely and drowns out all other feelings. I do not know what sadness, sorrow, or boredom is. Here I am not sleeping, I have insomnia, but I am not bored. Seriously speaking: I am beginning to be puzzled.

     – But why? – the monk was astonished. – Is joy a supernatural feeling? Should it not be the normal state of man? The higher a man is in mental and moral development, the freer he is, the greater pleasure life brings him. Socrates,Diogenes and Marcus Aurelius experienced joy, not sorrow. And the apostle says: rejoice always. Rejoice then and be happy.

     – But what if the gods become angry? – Kovrin joked and laughed. – If they take away my comfort and force me to shiver and go hungry, then I hardly think I would enjoy that.

     Tanya meanwhile woke up and looked at her husband with amazement and horror. He was speaking, addressing the armchair, gesticulating and laughing: his eyes were shining and there was something strange in his laughter.

     – Andryusha, who are you talking to? – she asked, grabbing his hand, which he had extended toward the monk. – Andryusha! To whom?

     – What? To whom? – Kovrin became confused. – To him… There he is sitting, – he said, pointing at the black monk.

     – There is no one here… no one! Andryusha, you are ill!

     Tanya embraced her husband and pressed herself against him, as if protecting him from visions, and covered his eyes with her hand.

     – You are ill! – she sobbed, trembling all over. – Forgive me, my dear, my darling, but I have long noticed that your soul is troubled by something… You are mentally ill, Andryusha…

     Her trembling communicated itself to him. He glanced once more at the armchair, which was now empty, suddenly felt weakness in his arms and legs, became frightened and began to dress.

     – It is nothing, Tanya, nothing… – he muttered, trembling. – In truth I am somewhat unwell… it is time I admitted it.

     – I have long noticed… and papa noticed, – she said, trying to restrain her sobs. – You talk to yourself, smile in such a strange way… you do not sleep. Oh, my God, my God, save us! – she said in horror. – But do not be afraid, Andryusha, do not be afraid, for God's sake, do not be afraid…

     She too began to dress. Only now, looking at her, Kovrin understood the full danger of his situation, understood what the black monk and conversations with him meant. For him it was now clear that he was mad.

     Both, not knowing why themselves, dressed and went into the hall: she ahead, he behind her. There, awakened by the sobs, in his dressing gown and with a candle in his hand stood Egor Semenyich, who was visiting them.

     – Do not be afraid, Andryusha, – Tanya said, trembling as if in fever, – do not be afraid… Papa, it will all pass… it will all pass…

     Kovrin could not speak from agitation. He wanted to say to his father-in-law in a joking tone:

     – Congratulate me, I seem to have gone mad, – but only moved his lips and smiled bitterly.

     At nine o'clock in the morning they put a coat and fur coat on him, wrapped him in a shawl and took him by carriage to the doctor. He began treatment.

VIII

Summer came again, and the doctor ordered him to go to the country. Kovrin had already recovered, stopped seeing the black monk, and all that remained was to restore his physical strength. Living at his father-in-law's in the country, he drank much milk, worked only two hours a day, did not drink wine and did not smoke.

     On the evening of Ilyin Day a vigil service was held in the house. When the deacon handed the censer to the priest, the old vast hall smelled exactly like a cemetery, and Kovrin became bored. He went out into the garden. Not noticing the luxurious flowers, he walked through the garden, sat on a bench, then strolled through the park; reaching the river, he went down and stood there in thought, looking at the water. The gloomy pines with shaggy roots, which last year saw him here so young, joyful and vigorous, now did not whisper, but stood motionless and silent, as if they did not recognize him. And indeed, his head was shorn, the long beautiful hair was gone, his gait was listless, his face, compared to last summer, had become fuller and paler.

     By the planks he crossed to the other bank. Where last year there was rye, now lay in rows the mown oats. The sun had already set, and on the horizon burned a wide red glow, foretelling windy weather for tomorrow. It was quiet. Looking in the direction where last year the black monk first appeared, Kovrin stood for about twenty minutes until the evening glow began to fade…

     When he, listless and dissatisfied, returned home, the vigil service was already over. Egor Semenyich and Tanya were sitting on the terrace steps and drinking tea. They were talking about something, but, seeing Kovrin, suddenly fell silent, and he concluded from their faces that the conversation had been about him.

     – It seems to you,It's time to drink milk already, – Tanya said to her husband.

     – No, it's not time… – he replied, sitting down on the lowest step. – Drink it yourself. I don't want to.

     Tanya exchanged an anxious glance with her father and said in a guilty voice:

     – You yourself notice that milk is good for you.

     – Yes, very good! – Kovrin smirked. – I congratulate you: since Friday I've gained another pound in weight. – He pressed his head tightly with his hands and spoke with anguish: – Why, why did you treat me? Bromide preparations, idleness, warm baths, supervision, cowardly fear for every sip, for every step – all this will eventually drive me to idiocy. I was going mad, I had delusions of grandeur, but in return I was cheerful, vigorous and even happy, I was interesting and original. Now I've become more reasonable and respectable, but in return I'm like everyone else: I'm mediocre, I'm bored with life… Oh, how cruelly you've treated me! I saw hallucinations, but who did it bother? I ask: who did it bother?

     – God knows what you're saying! – sighed Egor Semyonich. – It's even boring to listen to.

     – Then don't listen.

     The presence of people, especially Egor Semyonich, now irritated Kovrin; he answered him dryly, coldly and even rudely and looked at him in no other way than mockingly and with hatred, while Egor Semyonich became confused and guiltily coughed, although he felt no guilt whatsoever. Not understanding why their kind, good-natured relations had changed so sharply, Tanya clung to her father and looked anxiously into his eyes; she wanted to understand and could not, and for her it was clear only that relations were getting worse with each passing day, that her father had aged greatly of late, and her husband had become irritable, capricious, fault-finding and uninteresting. She could no longer laugh and sing, ate nothing at dinner, did not sleep whole nights, waiting for something terrible, and was so tormented that once she lay in a faint from afternoon until evening. During the all-night vigil it seemed to her that her father was crying, and now, when the three of them sat on the terrace, she made an effort not to think about it.

     – How happy Buddha and Mohammed or Shakespeare were that kind relatives and doctors did not treat them for ecstasy and inspiration! – said Kovrin. – If Mohammed had taken potassium bromide for his nerves, worked only two hours a day and drunk milk, then after this remarkable man there would have remained as little as after his dog. Doctors and kind relatives will eventually do what will make humanity stupid, mediocrity will be considered genius and civilization will perish. If you only knew, – said Kovrin with annoyance, – how grateful I am to you!

     He felt a strong irritation and, so as not to say too much, quickly got up and went into the house. It was quiet, and the aroma of tobacco and jalap wafted in through the open windows from the garden. In the enormous dark hall, moonlight lay on the floor and on the piano in green patches. Kovrin recalled the raptures of last summer, when it smelled the same of jalap and the moon shone in the windows. To recapture last year's mood, he quickly went to his study, lit a strong cigar and ordered the footman to bring wine. But the cigar tasted bitter and unpleasant in his mouth, and the wine turned out not to be the same flavor as last year. And what does it mean to lose the habit! From the cigar and two sips of wine his head began to spin and his heart began to race, so that he had to take potassium bromide.

     Before going to bed, Tanya said to him:

     – Father adores you. You're angry with him for something, and it's killing him. Look: he's aging not by days but by hours. I beg you, Andryusha, for God's sake, for the sake of your late father, for my peace of mind, be kind to him!

     – I can't and I won't.

     – But why? – asked Tanya, beginning to tremble all over. – Explain to me, why?

     – Because I don't like him, that's all, – Kovrin said carelessly and shrugged his shoulders, – but let's not talk about him: he's your father.

     – I can't, I can't understand! – said Tanya, pressing her temples and staring at one point. – Something incomprehensible, terrible is happening in our house. You've changed, you're not yourself… You, intelligent,an extraordinary man, you get irritated over trifles, you meddle in squabbles… Such petty things worry you that sometimes you simply marvel and don't believe: is that really you? Well, well, don't be angry, don't be angry, – she continued, frightened by her own words and kissing his hands. – You are intelligent, kind, noble. You will be fair to father. He is such a good man!

     – He is not good, but good-natured. Vaudeville uncles, like your father, with their well-fed good-natured faces, extraordinarily hospitable and eccentric, once touched my heart and made me laugh both in stories and in vaudevilles and in life, but now they repel me. They are egoists to the core. What repels me most is their satiation and this gastric, purely bovine or porcine optimism.

     Tanya sat on the bed and laid her head on the pillow.

     – This is torture, – she said, and from her voice it was evident that she was already extremely exhausted and that it was difficult for her to speak. – Since winter itself, not a single peaceful moment… This is terrible, my God! I am suffering…

     – Yes, of course, I am Herod, and you and your papa are Egyptian infants. Of course!

     His face seemed to Tanya ugly and unpleasant. Hatred and a mocking expression did not suit him. And even before she had noticed that something was already missing from his face, as if since he had cut his hair, his face had changed too. She wanted to say something hurtful to him, but immediately she caught herself in an unfriendly feeling, became frightened and left the bedroom.

IX

Kovrin received an independent professorship. The inaugural lecture was scheduled for December 2nd and an announcement about this was posted in the university corridor. But on the appointed day he notified the student inspector by telegram that he would not give lectures due to illness.

     He was coughing up blood. He spat blood, but it happened about twice a month that it flowed abundantly, and then he became extremely weak and fell into a drowsy state. This illness did not particularly frighten him, as he knew that his late mother lived with exactly the same illness for ten years, even longer; and the doctors assured him that it was not dangerous, and advised only not to worry, to lead a regular life and to speak less.

     In January the lecture did not take place again for the same reason, and in February it was already too late to begin the course. It had to be postponed until next year.

     He was now living not with Tanya, but with another woman who was two years older than him and cared for him like a child. His mood was peaceful, submissive: he willingly obeyed, and when Varvara Nikolaevna – that was the name of his companion – decided to take him to the Crimea, he agreed, although he had a premonition that nothing good would come of this trip.

     They arrived in Sevastopol in the evening and stopped at a hotel to rest and travel to Yalta the next day. Both were exhausted from the journey. Varvara Nikolaevna drank tea, lay down to sleep and soon fell asleep. But Kovrin did not go to bed. Still at home, an hour before leaving for the station, he had received a letter from Tanya and did not dare to open it, and now it lay in his side pocket, and the thought of it unpleasantly agitated him. Sincerely, in the depths of his soul, his marriage to Tanya he now considered a mistake, was glad that he had finally parted with her, and the memory of this woman, who in the end had turned into a walking living relic, and in whom, it seemed, everything was already dead except for large, intently gazing, intelligent eyes, the memory of her aroused in him only pity and annoyance with himself. The handwriting on the envelope reminded him how two years ago he had been unjust and cruel, how he had vented on innocent people his spiritual emptiness, boredom, loneliness and dissatisfaction with life. By the way, he recalled how once he had torn his dissertation into small pieces and all the articles written during his illness, and how he had thrown them out the window, and the pieces, flying in the wind, caught on trees and flowers; in every line he saw strange, unfounded pretensions, frivolous audacity, impudence, megalomania, and this made such an impression on him as if he were reading a description of his own vices;but when the last notebook was torn and flew out the window, for some reason he suddenly felt annoyed and bitter, he went to his wife and said harsh words to her

VOLODYA

On one of the summer Sundays, around five o'clock in the evening, Volodya, a seventeen-year-old youth, unattractive, sickly and timid, sat in a gazebo on the dacha of the Shumikhins and was bored. His gloomy thoughts flowed in three directions. First, tomorrow, on Monday, he had to take an exam in mathematics; he knew that if he failed to solve the written problem tomorrow, he would be expelled, since he had been sitting in the sixth grade for two years and had an annual mark in algebra of 2 3/4. Second, his stay with the Shumikhins, people who were wealthy and pretended to aristocracy, caused constant pain to his pride. It seemed to him that Mme Shumikhina and her nieces looked at him and his maman as poor relatives and hangers-on, that they did not respect maman and made fun of her. Once he accidentally overheard Mme Shumikhina saying on the terrace to her cousin Anna Fedorovna that his maman continued to try to look young and make herself beautiful, that she never paid her debts and had a fondness for other people's shoes and other people's tobacco. Every day Volodya begged maman not to go to the Shumikhins, described to her what an insulting role she played with these people, tried to convince her, spoke rudely, but she, frivolous, spoiled, having spent two fortunes in her lifetime - her own and her husband's, always drawn to high society, did not understand him, and Volodya had to accompany her to the hated dacha twice a week.

Third, the youth could not for a moment rid himself of a strange, unpleasant feeling that was completely new to him... It seemed to him that he was in love with the cousin and guest of the Shumikhins, Anna Fedorovna. She was an active, loud-voiced and laughing lady, about thirty years old, healthy, strong, rosy, with round shoulders, a round fat chin and with a constant smile on thin lips. She was not beautiful and not young - Volodya knew this perfectly well, but for some reason he was unable to stop thinking about her, not looking at her, when she, playing croquet, shrugged her round shoulders and moved her smooth back or after a long laugh and running up the stairs fell into an armchair and, squinting her eyes, breathing heavily, pretended that her chest was tight and stuffy. She was married. Her husband, a respectable architect, came to the dacha once a week, slept well and returned to the city. The strange feeling began in Volodya with the fact that he unreasonably came to hate this architect and was glad every time he left for the city.

Now, sitting in the gazebo and thinking about tomorrow's exam and about maman, who was being laughed at, he felt a strong desire to see Nyuta (as the Shumikhins called Anna Fedorovna), to hear her laughter, the rustle of her dress... This desire was not like that pure, poetic love that was familiar to him from novels and which he dreamed of every evening as he went to bed; it was strange, incomprehensible, he was ashamed of it and feared it, as something very bad and unclean, something that was hard to admit to oneself...

- This is not love, - he said to himself. - One does not fall in love with thirty-year-old married women... This is simply a little intrigue... Yes, an intrigue...

Thinking about the intrigue, he remembered his invincible timidity, the absence of a mustache, freckles, narrow eyes, imagined himself standing next to Nyuta - and this pair seemed to him impossible; then he hastened to imagine himself as handsome, bold, witty, dressed in the very latest fashion...

At the height of his reverie, when he, hunched over and looking at the ground, sat in a dark corner of the gazebo, light footsteps were heard. Someone was slowly walking along the path. Soon the footsteps faded and something white flickered at the entrance.

- Is anyone here? - asked a woman's voice.

Volodya recognized this voice and looked up in fright.

- Who is there? - asked Nyuta, entering the gazebo. - Ah, it's you, Volodya? What are you doing here? Thinking? And how can one do nothing but think, think, think... one could go mad that way!

Volodya stood up and looked at Nyuta in confusion. She had just returned from the bathhouse. On her shoulders hung a sheet and a fluffy towel,and from under the white silk headscarf wet hair peeked out, sticking to her forehead. From her came a damp, cool smell of the bathhouse and almond soap. From the quick walk she was out of breath. The top button of her blouse was unbuttoned, so the young man could see both her neck and chest.

     - Why are you silent? - asked Nyuta, looking over Volodya. - It is impolite to be silent when a lady is speaking to you. What a seal you are, Volodya! You just sit there, say nothing, think like some philosopher. There is no life or fire in you at all! You are disagreeable, really... At your age one should live, jump about, chatter, court women, fall in love.

     Volodya looked at the sheet that was held up by a white, plump hand, and thought...

     - He is silent! - Nyuta was surprised. - This is even strange... Listen, be a man! Well, at least smile! Ugh, disagreeable philosopher! - she laughed. You know, Volodya, why are you such a seal? Because you do not court women. Why do you not court them? True, there are no young ladies here, but nothing prevents you from courting ladies! Why do you not court me, for example?

     Volodya listened and, in heavy, tense thought, scratched his temple.

     - Only very proud people are silent and love solitude, - Nyuta continued, pulling his hand away from his temple. - You are a proud man, Volodya. Why do you look from under your brows? Be so kind as to look me straight in the face! Come on, seal!

     Volodya decided to speak. Wishing to smile, he twitched his lower lip, blinked his eyes and again reached his hand to his temple.

     - I... I love you! - he said.

     Nyuta raised her eyebrows in surprise and laughed.

     - What do I hear?! - she sang, as opera singers sing when they hear something terrible. - What? What did you say? Repeat it, repeat it...

     - I... I love you! - Volodya repeated.

     And without any will of his own, understanding nothing and thinking of nothing, he took half a step toward Nyuta and took her hand above the wrist. His eyes grew dim and tears appeared, the whole world turned into one large, shaggy towel that smelled of the bathhouse.

     - Bravo, bravo! - he heard cheerful laughter. - Why are you silent? I want you to speak! Well?

     Seeing that he was not prevented from holding her hand, Volodya looked at Nyuta's laughing face and awkwardly, uncomfortably took her by the waist with both hands, with the fingers of both his hands meeting on her back. He held her by the waist with both hands, and she, throwing her arms back over her head and showing dimples on her elbows, adjusted her hair under the headscarf and spoke in a calm voice:

     - You must, Volodya, be clever, amiable, charming, and one can become so only under the influence of female society. However, you have such an unpleasant... angry face. You must speak, laugh... Yes, Volodya, do not be a bore, you are young and will have time yet to philosophize. Well, let me go, I will go. Let me go!

     She freed her waist without effort and, humming something, left the arbor. Volodya remained alone. He smoothed his hair, smiled and walked from corner to corner three times, then sat on a bench and smiled again. He was unbearably ashamed, so much so that he was even surprised that human shame could reach such sharpness and strength. From shame he smiled, whispered some incoherent words and gestured.

     He was ashamed that he had just been treated like a boy, ashamed of his timidity, and mainly ashamed that he had dared to take a respectable married woman by the waist, although by age, by his outward qualities, or by his social position, as it seemed to him, he had no right to do so.

     He jumped up, left the arbor and, without looking back, went deep into the garden away from the house.

     "Ah, if only I could leave here soon! - he thought, clutching his head. God, if only I could!"

     The train on which Volodya was to travel with maman departed at eight forty. There were about three hours left before the train, but he would have gladly gone to the station now with pleasure, without waiting for maman.

     At eight o'clock he approached the house. His entire figure expressed determination: what will be, will be! He decided to enter boldly, to look straight,speak loudly, no matter what.

     He passed through the terrace, a large hall, the living room and stopped in the last one to catch his breath. From here one could hear how they were drinking tea in the neighboring dining room. Mme Shumikhin, maman and Nyuta were talking about something and laughing.

     Volodya listened.

     - I assure you! - said Nyuta. - I couldn't believe my own eyes! When he began to declare his love to me and even, imagine, took me by the waist, I didn't recognize him. And you know, he has a manner! When he said he was in love with me, there was something bestial in his face, like a Circassian.

     - Really! - gasped maman, bursting into prolonged laughter. - Really! How he reminds me of his father!

     Volodya ran back and rushed into the fresh air. "And how can they talk about this out loud! - he tormented himself, throwing up his hands and looking at the sky in horror. - They talk out loud, coldly... And maman laughed... maman! My God, why did you give me such a mother? Why?"

     But he had to go back into the house, no matter what. He walked along the alley three times, calmed down a little and entered the house.

     - Why don't you come to tea on time? - asked Mme Shumikhin sternly.

     - I'm sorry, I... I have to go, - he muttered, not raising his eyes. Maman, it's already eight o'clock!

     - Go yourself, my dear, - said maman languidly, - I'm staying the night at Lili's. Goodbye, my friend... Let me make the sign of the cross over you...

     She made the sign of the cross over her son and said in French, addressing Nyuta:

     - He looks a little like Lermontov... Don't you think?

     Barely saying goodbye and not looking at anyone's face, Volodya left the dining room. Ten minutes later he was already walking along the road to the station and was glad of it. Now he was neither frightened nor ashamed, he breathed easily and freely.

     Half a verst from the station he sat down on a stone by the road and began to look at the sun, which had hidden more than halfway behind the embankment. At the station, lights were already lit here and there, one dim green light flickered, but the train was not yet visible. It was pleasant for Volodya to sit, not move and listen to how evening was gradually coming on. The twilight of the gazebo, footsteps, the smell of the bathhouse, laughter and the waist - all this appeared in his imagination with striking clarity and all this was no longer as frightening and significant as before...

     "Nonsense... She didn't pull her hand away and laughed when I held her by the waist, - he thought, - so she liked it. If she had disliked it, she would have gotten angry..."

     And now Volodya was annoyed that there, in the gazebo, he hadn't had enough courage. He was sorry that he was leaving so foolishly, and he was already convinced that if that moment were to happen again, he would be braver and would look at things more simply.

     And for the moment to happen again was not difficult. The Shumikhins walk for a long time after supper. If Volodya goes for a walk with Nyuta through the dark garden, then - there's the moment!

     "I'll go back, - he thought, - and I'll leave tomorrow with the morning train... I'll say I missed the train".

     And he went back... Mme Shumikhin, maman, Nyuta and one of the nieces were sitting on the terrace and playing whist. When Volodya lied to them that he had missed the train, they became worried that he might miss his exam tomorrow, and advised him to get up early. All the time they were playing cards, he sat aside, eagerly looking over Nyuta and waiting... In his head a plan was already ready: he would approach Nyuta in the darkness, take her by the hand, then embrace her; there was no need to say anything, as both would understand everything without words.

     But after supper the ladies did not go for a walk in the garden and continued to play cards. They played until one o'clock in the morning and then went to bed.

     "How stupid this all is! - Volodya was annoyed, getting into bed. - But never mind, I'll wait for tomorrow... Tomorrow again in the gazebo. Never mind..."

     He did not try to fall asleep, but sat in bed, hugging his knees, and thought. The thought of the exam was repugnant to him. He had already decided that he would be expelled and that there was nothing terrible in this expulsion. On the contrary, everything was very good, even very good. Tomorrow he would be free as a bird, would put on civilian clothes, would smoke openly, come here and court Nyuta,whenever; and he would no longer be a gymnasium student, but a "young man". And the rest, what is called a career and the future, is so clear: Volodya will enlist as a volunteer, become a telegraph operator, or finally work in a pharmacy, where he will rise to the rank of pharmacist... how many positions are there? An hour or two passed, and he still sat and thought...

At three o'clock, when it was already getting light, the door creaked cautiously and maman entered the room.

- You're not sleeping? - she asked, yawning. - Sleep, sleep, I'm just here for a moment... I only need to get some drops...

- What for?

- Poor Lili has spasms again. Sleep, my child, you have an exam tomorrow...

She took a bottle of something from the small cupboard, went to the window, read the label, and left.

- Marya Leontyevna, those aren't the right drops! - Volodya heard a woman's voice a moment later. - That's lily of the valley, but Lili is asking for morphine. Is your son sleeping? Ask him to find it...

It was Nyuta's voice. Volodya turned cold. He quickly put on his trousers, threw his overcoat over his shoulders, and went to the door.

- Do you understand? Morphine! - Nyuta explained in a whisper. - It should be written in Latin. Wake Volodya, he'll find it...

Maman opened the door, and Volodya saw Nyuta. She was wearing the same blouse she wore for swimming. Her hair was uncombed, scattered over her shoulders, her face sleepy, dark in the twilight...

- Here's Volodya, he's not sleeping... - she said. - Volodya, look for morphine in the cupboard, dear! This Lili is such a trial... She always has something wrong.

Maman mumbled something, yawned, and left.

- Look then, - said Nyuta. - Why are you standing there?

Volodya went to the small cupboard, knelt down, and began sorting through bottles and boxes of medicines. His hands were trembling, and in his chest and stomach there was a sensation as if cold waves were running through all his insides. From the smell of ether, carbolic acid, and various herbs, which he grasped at unnecessarily with trembling hands and which scattered because of this, he felt suffocated and his head was spinning.

"It seems maman has left, - he thought. - That's good... good..."

- Will you be long? - asked Nyuta in a drawn-out voice.

- Just a moment... Here, this seems to be morphine... - said Volodya, reading the word "morph..." on one of the labels. - Here you are!

Nyuta stood in the doorway so that one of her legs was in the corridor and the other in his room. She was fixing her hair, which was so thick and long it was difficult to fix! - and looked at Volodya absently. In her loose blouse, sleepy, with her hair down, in the meager light that came into the room from the white but not yet sun-lit sky, she seemed to Volodya enchanting, luxurious... Enchanted, trembling all over and with pleasure recalling how he had embraced this wonderful body in the arbor, he handed her the drops and said:

- How you are...

- What?

She entered the room.

- What? - she asked, smiling.

He was silent and looked at her, then, as in the arbor, took her hand... And she looked at him, smiled, and waited: what would happen next?

- I love you... - he whispered.

She stopped smiling, thought, and said:

- Wait, it seems someone is coming. Oh, these gymnasium students! - she said in a low voice, going to the door and looking out into the corridor. - No, I don't see anyone...

She returned...

Then it seemed to Volodya that the room, Nyuta, the dawn, and he himself - everything merged into one sensation of acute, extraordinary, unprecedented happiness, for which one could give up one's whole life and go to eternal torment, but half a minute passed, and it all suddenly disappeared. Volodya saw only a full, ugly face, distorted by an expression of disgust, and suddenly felt revulsion toward what had happened.

- However, I need to go, - said Nyuta, looking at Volodya with disgust. - How ugly, pitiful... ugh, nasty duckling!

How ugly her long hair now seemed to Volodya, her loose blouse, her steps, her voice!..

"Nasty duckling... - he thought after she left. - I really am nasty... Everything is nasty".

In the yard the sun was already rising, birds were singing loudly; one could hear,how the gardener walked in the garden and how his wheelbarrow creaked... And a little later came the lowing of cows and the sounds of a shepherd's pipe. Sunlight and sounds spoke of the fact that somewhere in this world there existed a life that was pure, elegant, poetic. But where was it? Maman had never spoken to Volodya of it, nor had any of the people who surrounded him.

     When the footman woke him for the morning train, he pretended to be asleep...

     "To hell with it all!" he thought.

     He got out of bed at eleven o'clock. Combing his hair before the mirror and looking at his ugly face, pale from a sleepless night, he thought:

     "Absolutely right... The Ugly Duckling."

     When maman saw him and was horrified that he was not at the exam, Volodya said:

     - I overslept, maman... But don't worry, I will present a medical certificate.

     Mme Shumikhin and Nyuta woke up at one o'clock. Volodya heard how the awakened Mme Shumikhin loudly opened her window, how Nyuta answered her rough voice with a rolling laugh. He saw how the door opened and from the drawing room a procession of nieces and hangers-on stretched toward breakfast (among the latter was maman too), how Nyuta's washed, laughing face flashed by, and next to her face the black eyebrows and beard of the architect who had just arrived.

     Nyuta was wearing a Little Russian costume, which did not suit her at all and made her clumsy; the architect made crude and flat jokes; the cutlets served for breakfast had far too much onion in them - so it seemed to Volodya. It also seemed to him that Nyuta was deliberately laughing loudly and glancing in his direction, thereby trying to make him understand that the memory of the night did not trouble her in the least and that she did not notice the presence at the table of the ugly duckling.

     At four o'clock Volodya was driving with maman to the station. Dirty memories, a sleepless night, the impending expulsion from the gymnasium, pangs of conscience - all of this now aroused in him a heavy, gloomy malice. He looked at maman's thin profile, at her small nose, at the waterproof coat given to her by Nyuta, and muttered:

     - Why do you powder yourself? It is not becoming at your age! You are trying to make yourself beautiful, you don't pay your debts, you smoke other people's tobacco... it's disgusting! I don't love you... I don't love you!

     He insulted her, and she looked about in fright, threw up her hands and whispered in horror:

     - What are you saying, my dear? My God, the coachman will hear! Be quiet, or the coachman will hear! He hears everything!

     - I don't love you... I don't love you! - he continued, gasping. - You are immoral, soulless... Don't you dare wear that waterproof coat! Do you hear? Or I will tear it to shreds...

     - Come to your senses, my child! - maman wept. - The coachman will hear!

     - And where is my father's fortune? Where is your money? You have squandered it all! I am not ashamed of my poverty, but I am ashamed that I have such a mother... When my friends ask about you, I always blush.

     On the train they had to travel two stations to the city. All the time Volodya stood on the platform and trembled all over. He did not want to enter the carriage, since his mother, whom he hated, was sitting there. He hated himself, the conductors, the smoke from the locomotive, the cold, which he blamed for his trembling... And the heavier his heart became, the more strongly he felt that somewhere in this world, among some people, there existed a life that was pure, noble, warm, elegant, full of love, tenderness, joy, freedom... He felt this and yearned so strongly that even one passenger, having looked intently at his face, asked:

     - Probably your teeth are aching?

     In the city maman and Volodya lived with Maria Petrovna, a lady of noble birth, who rented a large apartment and sublet it to tenants. Maman rented two rooms: in one, with windows, where her bed stood and two pictures in gold frames hung on the walls, she lived herself, and in the other, adjoining, small and dark room, lived Volodya. There stood a sofa on which he slept, and besides this sofa there was no other furniture; the entire room was occupied by wicker baskets with clothes, hatboxes and all sorts of junk that maman kept for some reason.Volodya prepared lessons in his mother's room or in the "common room" - that's what the large room was called where all the residents gathered during dinner and in the evenings.

I

When visitors to the provincial city of S. complained about boredom and the monotony of life, the local residents, as if justifying themselves, would say that on the contrary, S. was very pleasant, that S. had a library, a theater, a club, there were balls, and finally, there were intelligent, interesting, and agreeable families with whom one could become acquainted. And they pointed to the Turkin family as the most educated and talented.

This family lived on the main street, near the governor's residence, in their own house. Turkin himself, Ivan Petrovich, a full-figured, handsome brunette with sideburns, organized amateur theatrical performances for charitable purposes, himself played old generals and did so while coughing in a very amusing way. He knew many anecdotes, riddles, and sayings, loved to joke and jest, and always had such an expression that one could not tell whether he was joking or speaking seriously. His wife, Vera Iosifovna, a thin, pretty lady in pince-nez, wrote stories and novels and willingly read them aloud to her guests. Their daughter, Ekaterina Ivanovna, a young girl, played the piano. In short, each family member had some talent of their own. The Turkins received guests warmly and displayed their talents cheerfully, with heartfelt simplicity. In their large stone house it was spacious and cool in summer, half the windows opened onto an old shady garden where nightingales sang in spring; when guests sat in the house, knives clattered in the kitchen, the courtyard smelled of fried onions – and this each time promised an abundant and delicious supper.

And Doctor Startsev, Dmitry Ionych, when he had just been appointed a district physician and settled in Dyalyzh, nine versts from S., was also told that as an educated man, he absolutely must become acquainted with the Turkins. One winter on the street he was introduced to Ivan Petrovich; they talked about the weather, the theater, cholera, and an invitation followed. In spring, on a holiday – it was Ascension Day – after seeing patients, Startsev set off to the city to amuse himself a little and incidentally buy himself a few things. He walked on foot, unhurried (he did not yet have his own horses), and all the while hummed: When I had not yet drunk tears from the cup of existence…

In the city he had dinner, walked in the garden, then somehow the invitation from Ivan Petrovich came to mind of its own accord, and he decided to go to the Turkins to see what sort of people they were.

– Good day, please, – said Ivan Petrovich, meeting him on the porch. – Very, very glad to see such a pleasant guest. Come, I shall introduce you to my dear wife. I tell him, Verochka, – he continued, introducing the doctor to his wife, – I tell him that he has no right whatsoever to sit in his hospital, he must devote his leisure to society. Is that not so, my dear?

– Sit here, – said Vera Iosifovna, seating the guest beside her. – You may attend to me. My husband is jealous, he is an Othello, but we shall try to behave so that he notices nothing.

– Ah, you little minx, you spoiled thing… – Ivan Petrovich murmured tenderly and kissed her on the forehead. – You have come at a most opportune time, – he turned again to the guest, – my dear wife has written a major novel and today will read it aloud.

– Zhanchik, – said Vera Iosifovna to her husband, – tell them to bring us tea.

Startsev was introduced to Ekaterina Ivanovna, an eighteen-year-old girl, very much like her mother, equally thin and pretty. Her expression was still childlike and her waist thin and delicate; and her virginal, already developed breast, beautiful and healthy, spoke of spring, real spring. Then they drank tea with jam, with honey, with candies and with very tasty cookies that melted in the mouth. As evening approached, guests gradually gathered, and to each of them Ivan Petrovich turned his laughing eyes and said:

– Good day, please.

Then everyone sat in the drawing room with very serious faces, and Vera Iosifovna read her novel. She began thus: "The frost was growing stronger…" The windows were wide open, one could hear knives clattering in the kitchen, and the smell of fried onions wafted in… In the soft, deep armchairs it was comfortable, the lights flickered so tenderly in the twilight of the drawing room; and now,On a summer evening, when voices and laughter drifted in from the street and the scent of lilac wafted from the courtyard, it was hard to understand how the frost was strengthening and how the setting sun illuminated the snowy plain with its cold rays and a lone traveler walking along the road; Vera Iosifovna was reading about how a young, beautiful countess established schools, hospitals, and libraries in her country estate and how she fell in love with a wandering artist, – she was reading about things that never happen in life, and yet it was pleasant to listen, comfortable, and such good, peaceful thoughts came to mind, – one did not want to get up.

     – Not bad… – Ivan Petrovich said quietly.

     And one of the guests, listening and letting his thoughts wander somewhere very, very far away, said barely audibly:

     – Yes… indeed…

     An hour passed, then another. In the city garden nearby an orchestra was playing and a choir of singers was singing. When Vera Iosifovna closed her notebook, they were silent for about five minutes and listened to "Luchinushka," which the choir was singing, and this song conveyed what was not in the novel and what does happen in life.

     – Do you publish your works in journals? – Startsev asked Vera Iosifovna.

     – No, – she answered, – I don't publish anywhere. I write and hide it in my cabinet. Why publish? – she explained. – After all, we have the means.

     And everyone sighed for some reason.

     – Now you, Kotik, play something, – Ivan Petrovich said to his daughter.

     They raised the lid of the piano, opened the notes that were already laid out. Ekaterina Ivanovna sat down and struck the keys with both hands; and then immediately struck again with all her might, and again, and again; her shoulders and chest shook, she stubbornly struck the same spot over and over, and it seemed she would not stop until she drove the keys into the piano. The drawing room filled with thunder; everything thundered: the floor, the ceiling, the furniture… Ekaterina Ivanovna was playing a difficult passage, interesting precisely because of its difficulty, long and monotonous, and Startsev, listening, imagined stones tumbling down from a high mountain, tumbling and tumbling, and he wished they would stop falling soon, and at the same time Ekaterina Ivanovna, flushed from exertion, strong, energetic, with a curl fallen on her forehead, pleased him very much. After the winter spent in Dyalyzh, among the sick and peasants, to sit in a drawing room, to look at this young, elegant and probably pure being and to listen to these loud, tiresome, but still cultured sounds, – was so pleasant, so new…

     – Well, Kotik, today you played as never before, – said Ivan Petrovich with tears in his eyes when his daughter finished and stood up. – Die, Denis, you won't write better.

     Everyone surrounded her, congratulated her, marveled, assured her that they had not heard such music in a long time, and she listened silently, smiling slightly, and triumph was written all over her figure.

     – Splendid! Excellent!

     – Splendid! – said Startsev as well, carried away by the general enthusiasm. – Where did you study music? – he asked Ekaterina Ivanovna. – At the conservatory?

     – No, I'm only planning to go to the conservatory, but for now I studied here with Madame Zavlovskaya.

     – Did you finish the course at the local gymnasium?

     – Oh no! – Vera Iosifovna answered for her. – We hired tutors at home; in a gymnasium or institute, you must agree, there could be bad influences; while a girl is growing up, she should be under the influence of her mother alone.

     – But I will still go to the conservatory, – said Ekaterina Ivanovna.

     – No, Kotik loves her mama. Kotik will not upset papa and mama.

     – No, I will go! I will go! – said Ekaterina Ivanovna, joking and being capricious, and stamped her foot.

     And at supper Ivan Petrovich was already showing off his talents. He, laughing only with his eyes, told anecdotes, made witty remarks, posed funny riddles and solved them himself, and all the time spoke in his extraordinary language, developed through long practice in wit and obviously long since become his habit: bolshinsky, not bad, you twisted me thank you…

     But that was not all. When the guests, sated and satisfied, crowded in the entrance hall, picking up their coats and canes, the footman Pavlusha fussed around them, or,как его звали здесь, Pava, a boy of about fourteen, with a close-cropped haircut and full cheeks.

     – Come on, Pava, show us! – Ivan Petrovich said to him.

     Pava struck a pose, raised his hand upward and spoke in a tragic tone:

     – Die, unhappy one!

     And everyone burst out laughing.

     "Amusing," thought Startsev, going out into the street.

     He went into a restaurant and had a beer, then set off on foot to his place in Dyalyzh. As he walked, he hummed the whole way: Your voice for me, both tender and languorous…



     After walking nine versts and then going to bed, he did not feel the slightest fatigue, but on the contrary, it seemed to him that he would gladly have walked another twenty versts.

     "Not bad…" he recalled as he was falling asleep, and laughed.



II



     Startsev kept meaning to visit the Turkins, but there was a great deal of work at the hospital, and he could not find a free hour. More than a year passed in this way in labor and solitude; but then a letter arrived from the city in a blue envelope…

     Vera Iosifovna had long suffered from migraines, but recently, when Kotik frightened her every day by saying she would go to the conservatory, the attacks had begun to recur more and more frequently. All the city doctors had visited the Turkins; finally it came to the turn of the zemstvo doctor. Vera Iosifovna wrote him a touching letter in which she asked him to come and ease her suffering. Startsev came and after that began to visit the Turkins often, very often… He had indeed helped Vera Iosifovna somewhat, and she was already telling all her guests that he was an extraordinary, wonderful doctor. But he was going to the Turkins now not for her migraine…

     A holiday. Ekaterina Ivanovna had finished her long, tedious exercises on the piano. Then they sat for a long time in the dining room and drank tea, and Ivan Petrovich told something funny. But then the doorbell rang; someone had to go to the entrance to greet a guest; Startsev took advantage of the moment of confusion and said to Ekaterina Ivanovna in a whisper, greatly agitated:

     – For God's sake, I beg you, do not torment me, let us go to the garden!

     She shrugged her shoulders as if puzzled and not understanding what he wanted from her, but she got up and went.

     – You play the piano for three, four hours, – he said, following her, – then you sit with mama, and there is no possibility of talking with you. Give me at least a quarter of an hour, I beg you.

     Autumn was approaching, and in the old garden it was quiet and sad, and dark leaves lay on the paths. It was already getting dark early.

     – I have not seen you for a whole week, – Startsev continued, – and if you only knew what suffering that is! Let us sit down. Listen to me.

     They both had a favorite place in the garden: a bench under an old, wide maple tree. And now they sat on this bench.

     – What do you want? – asked Ekaterina Ivanovna dryly, in a businesslike tone.

     – I have not seen you for a whole week, I have not heard you for so long. I passionately want, I crave your voice. Speak.

     She delighted him with her freshness, the naive expression of her eyes and cheeks. Even in the way her dress sat on her, he saw something extraordinarily charming, touching in its simplicity and naive grace. And at the same time, despite this naivety, she seemed to him very intelligent and developed beyond her years. With her he could talk about literature, about art, about anything, could complain to her about life, about people, although during serious conversation, it sometimes happened that she would suddenly start laughing inappropriately or run into the house. She, like almost all educated girls, read a great deal (in general, very little was read in S., and in the local library they even said that if it were not for the girls and young Jews, they might as well close the library); this pleased Startsev infinitely, he asked her with excitement each time what she had been reading in recent days, and, enchanted, listened when she told him.

     – What have you read this week while we have not seen each other? – he asked now. – Speak, I beg you.

     – I read Pisemsky.

     – What exactly?

     – "A Thousand Souls," – answered Kotik. – And how funny Pisemsky's name was: Alexei Theofilaktych!

     – Where are you going? – Startsev was horrified when she suddenly got up and went toward the house. – I absolutely must talk with you,I must explain myself… Stay with me for just five minutes! I beg you!

She stopped, as if wanting to say something, then awkwardly thrust a note into his hand and ran into the house, and there she sat down at the piano again.

"Today, at eleven o'clock in the evening," Startsev read, "be at the cemetery near the Demetti monument."

"Well, this is not clever at all," he thought, coming to his senses. "What does the cemetery have to do with it? Why?"

It was clear: Kotik was joking. Who, in fact, would seriously think of arranging a meeting at night, far outside the city, at a cemetery, when it could easily be arranged on the street, in the city garden? And was it fitting for him, a zemstvo doctor, an intelligent, respectable man, to sigh, receive little notes, wander about cemeteries, do foolish things that even gymnasium students now laugh at? Where would this romance lead? What would his colleagues say when they found out? So thought Startsev, wandering around the tables at the club, and at half past ten he suddenly got up and drove to the cemetery.

He already had his own pair of horses and a coachman named Panteleimon in a velvet waistcoat. The moon was shining. It was quiet, warm, but warm in an autumnal way. In the suburbs, near the slaughterhouses, dogs were howling. Startsev left his horses at the edge of the city, in one of the side streets, and walked to the cemetery on foot. "Everyone has their own peculiarities," he thought. "Kotik is also strange and – who knows? – perhaps she is not joking, she will come," and he surrendered to this weak, empty hope, and it intoxicated him.

He walked half a verst across the field. The cemetery was marked in the distance by a dark strip, like a forest or a large garden. A white stone fence appeared, gates… In the moonlight, one could read on the gates: "The hour cometh…" Startsev entered through the gate, and the first thing he saw was white crosses and monuments on both sides of a wide avenue and black shadows from them and from the poplars; and all around, far and wide, white and black were visible, and sleepy trees bent their branches over the white. It seemed that it was brighter here than in the field; the maple leaves, resembling paws, stood out sharply against the yellow sand of the avenues and on the slabs, and the inscriptions on the monuments were clear. At first, Startsev was struck by what he now saw for the first time in his life and what would probably never happen to him to see again: a world unlike anything else – a world where the moonlight is so beautiful and soft, as if here were its cradle, where there is no life, no and no, but in each dark poplar, in each grave one feels the presence of a mystery, promising a quiet, beautiful, eternal life. From the slabs and withered flowers, together with the autumn scent of leaves, comes forgiveness, sorrow, and peace.

All around was silence; in deep humility the stars looked down from the sky, and Startsev's footsteps sounded so sharp and out of place. And only when the church bells began to strike and he imagined himself dead, buried here forever, did it seem to him that someone was looking at him, and for a moment he thought that this was not peace and not silence, but the dull anguish of non-being, suppressed despair…

The Demetti monument in the form of a chapel, with an angel on top; once an Italian opera company had passed through the city, one of the singers died, she was buried and this monument was erected. No one in the city remembered her anymore, but the lamp above the entrance reflected the moonlight and seemed to be burning.

No one was there. And who would come here at midnight? But Startsev waited, and, as if the moonlight were heating passion in him, he waited passionately and imagined kisses, embraces. He sat by the monument for about half an hour, then walked along the side avenues, hat in hand, waiting and thinking about how many beautiful, charming women and girls were buried here in these graves, who had loved, burned with passion at night, surrendering to caresses. How poorly mother nature jokes with man, how painful it is to realize this! Startsev thought so, and at the same time he wanted to cry out that he wanted, that he was waiting for love at any cost; before him were no longer pieces of marble, but beautiful bodies, he saw forms that modestly hid in the shadow of trees, he felt warmth, and this yearning became painful…

And just as if a curtain had fallen, the moon went behind the clouds,And suddenly everything grew dark around him. Startsev barely found the gate – it was already as dark as on an autumn night – then for about an hour and a half he wandered, searching for the lane where he had left his horses.

     – I am tired, I can barely stand on my feet, – he said to Panteleimon.

     And, settling with pleasure into the carriage, he thought:

     "Oh, I shouldn't get fat!"



III



     The next evening he went to the Turkins to make a proposal. But this turned out to be inconvenient, as Ekaterina Ivanovna was having her hair done by a hairdresser in her room. She was preparing to go to the club for a dance evening.

     He had to sit in the dining room for a long time again and drink tea. Ivan Petrovich, seeing that his guest was pensive and bored, took some notes from his waistcoat pocket and read a funny letter from a German manager about how all the locks in the estate had broken and the pantry had collapsed.

     "They will probably give a considerable dowry," thought Startsev, listening absently.

     After a sleepless night he was in a state of bewilderment, as if he had been given something sweet and soporific; his soul felt foggy but joyful and warm, and at the same time in his head some cold, heavy piece reasoned:

     "Stop, before it is too late! Is she a match for you? She is spoiled, capricious, sleeps until two o'clock, and you are a deacon's son, a rural doctor…"

     "Well, what of it?" he thought. "So be it."

     "Besides, if you marry her," the piece continued, "her relatives will force you to leave rural service and live in the city."

     "Well, what of it?" he thought. "In the city, then in the city. They will give a dowry, we will furnish a place…"

     Finally Ekaterina Ivanovna entered in a ball gown, with a low neckline, pretty and neat, and Startsev admired her and became so delighted that he could not utter a single word, but only looked at her and laughed.

     She began to say goodbye, and he – there was no reason for him to stay – rose, saying that it was time for him to go home: patients were waiting.

     – There is nothing to be done, – said Ivan Petrovich, – go ahead, and by the way take Kotik to the club.

     In the yard it was drizzling, it was very dark, and only by Panteleimon's hoarse cough could one guess where the horses were. They raised the top of the carriage.

     – I go on the carpet, you go while you lie, – said Ivan Petrovich, seating his daughter in the carriage, – he goes while he lies… Let's go! Goodbye please!

     They drove off.

     – I was at the cemetery yesterday, – began Startsev. – How ungracious and merciless of you…

     – You were at the cemetery?

     – Yes, I was there and waited for you until almost two o'clock. I suffered…

     – And suffer, if you do not understand jokes.

     Ekaterina Ivanovna, pleased that she had played such a clever trick on the man in love and that she was loved so strongly, burst out laughing and suddenly cried out in fright, as at that very moment the horses were turning sharply into the club gates and the carriage tilted. Startsev embraced Ekaterina Ivanovna by the waist; she, frightened, pressed against him, and he could not restrain himself and passionately kissed her on the lips, on the chin, and embraced her more tightly.

     – That is enough, – she said coldly.

     And a moment later she was no longer in the carriage, and a policeman near the lit entrance of the club was shouting in a vile voice at Panteleimon:

     – Why did you stop, you crow? Drive on!

     Startsev drove home, but soon returned. Dressed in someone else's tailcoat and a white stiff collar, which somehow kept sticking up and wanted to slip off his neck, at midnight he sat in the club in the drawing room and spoke to Ekaterina Ivanovna with enthusiasm:

     – Oh, how little those know who have never loved! It seems to me that no one has ever described love truly, and it is hardly possible to describe this tender, joyful, agonizing feeling, and whoever has experienced it even once will not convey it in words. Why preambles, descriptions? Why unnecessary eloquence? My love is boundless… I beg you, I implore you, – Startsev finally uttered, – be my wife!

     – Dmitry Ionych, – said Ekaterina Ivanovna with a very serious expression, after thinking. – Dmitry Ionych, I am very grateful to you for the honor, I respect you, but… – she rose and continued standing, – but, forgive me, I cannot be your wife. Let us speak seriously.Dmitry Ionych, you know, more than anything in life I love art.
