CHAPTER THREE





     And Chichikov, in a contented mood, sat in his carriage, which had long been rolling along the main road. From the previous chapter, it was already clear what the main object of his taste and inclinations was, so it was no wonder that soon he was completely immersed in it, both in body and soul. The suppositions, estimates, and considerations wandering across his face were evidently very pleasant, as they left behind traces of a satisfied smile every minute. Engaged with them, he paid no attention to how his coachman, pleased with the reception by Manilov’s servants, was making rather sensible remarks to the dun gray lead horse, harnessed on the right side. This dun gray horse was very sly and was only pretending to pull, while the bay lead horse and the near-side dun horse, called the Assessor because he was purchased from some assessor, worked wholeheartedly, so much so that even in their eyes one could see the pleasure they got from this. “Cunning, cunning! Now I’ll outsmart you! – Selifan said, lifting himself up and cracking his whip at the lazy one. – You mind your business, you German trousers wearer! The bay is an honorable horse, he fulfills his duty, and I’ll gladly give him an extra measure because he’s an honorable horse, and the Assessor is a good horse too… Well, well! Why are you flicking your ears? You fool, listen when you're spoken to! I’m not going to teach you rude ways, you ignoramus. Look where you’re crawling!” Here he cracked his whip again, saying; “Oh, barbarian! Damn you, Bonaparte!” Then he shouted at everyone: “Hey, you dear ones!” – and whipped all three, no longer as punishment, but to show that he was pleased with them. Having given such pleasure, he again turned his speech to the dun gray horse: “You think you can hide your behavior. No, live honestly if you want to be respected. Our landlord, whose place we were at, had good people. I gladly talk when it's a good person; with a good person, we are always friends, fine acquaintances; to drink tea or have a snack – gladly, if a good person. Every good person will be given respect. Our master is respected by all, because he, hear me, fulfilled state service, he’s a councilor of course…”

     While reasoning so, Selifan finally got lost in the most distant abstractions. Had Chichikov listened closely, he would have learned many details personally related to him; but his thoughts were so occupied with his subject that only a strong thunderclap caused him to awaken and look around; the whole sky was completely covered in clouds, and the dusty postal road was sprinkled with raindrops. Finally, the thunderclap sounded again, louder and closer, and the rain suddenly poured down like from a bucket. At first, taking a slanting direction, it whipped on one side of the kibitka body, then on the other, then, changing its way of falling and becoming completely vertical, it drummed directly on the top of the body; finally, the splashes reached him in the face. This made him pull the leather curtains with two small round windows meant for observing road scenes and order Selifan to drive faster. Selifan, interrupted also in the middle of his speech, realized that there was certainly no time to waste, pulled out from under the shafts some rubbish made of gray cloth, put it on over his sleeves, grabbed the reins, and shouted at his troika, which was just shifting its feet a little as it enjoyed the relaxing effect of instructive speeches. But Selifan could not remember whether he had passed two or three turns. Thinking and recalling the road a bit, he guessed there had been many turns, all of which he had missed. Since a Russian man in decisive moments finds what to do without going into long reflections, turning right on the first crossroad, he cried: “Hey you, honorable friends!” – and set off at a gallop, hardly thinking where the chosen road would lead.

     However, the rain seemed to have set in for a long time. The dust on the road quickly turned to mud, and it became heavier for the horses to pull the carriage every minute. Chichikov had already begun to worry greatly, not seeing Sobakevich’s village for so long.According to his calculation, it should have been time to arrive long ago. He looked around, but the darkness was so thick you couldn't see a thing.

– Selifan! – he finally said, sticking his head out of the carriage.

– What, sir? – Selifan answered.

– Look around, can you see the village?

– No, sir, nowhere in sight! – After that, Selifan, waving his whip, began a long song-song, but it was something so long it seemed endless. Everything went into it: all the encouraging and urging cries used to spur horses on all across Russia from one end to the other; adjectives of all kinds thrown together without any logic, just whatever first popped into his head. Eventually, he even started calling them secretaries.

Meanwhile, Chichikov began to notice that the carriage was rocking in all directions and giving him strong jolts; this made him realize they had gone off the road and were probably dragging through a plowed field. Selifan seemed to figure it out himself but didn’t say a word.

– Hey, trickster, what road are you taking? – asked Chichikov.

– What can I do, sir, it’s this time; don’t you see the whip, it’s so dark! – Having said this, he turned the carriage so sharply that Chichikov had to hold on with both hands. Only then did he notice that Selifan was drunk.

– Hold on, hold on, you’ll tip us over! – he shouted.

– No, sir, how could I possibly tip it over, – said Selifan. – That’s not right, I know that; I definitely won’t tip it over. – Then he began to turn the carriage slightly, and turned and turned it until finally he turned it completely sideways. Chichikov, arms and legs, slipped into the mud. Selifan stopped the horses, though they would have stopped anyway because they were very exhausted. This unexpected event totally amazed him. He got down from the driver's seat, stood in front of the carriage, rested his hands on his hips while the master struggled in the mud trying to get out, and after some thought said, “Look at that, it tipped over!”

– You’re drunk as a cobbler! – said Chichikov.

– No, sir, how could I be drunk! I know it’s not good to be drunk. I talked with a friend, because with a good person you can talk, that’s not a bad thing; and we ate some snacks together. Snacks are no offense; with a good person you can have a snack.

– What did I tell you the last time you got drunk? Huh? Forgot? – said Chichikov.

– No, your honor, how could I forget? I already know my business. I know it’s not good to be drunk. I talked with a good person because…

– Just wait until I whip you, then you’ll know how to talk to a good person!

– As Your Honor wishes, – Selifan replied, agreeing to everything, – if you want to whip me, then whip me; I don’t mind at all. Why not punish me if it’s for a good reason, that’s God’s will. It’s necessary to punish because a man is playing around; order must be kept. If it’s for good cause, then whip me; why not?

To that reasoning, the master was completely at a loss for what to say. But at that moment, it seemed as if fate itself decided to take pity on him. From afar came the sound of dogs barking. Rejoicing, Chichikov ordered to spur the horses. The Russian coachman has a good instinct instead of eyes; because of this, sometimes, squinting his eyes, he drives full speed and always manages to get somewhere. Selifan, seeing not a bit, directed the horses straight toward the village, stopping only when the carriage poles hit the fence and there was absolutely nowhere else to go. Chichikov could just make out something like a roof through the thick curtain of pouring rain. He sent Selifan to find the gates, which undoubtedly would have taken a long time if there weren’t, instead of doormen in Russia, fearless dogs that announced his arrival so loudly that he put his fingers to his ears. A light flashed in one window and reached in a misty beam to the fence, showing our travelers the gates. Selifan started knocking, and soon, opening the gate, some figure covered in an outer garment appeared, and the master with his servant heard a hoarse woman’s voice:

– Who’s knocking?What were you arguing about?

     – Travelers, mother, please let me stay the night, – said Chichikov.

     – Look at you, all eager and spry, – said the old woman, – to come at this hour! This is no inn: a landowner’s house lives here.

     – What can I do, mother: you see, we lost our way. You can’t spend the night out in the steppe at this time.

     – Yes, it’s a dark hour, a bad time, – added Selifan.

     – Quiet, fool, – said Chichikov.

     – And who are you? – said the old woman.

     – A nobleman, mother.

     The word “nobleman” seemed to make the old woman think for a moment.

     – Wait, I’ll tell the lady, – she said, and after about two minutes returned holding a lantern.

     The gate opened. A light flickered in another window. The carriage, having entered the yard, stopped in front of a small house, which was hard to make out in the darkness. Only one half was lit by light coming from the windows; a puddle in front of the house was also visible, directly struck by the same light. Rain sounded loudly on the wooden roof and streamed down in small rivulets into a barrel set underneath. Meanwhile, the dogs broke out in all possible voices: one, throwing its head back, howled so long and with such effort as if receiving some god-knows-what kind of payment for it; another barked briskly, like a sexton; between them rang the restless treble, probably a young puppy’s, like a postal bell, and finally all that was topped by a bass, maybe an old dog gifted with a powerful canine nature, because it wheezed like a contrabass singer when the concert is in full swing: tenors rise on tiptoe, eager to reach a high note, and everything tries to go up, throwing back its head, but he alone, with a shaven chin tucked into his collar, squatting and sinking almost to the ground, lets out his note that shakes and rattles the glass.

     From the dogs’ barking alone, composed of such musicians, one could guess the village was decent; but the soaked and chilled hero thought of nothing but a bed. The carriage hadn’t fully stopped when he had already jumped onto the porch, staggered, and nearly fell. A woman, younger but very much like the previous one, appeared on the porch again. She led him inside. Chichikov glanced briefly around: the room was papered with old striped wallpaper; paintings of some birds; between the windows were antique small mirrors with dark frames shaped like curled leaves; behind each mirror was hidden either a letter, or an old deck of cards, or a stocking; a wall clock with painted flowers on the dial… it was impossible to notice anything more. He felt his eyes stick as if someone had smeared them with honey. A minute later, the hostess entered, a woman of advanced years, wearing a hastily put-on sleeping cap, flannel around her neck, one of those mothers, small landowners, who complain about bad harvests, losses, and keep their heads tilted slightly, while quietly gathering small amounts of money in multicolored pouches arranged in the drawers of the dresser. In one pouch, they put whole rubles, in another fifty-kopeck coins, in the third, quarters and kopecks, although at first glance it looks as if the dresser contains nothing but underwear, night shirts, spools of thread, and a torn-out salop, meant later to be turned into a dress if the old one should somehow burn during baking holiday flatbreads with various fried goodies or simply wear out on its own. But the dress won’t burn or wear out on its own: the thrifty old woman, and the salop is bound to lie long in torn condition, then pass according to her spiritual will to the niece of her grand-niece together with all sorts of other junk.

     Chichikov apologized for disturbing them with his unexpected arrival.

     – It’s nothing, nothing, – said the hostess. – What time the Lord has brought you! Such a confusion and blizzard… It would have been proper to eat something after the road, but it’s the middle of the night, no way to prepare anything.

     The hostess’s words were interrupted by a strange hissing, so that the guest was frightened; the sound was like the whole room was filled with snakes; but looking up, he calmed down, realizing,The wall clock decided to strike. Immediately after the hissing came a wheezing, and finally, straining with all its might, it struck two hours with a sound like someone banging a stick against a broken pot, after which the pendulum resumed its quiet ticking back and forth.

Chichikov thanked the hostess, saying that he needed nothing, so she shouldn’t worry about anything, that besides a bed he required nothing, and only became curious to know which places he had passed through and whether the way to landowner Sobakevich was far from here, to which the old woman replied that she had never heard of that name and that such a landowner did not exist at all.

— At least do you know Manilov? — said Chichikov.

— And who is Manilov?

— A landowner, mother.

— No, I haven’t heard of him, there’s no such landowner.

— Which ones are there?

— Bobrov, Svinin, Kanapatyev, Kharpakin, Trepakin, Pleshakov.

— Are they wealthy people or not?

— No, father, there aren’t very rich ones. Some have twenty souls, some have thirty, but there’s no one with a hundred.

Chichikov noted that he had entered a rather remote area.

— At least, how far is it to the city?

— About sixty versts. How sorry I am that there’s nothing for you to eat! Would you like some tea, sir?

— Thank you, mother. I need nothing but a bed.

— Indeed, after such a journey, you really need to rest. Here you can settle down, sir, on this couch. Hey, Fetinya, bring the mattress, pillows, and sheet. That kind of storm was sent by God: the thunder was so loud that my candle burned all night before the icon. Oh, my father, but you’re all covered in dirt like a boar! Where did you get so filthy?

— Thank God it’s only dirt, you should be thankful I didn’t break my ribs completely.

— Holy saints, what a state! Do you need me to rub your back?

— Thank you, thank you. Don’t trouble yourself, just tell your girl to dry and clean my clothes.

— Do you hear, Fetinya! — said the hostess, addressing the woman who was coming out on the porch with a candle, having already dragged in the mattress and, fluffing it on both sides with her hands, sent a flood of feathers all over the room. — Take that coat of his along with the undergarments and dry them first by the fire, like they did for the late master, then clean and beat them thoroughly.

— Yes, ma’am! — said Fetinya, spreading the sheet over the mattress and laying down the pillows.

— Well, here’s your bed ready, — said the hostess. — Farewell, sir, I wish you a restful night. Do you need anything else? Maybe you’re used, my father, to having someone scratch your heels at night? My late master couldn’t fall asleep without that.

But the guest refused even the scratching of his heels. The hostess left, and he at once hastened to undress, giving Fetinya all the gear he had taken off, both upper and lower, and Fetinya, also wishing him a peaceful night, took away these wet armor pieces. Left alone, he looked at his bed with no small pleasure; it was almost up to the ceiling. Fetinya was obviously an expert at fluffing mattresses. When he climbed onto the bed by putting a chair against it, the mattress sank almost to the floor beneath him, and the feathers, pressed out by him, scattered into every corner of the room. Extinguishing the candle, he covered himself with a calico blanket and, curling up like a pretzel under it, fell asleep at once.

He woke the next day quite late in the morning. The sun was shining through the window right into his eyes, and the flies, which had rested quietly on the walls and ceiling last night, all turned their attention to him: one sat on his lip, another on his ear, a third tried to perch right on his eye. The one that had the misfortune to land near his nostril he pulled blindly into his nose, which made him sneeze violently—an incident that caused him to wake up. Casting his eyes around the room, he now noticed that not all the pictures were birds: among them hung a portrait of Kutuzov and some old man painted in oils wearing red cuffs on his uniform, as they wore during Pavel Petrovich’s time. The clock hissed again and struck ten.A woman’s face peeked out the door and immediately withdrew, because Chichikov, wanting to sleep better, had taken off absolutely everything. The face that appeared seemed somewhat familiar to him. He began to recall: who could it be, and finally remembered that it was the hostess. He put on his shirt; the dress, already dried and cleaned, lay nearby. Dressed, he approached the mirror and sneezed again so loudly that the Indian rooster who had just come to the window – the window was very close to the ground – suddenly started chattering at him very quickly in its strange language, probably saying "I wish you health," to which Chichikov replied "fool." Approaching the window, he began to examine the views before him: the window looked almost directly into the chicken coop; at least, the narrow yard in front of him was filled with birds and all kinds of domestic animals. There was no end to the turkeys and hens; among them marched a rooster with measured steps, shaking its comb and turning its head to the side, as if listening to something; a pig with its family appeared nearby; there, rummaging through a pile of trash, it casually ate a chick and, not noticing this, continued devouring watermelon rinds in its usual manner. This small yard, or chicken coop, was blocked by a wooden fence, behind which stretched spacious gardens with cabbage, onions, potatoes, light and other household vegetables. Scattered here and there in the garden were apple trees and other fruit trees, covered with nets to protect them from magpies and sparrows, the latter moving in entire indirect flocks from one place to another. For the same reason, several scarecrows were set on tall poles, with arms outstretched; one of them wore the hostess’s own bonnet. Beyond the gardens were peasant huts, which although built scattered and not enclosed in proper streets, according to Chichikov’s observation, showed the contentment of the inhabitants, as they were properly maintained: the weathered roofing everywhere was replaced with new; gates were nowhere crooked, and in the peasants’ covered sheds facing him, he noticed a spare, almost new cart standing here and there even two. “She has a village that isn’t small,” he said and immediately began to chat and get acquainted with the hostess briefly. He looked through the crack of the door from which she had poked her head out, and, seeing her sitting at the tea table, entered with a cheerful and gentle expression.

“Hello, father. How did you sleep?” said the hostess, rising from her seat. She was dressed better than yesterday – in a dark dress and no longer in a nightcap, but something was still tied around her neck.

“Good, good,” Chichikov said, sitting in a chair. “And how are you, mother?”

“Bad, my father.”

“How so?”

“Insomnia. My lower back hurts, and the leg just above the ankle aches like this.”

“It will pass, it will pass, mother. Nothing to worry about.”

“God grant that it passes. I rubbed it with pig fat and also moistened it with turpentine. And what will you have with your tea? There’s fruit brandy in the flask.”

“Not bad, mother, we’ll sip the fruit brandy.”

Reader, I think you have already noticed that Chichikov, despite his gentle manner, spoke with more freedom than with Manilov and did not fuss at all. It must be said that if in Russia we have not yet caught up with foreigners in some things, then we have far surpassed them in manners. The shades and subtleties of our etiquette cannot be counted. A Frenchman or German never will grasp or understand all its features and differences; they will almost always speak in the same voice and language both to a millionaire and a petty tobacco dealer, though, of course, they will internally adjust somewhat before the first. Not so with us: we have sages who will speak quite differently with a landowner having two hundred souls than with one who has three hundred, and of the one with three hundred they will speak again differently than with one who has five hundred, and with the one who has five hundred, again differently than with the one who has eight hundred – in short, no matter how high it goes up to a million, all the nuances will be found. CHAPTER FOUR





     Having arrived at the tavern, Chichikov ordered to stop for two reasons. On one hand, to give the horses a rest, and on the other hand, so he himself could have a snack and refresh himself a bit. The author must admit that he greatly envies the appetite and stomach of such people. For him, all the big shots living in Petersburg and Moscow, who spend their time thinking about what to eat tomorrow and what kind of lunch to prepare the day after, and who start that lunch only after first swallowing a pill; who gorge on oysters, sea spiders, and other such wonders, and then go off to Karlsbad or the Caucasus, mean absolutely nothing. No, these gentlemen never stirred envy in him. But the middle-ranking gentlemen, who at one station order ham, at another a suckling pig, at a third a slice of sturgeon or some baked sausage with onions, and then as if nothing happened sit down at the table whenever they please, and sturgeon fish soup with burbots and milt sizzles and grumbles between their teeth, eaten with a rasstegai or kulebyaka stuffed with catfish, so that an appetite pierces one through and through – these gentlemen, precisely, enjoy an enviable gift of heaven! Not one big shot would give up half the souls of his peasants and half his estates, mortgaged as they are, just to have the kind of stomach a middle-ranking gentleman has; but the trouble is that no matter the price, estates with or without improvements cannot buy the kind of stomach that a middle-ranking gentleman has.

     The wooden, darkened tavern welcomed Chichikov under its narrow hospitable awning supported by wooden turned posts, resembling old church candlesticks. The tavern was something like a Russian izba, but somewhat larger. Carved ornamental cornices made of fresh wood around the windows and under the roof stood out sharply and vividly against its dark walls; jugs with flowers were painted on the shutters.

     Climbing the narrow wooden staircase upstairs into the wide entry hall, he met a door opening with a creak and a stout old woman in colorful calico, who said: "Come this way!" In the room were all the old acquaintances found in any small wooden tavern, which are built in considerable numbers along the roads, namely a frosted samovar, smoothly scraped pine walls, a triangular cabinet with teapots and cups in the corner, porcelain gilded eggs before the icons, hanging on blue and red ribbons, a recently rounded-up cat, a mirror reflecting four eyes instead of two, and instead of a face some kind of blotch; finally, bundles of fragrant herbs and carnations stuck by the icons, dried to such an extent that anyone wanting to smell them would only sneeze and no more.

     "Is there suckling pig?" Chichikov asked the woman standing there.

     "There is."

     "With horseradish and sour cream?"

     "With horseradish and sour cream."

     "Bring it here!"

     The old woman went rummaging and brought a plate, a napkin starched so stiff it cracked like dried bark, then a knife with a yellowed bone handle, thin like a penknife, a two-pronged fork, and a salt shaker which could not be placed upright on the table.

     Our hero, as usual, immediately struck up a conversation with her and asked whether she ran the tavern herself or if there was an owner, how much income the tavern brought, whether her sons lived with her, and if the eldest son was single or married, and what kind of wife he took, whether she had a large dowry or not, if the father-in-law was satisfied, and if he was offended for receiving few gifts at the wedding—in short, he missed nothing. It goes without saying that he was curious to find out what landlords lived in the area, and learned that there were all kinds of landlords: Plotin, Pochitaev, Mylnoi, Cheprakov the colonel, Sobakevich. "Ah! You know Sobakevich?" he asked, and immediately heard that the old woman knew not only Sobakevich but also Manilov, and that Manilov was a bit more delicate than Sobakevich: he immediately orders a chicken to be cooked, asks for veal; if there is lamb liver, he asks for lamb liver, and only tastes a little,Sobakevich will ask for one thing or another, but he’ll eat everything, even demanding extra for the same price.

While he was talking like this, eating the piglet, of which only the last piece remained, the sound of wheels of an approaching carriage was heard. Looking out the window, he saw a light britzka stopped in front of the tavern, harnessed to a troika of good horses. Two men were getting out of the britzka. One was blond, tall; the other a bit shorter, dark-haired. The blond wore a dark blue Hungarian coat, the dark-haired one simply a striped arkhaluk. A small empty carriage was trailing behind at a distance, drawn by some long-haired quadruped with torn collars and rope harnesses. The blond immediately went upstairs, while the dark-skinned one stayed behind, feeling something in the britzka, talking to the servant right there and waving to the carriage following them. His voice seemed somewhat familiar to Chichikov. While he was looking at him, the blond had already found the door and opened it. He was a tall man, thin-faced, or what you would call pallid, with reddish mustaches. Judging by his tanned face, he knew what smoke was, if not gunpowder smoke, then at least tobacco smoke. He politely bowed to Chichikov, who responded in kind. Within a few minutes they probably would have started a conversation and gotten to know each other well, since the beginning had been made, and both almost simultaneously expressed pleasure that the dust on the road had been completely settled by yesterday’s rain and now traveling was cool and pleasant, when the dark-haired companion entered, tossing his cap on the table after removing it from his head, brusquely ruffling his thick black hair with his hand. He was of medium height, a very well-built young man with full rosy cheeks, teeth white as snow, and sideburns as black as pitch. He looked fresh as blood and milk; health seemed to radiate from his face.

"Ba, ba, ba!" he suddenly exclaimed, spreading both hands at the sight of Chichikov. "What are the odds?"

Chichikov recognized Nozdrev, the very same one with whom he had dined at the prosecutor’s and who had quickly come to such familiarity with him that he began addressing him informally with "you," although, in fact, he had given no reason for that on his part.

"Where have you been?" Nozdrev said, not waiting for an answer but continuing: "Brother, I just came from the fair. Congrats: I blew all my money! Can you believe I’ve never been blown like this before in my life? I even showed up as a layman! Just look out the window!" Here he bent Chichikov’s own head so that he almost hit it against the frame. "See what rubbish! They barely dragged me along, damn it, I already climbed into his britzka." While saying this, Nozdrev pointed to his companion. "And you two aren’t acquainted yet? This is my brother-in-law Mizuyev! We talked about you all morning. 'Well, look,' I said, 'if we don’t meet Chichikov' Well, brother, if you only knew how much I lost! Believe it or not, I not only drank away four trotters — lost it all. And I have neither a chain nor a watch on me…" Chichikov looked and saw exactly that: he had neither chain nor watch. It even seemed to him that one sideburn was smaller and less thick than the other. "But if only I had twenty rubles in my pocket," Nozdrev continued, "not more than twenty exactly, I’d have won it all back, that is, besides what I won, I’d have honestly put thirty thousand in my wallet right now."

"You always said that," the blond replied, "but when I gave you fifty rubles, you lost them immediately."

"And I wouldn’t have lost them! I swear, I wouldn’t have! If I hadn’t made a fool of myself, really, I wouldn’t have. If I hadn’t bluffed on the cursed seven, I could have taken the whole bank."

"But you didn’t take it," said the blond.

"Didn’t take it because I bluffed the seven duck too soon. And you think your major plays well?"

"Whether well or not, he did beat you."

"Big deal!" said Nozdrev. "I can beat him too. No, just let him try to play doubles, then we'll see."I’ll see then what kind of player he is! But, brother Chichikov, how we partied in the first days! Truly, the fair was excellent. The merchants themselves say there has never been such a gathering. Everything I brought from the village sold at the best possible price. Ah, brother, how we partied! Now even, when you remember it... damn it! I mean, how sorry I am that you weren’t there. Imagine, just three versts from the city there stood a dragoon regiment. Can you believe that officers, no matter how many there were—forty officers alone in the city—once we started drinking, brother… Staff Captain Potselyuev… such a fine man! Mustache, brother, such mustaches! Bordeaux simply calls it a burdashka. “Bring it, brother,” he says, “the burdashka!” Lieutenant Kuvshinnikov… Ah, brother, what a lovely person! You can really say he was a ruffian in full uniform. We were all with him. What kind of wine Ponomarev gave us! You should know he’s a cheat and nothing in his shop can be trusted: he mixes all sorts of trash in the wine—sandalwood, burnt cork, and even elderberry, the scoundrel, he covers it up; but when he pulls out from the back room, which he calls special, some bottle—well, brother, you’re just in the empyrean. Our champagne was such—that next to it the governor’s is just kvass. Imagine, not cliqua, but some cliqua-matradura, which means double cliqua. And he also got one bottle of French called: bonbon. The smell? – rosette and anything you want. We partied so much!.. After us came some prince, he sent to the shop for champagne, and there wasn’t a single bottle left in the whole city, all the officers drank it all. Can you believe I drank seventeen bottles of champagne alone during lunch!

“Well, you won’t drink seventeen bottles,” remarked the blond.

“As an honest man, I say I did,” answered Nozdrev.

“You can tell yourself whatever you want, but I’m telling you, you won’t drink even ten.”

“Well, want to bet that I will!”

“What’s the bet for?”

“Well, put up the rifle you bought in the city.”

“I don’t want to.”

“Well, then put it up, try.”

“I don’t even want to try.”

“See, without a rifle you’d be like without a hat. Ah, brother Chichikov, I really regretted that you weren’t there. I know you wouldn’t have parted with Lieutenant Kuvshinnikov. You two would have gotten along so well! This isn’t like the prosecutor and all the provincial misers in our city, who tremble at every kopek. This one, brother, is in galbic, and in banchishka, and in everything you want. Ah, Chichikov, what would it have cost you to come? Really, you stink for this, such a cattle breeder! Kiss me, soul, I love you to death! Mizhuev, look, fate brought us together: what is he to me or I to him? He came from God knows where, I live here too… And how many, brother, carriages there were, and all that en gros1. I spun the fortune wheel: won two jars of pomade, a porcelain cup, and a guitar; then again I put a stake once and spun, scoundrel, still over six rubles.      Happy is the traveler who, after a long, boring road with its cold, slush, mud, sleepy station attendants, ringing bells, repairs, quarrels, coachmen, blacksmiths, and all sorts of road scoundrels, finally sees a familiar roof with lights rushing to meet them, and familiar rooms appear before them, the joyful shout of people running to meet them, the noise and running of children, and calming quiet words broken by burning kisses, commanding to destroy all sorrow from memory. Happy is the family person who has such a corner, but woe to the bachelor!

     Happy is the writer who, passing by boring, unpleasant characters striking with their sad reality, approaches characters that show the high dignity of a person who, from the great whirlpool of daily rotating images, has chosen only a few exceptions, who has never changed the lofty structure of their lyre, has not descended from their summit to poor, insignificant peers, and without touching the ground, completely immersed themselves in their images far removed and elevated from it. Double enviable is their beautiful fate: they are among them as in a native family; and yet their fame spreads far and wide. They have smoked human eyes with intoxicating smoke; they wonderfully flattered them by hiding the sad in life, showing them a beautiful person. All, applauding, follow them and rush after their triumphant chariot. They are called a great world poet, soaring high above all other geniuses of the world, like an eagle soars above other high fliers. At the very mention of their name, young passionate hearts tremble with excitement, tears of response shine in all eyes… No one equals them in strength – they are a god! But this is not the fate, and another destiny awaits the writer who dares to bring out all that is constantly before the eyes and what indifferent eyes do not see – all the terrible, shocking mire of trifles that entangle our life, all the depth of cold, fragmented, everyday characters that infest our earthly, sometimes bitter and boring road, and with the strong power of an unrelenting chisel dares to present them boldly and vividly for the nation to see! They will not gather popular applause, they will not see grateful tears and unanimous delight of souls moved by them; a sixteen-year-old girl with a spinning head and heroic enthusiasm will not fly to meet them; they will not lose themselves in the sweet charm of the sounds they themselves have torn out; they will not finally escape the modern court, the hypocritical, insensitive modern court, which will call the cherished creations worthless and low, assign them a despicable place among writers who insult humanity, attribute to them the qualities of the heroes they have depicted, take away both heart and soul, and the divine flame of talent. For the modern court will not recognize that the lenses that perceive the suns and convey the movements of unnoticed insects are equally wondrous; it will not recognize that great depth of soul is needed to illuminate a picture taken from a despised life and elevate it to a masterpiece; it will not recognize that high, ecstatic laughter deserves to stand alongside high lyrical movement and that there is an entire abyss between it and the buffoon’s farce! The modern court will not acknowledge this and will turn everything into reproach and disgrace against the unrecognized writer; without division, without response, without sympathy, like a familyless traveler, they will remain alone in the middle of the road. Their path is harsh, and they will bitterly feel their loneliness.
