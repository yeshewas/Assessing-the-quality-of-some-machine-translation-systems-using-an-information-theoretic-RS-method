Fortunately, since canal water passed near their house, she would fetch water from it, and when hunger struck her at times, she would boil ater and bean stew, eat, and keep watch over her husband's breath.

Ato Bogale remained speechless for seven days. She knew he was still alive by the warmth of his body and by listening to his breathing. On the eighth day, when his feet began to grow cold and his breathing became shallow, Emmet Wudnesh was confused and at a loss for what to do, pacing back and forth, when she suddenly heard a sound. But she could not tell whether the sound she heard came from inside the house or outside. Tilting first her right ear, then her left ear toward the door as if trying to see with them, she heard a faint, weak voice say, "My dear." That voice was the tormenting, longed-for voice of her husband.

"Is it you calling, my lord? I heard you speak, my dear? Is it a dream or is it real? Has God forgiven me, or is He playing a trick on me?" said Emmet Wudnesh, and in her joy she kissed the bed where her husband lay one moment, then kissed her husband the next.

"My dear, I crave tella, I crave thick tella!" said Ato Bogale.

"Before the tella, taste a little grain, my dear. It would be better if you ate some grain first, so the tella doesn't hit your heart empty," said Emmet Wudnesh, forgetting that it had been many days since any cooked food other than stew had been in the house.

"No, it is tella I crave, undiluted, thick tella," said Ato Bogale.

Then Emmet Wudnesh, stroking the air with her right hand and the ground with her left, went outside and stood at the doorstep, calling out to the neighbors one by one, "Biritu Shashie, Debritu Belayinesh, quickly bring me tella, tella that will be a cure for death!" But since all of them had locked their doors and fled, she found no one to answer her. Then, stroking the road with her left hand, she cried out alone, "Aya Bogale, on his eighth day he spoke and said he craves tella, and I have no one to give it to him. How can he die for want of a single cup of tella? How can he beg for a single cup of tella and die in the very place where he was born and raised, where his ancestors' umbilical cords are buried? How can I beg for a single cup of tella and lose him, I who watered him with a full jar?" crying out alone like a madwoman, she walked about half a chapter's distance, weeping, and returned empty-handed.

"I called until my throat was raw, I cried until my breath gave out, and I found no one to hear me, no one to ask what was wrong. I begged for a single cup of tella to be a cure for death and found none. Never mind, thank God you were brought to this point, thank God your mouth was opened to speak. A craving for tella will not kill you!" said Emmet Wudnesh, sitting down beside her husband and shedding those endless, undrying tears. Then she drew close and said, "Can you hear me, my dear? Did sleep take you? I could not find tella! Let go of your craving. Besides, you have been fasting and on an empty stomach, drinking tella would harm you, it would do you no good. Let it go." But for all she said, she received no answer. "Are you asleep? Did sleep take you? My dear, my heart!" she said, and gently reached out her hand to stroke her husband. But what lay on the bed in place of her husband was a mound of ice. Her hand recoiled and quickly drew back, but she herself remained frozen, turned to stone. After a little while, when she began to regain her senses, she somehow gathered her courage and reached out her hand again, and running it from top to bottom, the mound of ice on the bed had hands and feet, a nose and lips, eyes and a forehead, a head and ears. That mound of ice piled on the bed was, just a short while ago, before Emmet Wudnesh had gone out searching for tella, a living man full of warmth and life. That mound of ice had warmth enough to spare for Emmet Wudnesh. Now it had become ice, unable to give warmth or receive it!

Emmet Wudnesh sat in silence for a long time. To even weep, a person's senses must function to some degree, the heart must be well enough to feel grief!
