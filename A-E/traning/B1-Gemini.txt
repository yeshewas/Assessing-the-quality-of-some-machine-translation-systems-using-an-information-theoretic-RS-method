Ato Bogale remained silent for seven days, his tongue tied. His wife knew he was alive by the warmth of his body and by listening to his breathing. On the eighth day, his feet began to grow cold and his breathing became shallow. Emmet Wudnesh, confused and not knowing what to do, was pacing back and forth when she suddenly heard a sound. However, she couldn't distinguish whether the sound was from inside the house or outside, so she listened as if with her ears, turning her right ear one moment and her left ear the next towards the door, when she heard "My dear," that same weak voice. It was her husband's voice, tormented by agony.

"Was it you who called, my master? I heard you speak, my dear? Is it a dream or reality? Has God reconciled with me, or is he playing a trick on me?" Emmet Wudnesh said, and out of joy, she kissed her husband, who was lying on the bed, and then kissed him again.

"My dear, I crave tella, I crave thick tella!" said Ato Bogale.

"Before the tella, taste a little food, my father. It's better if you taste food first so the tella doesn't affect your heart," said Emmet Wudnesh, forgetting that it had been many days since any cooked food other than nifro had been in their house.

"No, it's tella I crave, pure tella without water!" said Ato Bogale.

Then, Emmet Wudnesh, groping the air with her hands and the ground with her feet, went out and stood by the door, calling out to her neighbors as if they had tella stored, "Birritu Shashe, Debritu Belaynesh, quickly bring me tella, tella that will be a cure for death!" She called her neighbors one by one, but since everyone had closed their houses and fled, she found no one to answer her. Then, groping the road with her feet, she said, "Aya Bogale, after eight days, he spoke today and said he craved tella, but I found no one to give it to him. How can he die for lack of one cup of tella? How can he, born and raised here, where the umbilical cord of his ancestors was buried, beg for one cup of tella and die? How can I, who used to serve him tella from a large jar, beg for one cup of tella and die?" she cried out alone, like a madwoman, and after walking about half a mile, she returned empty-handed, weeping.

"Even if the whole country gathered and I called until my throat was hoarse, until my breath ran out, I found no one to hear me, no one to ask me what was wrong. I begged for one cup of tella, a cure for death, and I found none. It doesn't matter, as long as God has brought you to this, as long as God has enabled you to open your mouth, the craving for tella won't kill you!" Emmet Wudnesh said, sitting beside her husband, and began to shed those endless, unceasing tears. Then she moved closer and said, "Do you hear me, my father? Have you fallen asleep? I couldn't find tella! Cut short your craving, and besides, if you drink tella on an empty stomach after fasting for days, it will harm you, it won't do you any good. Forget it." But she received no answer to all her babbling. "Are you asleep?" "Have you fallen asleep, my father, my heart!" she said, gently stroking her husband with her hand. But what was on the bed instead of her husband was a pile of ice. Her hand quickly recoiled as if struck, but she remained stiff and lifeless. After a short while, when she began to regain a little consciousness, she somehow mustered the courage and again stretched out her hand. When she stroked him from top to bottom, the ice piled on the bed had hands and feet, a nose and lips, eyes and a forehead, a head and ears, like a human being. That pile of ice on the bed had been a person with warmth and life a short while ago, before Emmet Wudnesh went to look for tella. That pile of ice, its warmth used to extend beyond itself to Emmet Wudnesh. Now, it had become ice, unable to give warmth, unable to receive warmth!

Emmet Wudnesh sat silently for a long time. To cry, one's human senses must function properly, and the heart must be sound enough to feel sorrow! But Emmet Wudnesh's senses had stopped working properly at that time, so she couldn't even cry. So she remained silent."""

After a long time alone, after her husband's death,

her being alone, at first like a memory,

then with full force, with its full weight, it came and pressed upon

her, and she began to cry, rising and falling. Then,

hitting the door and the floor, she went out and cried, "Oh, where is

humanity, where are the neighbors, I have been robbed for you - I have been killed for you,"

and though she fell and rose, no one said, "I will help you,"

no relative who would follow her was found. She

spent the day crying, falling and rising, and when she was tired,

she went in and sat by the corpse.

Emet Wudnesh spent the day crying, going in and out, and at night, she stopped

her crying and pondered why such an unknown suffering had befallen

her. One day, before her husband fell ill, he had said, "May God

not seem poor in suffering to you. He can bind and tie many evil things,

many sufferings, together and make you carry them, so it is better if you do not complain."

When she remembered this, fear seized her. "Indeed,

God is not poor in suffering! He might bind and make me carry

a heavier suffering than this! But what suffering is left? He has bound

all the suffering together and made me carry it. What suffering is left?

He made one of my children run away, blinded my eyes, killed my husband,

made people fear and shun me, made me lose my burial, and made me live

with the dead in my life. Can there be a heavier suffering than this? Even if there were,

could I, the weak one, or even a strong one, bear it? But let me not complain!

Indeed, the talk of suffering is heavier than its burden! If I had known

in advance that this suffering I am now carrying would befall me, its weight

would have killed me. But now, here I am, carrying it! May suffering not come,

but if it does, it gives added strength. Aaya Bogale, who seemed foolish,

was actually very wise and knowledgeable! It's just that I didn't listen,

but he knew all this in advance and told me. Oh, if he could come back

to life, I would accept all his advice as the word of God, like Saint

Gabriel, keeping my word and fearing!" Then she said,

"My Lord, have mercy on my soul, I have sinned, have mercy on me,

advise me, I will listen to you now, advise me, take me with you,

I will not displease you again, take me with you," and as she cried,

sometimes fearing that He might add more suffering to the suffering she was carrying,

saying, "You complained," she remained silent, and the night passed. The next day

also passed in the same way. On the second night, at the crowing of the rooster,

a man called from the courtyard. Emet Wudnesh heard the voice, got up,

and groped for the door, bumping into the pillar and the beam, and went out, saying,

"Who is it?"

"It's me, Priest Mihretu. How are you, Emet Wudnesh?"

"Thank God, how are you, Father?"

said Emet Wudnesh. Aba Mihretu was Ato Bogale's confessor.

"I stayed in another country for a long time without visiting you.

How is the patient?"

"The patient? The patient... has passed away," said

Emet Wudnesh, trying to find her way to Aba Mihretu.

"Passed away!? Oh, man! Oh, great man! Stay there,

Emet Wudnesh, for it is unknown who might follow you, so do not

come. When did he pass away?" said Aba Mihretu, as Emet Wudnesh

tried to go to him, and he moved backward.

"He passed away the day before yesterday, at noon."

"The day before yesterday, at noon! Oh, what a wonder! So...

what... what did he confess?"

"He did not confess."

"How did he die without confessing?"

"Was there no relative nearby for everything? Even I,

when he said he craved tella, I went to find some and when I returned,

he had died."

"Oh, a woman's way! You leave a weak man and go

to find tella? You did not do well."

"When he said he craved tella, I thought it was better,

I never thought he would die."

"Being a grown woman, don't you know that when a weak patient

craves tella, death is near?"

"How would I know, please! Let's leave all this now,

it's over. Instead, what about the burial?"

"As for the burial... what can we do now? When it gets light,

I will tell people, and the grave will be dug, and he will be buried

tomorrow night."

"And stay here tomorrow too?" said Emet Wudnesh, crying.

"If it's not at night, who would dare to enter a sick person's

house and take out a corpse? You cannot shroud and take out a corpse

by yourself!"

"How can I do it by myself?"

"This is the problem! What can be done now?

Come in from the cold now. When it gets light, the grave will be dug,

and tomorrow night, at this time... my servant Gebre will come

with a shroud for you. You can pay me for the shroud later,

it's fine." With that, Aba Mihretu returned to his house. Emet Wudnesh

also went in, crying.

When it got light, Aba Mihretu sent people to tell them about Ato Bogale's

passing, and after having the grave dug, it was difficult for him to send

Gebre to the sick person's house before his very eyes. He argued with his

own thoughts for a while and then said,

"Let him go, it's fine. If God has something for me, nothing will happen,

otherwise... there is nothing better than not being judged by people."

"""' they said, and in the midst of the commotion,

they had Gebre wrap the body and sent him to Emmet Wudnesh.

Then, Emmet Wudnesh and Gebre, with great difficulty, wrapped the old corpse.

He carried it from the head, and she from the feet.

After they had walked a short distance, Emmet Wudnesh became tired

and said, "Let's rest." As they sat and stood,

they traveled about half a chapter until they reached the people

who had gathered to receive and bury the body.

When the gathered people saw Emmet Wudnesh and Gebre,

they called out, "Put the body on him and return!"

It is said that if a disease kills once, it does not follow the dead, but the living.

So, the disease that killed Ato Bogale was to follow Emmet Wudnesh and her back.

Therefore, Emmet Wudnesh and Gebre, as instructed,

left the body and, taking the disease with them, returned to where they came from.

The gravediggers quickly picked up the body before dawn.
