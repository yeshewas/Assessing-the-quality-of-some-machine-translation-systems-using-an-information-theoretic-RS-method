The narrative continues with complex discussions about the nature of foolishness, wisdom, and the structure of society. Kasa presents philosophical arguments about how societies are built upon foundations that shift and change, much like stacked stones that eventually require reconstruction.

[The Story of Seble Wengel - Continued]

Seble Wengel, the beautiful young woman, had become the focus of much of the village's attention. Her marriage had been prevented by her father's insistence on proper procedures and conditions. Yet life moved forward, and eventually she came of age without a husband.

The text explores the tension between tradition and change, between what society expects and what individuals desire. Through the character of Seble Wengel, the narrative examines how a woman of great beauty and virtue can find herself trapped between the demands of her family's honor and her own life's possibilities.

[The Metaphor of the Heavy Stone]

Kasa uses the metaphor of stones stacked upon one another to explain how society functions. The lower stones bear the weight of all above them. In time, some stones crumble or shift, and the entire structure must be rebuilt. This metaphor applies to the social order: the poor and weak bear the weight of the rich and strong. When the foundations weaken, the entire structure is threatened.

Through this extended metaphor, the text explores how reform and change are sometimes necessary, not as destructive forces but as essential realignments that keep society from collapsing entirely.

[The Question of Divine Will vs. Human Agency]

A central question emerges: Are people bound by the structures society creates, or do they have the power to reshape those structures? The text suggests that while individuals are constrained by their circumstances, they retain the ability to recognize those constraints and to work toward change.

This is exemplified in the character of Bezabih, who despite his humble origins and the mockery of his peers, comes to understand that his status as an orphan does not diminish his worth. Through faith and understanding, he transcends the limitations that society had placed upon him.

[The Role of Wisdom and Understanding]

Throughout the narrative, wisdom is presented not as knowledge of rules and traditions, but as the ability to see beyond immediate circumstances to understand the underlying principles at work. Debtera Beyene, the monk and scholar, possesses this kind of wisdom. He helps Bezabih see that the insults directed at him—being called "a child of the Ark"—are actually honors, not shameful designations.

Similarly, Father Tamru, through patient counsel, helps Mother Wideness understand that remarriage is not a betrayal of her deceased husband, but a natural and appropriate response to grief and solitude.

[The Tension Between Individual and Community]

The narrative presents a fundamental tension: the individual's need for personal happiness and fulfillment versus the community's need for order and structure. Fitatawrari Asege's refusal to allow his daughter to marry according to the customs he disapproves of puts him in conflict with the expectations of his peers. Yet his intransigence also costs his daughter years of waiting and uncertainty.

The text suggests that this tension cannot be fully resolved, but must be continually negotiated. Too much emphasis on individual desire leads to chaos; too much emphasis on community conformity leads to injustice and suffering.

[The Power of Compassion and Forgiveness]

Near the end of this section, the narrative emphasizes the redemptive power of compassion and forgiveness. When Fitatawrari Asege finally accepts his daughter's marriage, it is not because he has been proven wrong, but because he comes to understand that forgiveness and compassion are more valuable than the maintenance of honor through rigid adherence to principle.

This theme is reinforced through multiple characters: Mother Wideness finding peace through remarriage and faith; Bezabih finding meaning through service to others; and even Kasa, the fool, offering insights that challenge and ultimately transform those around him.

[The Cycle of Life and Death]

The text frequently returns to meditations on the cycle of life and death. Just as plants bloom, bear fruit, and then wither, so too do human lives follow a pattern. The awareness of this cycle should not lead to despair but to a fuller appreciation of life while it lasts. This understanding is meant to encourage people to live fully and to focus on what truly matters—love, faith, service to others—rather than on petty grievances or rigid adherence to outdated customs.

[Conclusion of This Section]

This section of the text concludes with reflections on the nature of society, the role of the foolish person in challenging society's assumptions, and the possibility of transformation through understanding and compassion. The narrative suggests that wisdom lies not in the rigid maintenance of tradition, but in the flexibility to recognize when change is necessary and the compassion to implement that change with kindness and forgiveness.

The characters who succeed—Mother Wideness, Bezabih, Father Tamru, even Seble Wengel in her patient endurance—are those who combine faith in God's purpose with compassion for themselves and others. Those who fail or cause suffering are those who place ego, pride, or rigidity above human need and divine love.

"Never mind about that," he said. "Because when it comes to a fool, no one will tell them the truth to their face — they can only hear it indirectly. And even when told indirectly, you don't listen. That, then, is how things stand." What Gudu Kasa said was lost on everyone.

"Be that as it may! What kind of a curse is it, they say, that has bound my daughter? Is it a demon? Or......" said the Fitawrari, his eyes filling with tears. Woizero Truaynet began to weep.

"It is a demon, all right! And not a subtle, hidden demon — it is a massive, flesh-wearing devil, that horned, bearded demon with all his followers who has bound my sister. This demon that has bound her with his followers — it is he who has caused her to spend in grief and sorrow the years of childhood that God gave her to pass in joy and happiness. He who made my sister — whom God created to excel above others — fall behind all the rest, to live veiled behind a curtain of shame." said Gudu Kasa, and as he spoke, his laughter gradually turned to bitterness and frustration.

When Gudu Kasa first said that Seble Wongel had been bound by a demon-curse that kept her from marrying, everyone present was frightened, thinking it was true. But afterward, except for her parents, the others thought — as was customary — that he was only trying to bring up some pointless nonsense, and that if the matter were true, it could not have been the talk of the whole country for so many years without them having heard of it. So each word that came out of his mouth, which alarmed the Fitawrari and his wife, did not alarm the others even half as much.

The Fitawrari and Woizero Truaynet alone, being parents, were afraid — for a parent, even if they would tell their child that something bad had happened to someone they raised, would fear to say that such a bad thing would happen to their own child — so they believed everything Gudu Kasa said was true and were seized with terror.

"Shall I show you where this massive, horned, bearded demon and his followers are?" said Gudu Kasa.

"What?" said the Fitawrari, truly believing that the demon who had bound their daughter had come from there, and leaning forward from where they sat supported by their bed, they raised their eyes wide and stared ahead, as if wanting to rise and strangle him and take revenge.

"There they are!" said Gudu Kasa, pointing his index finger at the Fitawrari. "There is the horned demon who bound Seble Wongel and kept her from marrying! And those demons who serve as apostles and preach his wicked doctrine — there they are!" he said, pointing at his aunt and Aba Mogese. The Fitawrari, without taking their eyes off Gudu Kasa, stiffened again and leaned back against their bed support. Everything happening at that moment seemed to them like a dream and a delusion, not reality.

"You — who became enemies of your daughter Seble Wongel's marriage, and together with your confessor set up a demonic curse and evil eye and kept her back — it is you." As he said this, they felt the crushing burden heavier than a curse that had been pressing down on them lift from their shoulders, and: "Offfff......" said the Fitawrari, like a bellows that had been full of air for a long time finally exhaling.

"Alright, be quiet, enough!" " We thought you had told us news we had not heard, but what we mourned and wept for was our own belief and our own view — not the one you asked about, the one you thought was possessed by a demon and overshadowed by an evil eye." said Woizero Tiruaynет, the daughter of their eldest sister.

" Aunt, you have forgotten one thing — you have forgotten that it is I, Gudu Kassa, who am the problem. I am not your fool, not your madman. Is there anyone who asks a madman to pass judgment, or who prevents a madman from passing judgment? Do not listen to what I say; as is your habit, when I speak, plug your ears. But speaking is the right of the fool, the right of the madman. So I will speak like the madmen — do not interrupt me. And you, or anyone else, if you speak while I am speaking, remember that one who speaks with a fool is called a fool, and one who speaks with a madman is called a madman." said Gudu Kassa, and continued his speech:

" As I said a moment ago, you, being enemies of father and mother, held Seble back, you kept her away — you are her own parents. Among the nobility, what young man has not stood at Seble Wengel's gate to beg entry, has not gone down on his knees? And Enkuana, who has seen her from afar, who has heard news of her and traveled many days to come here and struggled? But you, like dogs sniffing out bones and blood, held her back and kept her there. Come now, if a quiet man, not mad like me, were to ask you: what is it that makes your bones and blood different from others'? Does your blood not stop when you die? Does it not run out? Does it not dry up? Does your flesh not decay? Does it not rot? Does it not stink? Does it not become foul? Do your bones not become worm-eaten? Are they not broken — do they not become dust? Among you, a strong, capable hero who fights poverty and, by his sweat, his toil, his blood, wins nobility, creates paradise and makes it his own — such a person has no worth, he is called a bastard sprung from the earth like a weed. Such a capable man, such a hero is despised and loathed among you. If such a hero came and begged to marry your daughter, for you it would be counted as the greatest shame, the greatest death. The one you value among you, the one you think worthy to be given your daughter, is a lazy man who does not go out or come in, does not burn or freeze, who simply waits for the death of his parents, who is mortgaged to his parents' death, who inherits their wealth, and who, if he fails to get it, sits down weeping and blaming God. If such a lazy man, such a beggar for his father's and mother's death, such a man who lives off others' wealth is not found, you will not give your daughter to a man who strives with his own wealth — and so you held her back and kept her there. Even now, Seble Wengel's fortune can still improve — but only if you have repented and changed this vain thinking of yours, or if, failing that, you have simply mourned for her and grieved over her."

Having spoken with heat and emotion, Gudu Kassa fell silent. Some of those present, as always, gave his words no weight and simply laughed and stayed quiet. Others, being of noble descent, heard him with sadness, lips pressed together, because speaking of inherited greatness in such a way only confirmed the deficiency of his mind. The Fitawrari, for his part, though the insults and abuse heaped upon him by his eldest sister's daughter were more than enough to bring out his anger and resentment close at hand — as he had been sitting there stunned, believing that what was called a true demon had attached itself to his daughter — when he finally realized that the demon was himself, the joy of that discovery overcame him and he too began to laugh along with those who were laughing.

" Oh, how wonderful! The demon of Dima's Le'um! When he said a demon had attached itself to her, I truly believed it and was completely stunned. But really — you called me and Tiruyе fools, so what does that make you? You are the fool, the horned one, the demon — you, the one from Dima!" said the Fitawrari, laughing from his heart, and except for Woizero Tiruaynет, everyone began to laugh. Gudu Kassa saw that his words had been dismissed, and as they simply laughed and let his insults and rebukes slide past them with amusement, he was stung to his core — he would actually have been very happy if they had insulted him or hit him, but as always, what he said did not sting them or pain them; they simply laughed and pressed their lips together and fell silent. He too, as was his custom, left for the moment, angry.

Kassa Damte, a nobleman from Negamay, was born of Fitawrari Damte Biru and Woizero Tiruaynет's eldest sister, Woizero Aster Tessema — a heavyweight of noble lineage that could not be lifted with both hands. As was the custom for children of the nobility, he studied the ways of the palace and attended Had he grown up, he would at least not have lost his father's domain. But since his father died when he was still a child, and his envious relatives, rather than encouraging him to seize this opportunity and push him forward, were instead determined to pull him back whenever they found him ahead, he grew up far from the palace, at his mother's side. Going back and forth from home, he memorized the Psalms of David at the local school in his village, and afterward, as his mind turned toward learning, he moved from one school to another, studied chanting and poetry, and when he finished, spent many years learning to write. In particular, there were many who admired his newly acquired wisdom.

Not only was his knowledge of local learning deep, but being the son of a nobleman, had he sought a position in the ecclesiastical hierarchy, it was beyond doubt that he could have obtained the headship of one of the great churches, or at the very least a sub-deanship. However, because his views were very different from those of the church leaders and clergy, and because he deeply detested their work, he began to see the church through their eyes and gradually distanced himself from it. Later, he even preached that the institution in which he lived, the customs upon which it was founded, its habits, rules, norms, and laws, were not only worthless but harmful, and that this old institution, without being reformed, was being revered like the word of God.

"The institution of our society, the customs, norms, and laws upon which it was built, are not like the institution of a living social order but like a pile of lifeless stones, one stacked upon another, the upper pressing down on the lower and the lower bearing the weight of the upper. Because it is inevitable that in time the lower will break free, and when that happens, lest the entire structure collapse, it must be reformed anew into a living social order that shows people in a better form than stone." Preaching thus, there were those who despised these slaves of custom, and sometimes those who pitied them — people who likened them to a heap of stones, especially those who bore the weight of the upper portion while the upper pressed down upon them — those who wanted them to know and learn that they had the full right to free themselves from the harmful old institution and build a new, beneficial one. But those same people, for their part, pitied him, saying, as if he had never been born of greatness, never been educated: "His mind being unsound, it has become so corrupted and so disordered." When he heard them pitying him and pursing their lips, and since he had no means other than speaking to show their error and his correctness, their deficiency and his full soundness of mind, he too grieved.

Therefore, Kassa Damte, hating and condemning the old institution of the society, despising and detesting the members of the society who had become slaves to the old institution, his spirit rebelling against both spiritual and worldly work and law, keeping his thoughts to himself, having no friend with whom to share them, what he said not suiting others and what others said not suiting him — he was a man who, within the world that surrounded him, had created his own world and lived alone in the midst of people.

Like all people who live in such a manner, he often talked to himself and sometimes laughed to himself, and those who saw this gave him the name Gudu Kassa.

Gudu Kassa, for his part, called the society in which he lived a "wretched society" — one that, though it had eyes, could not distinguish the grain from the chaff; though it had a mind, could not discern what was beneficial from what was harmful; that followed whatever went before it like a flock of sheep; that was dragged along bearing whatever was loaded on it like a beast of burden.

But since "the multitude prevails," the society won out, and "Gudu" became the name of Kassa Damte. He himself, even though he was displeased when they called him Gudu Kassa, knowing it was his name, would turn around when called by it.

Gudu Kassa, whether among relatives or strangers, the only one who, when he spoke, listened with pleasure — whether satisfied or not, without pursing her lips or twisting her mouth — and who gave answers to his questions with the wisdom a young woman her age could offer, was Seble Wengel alone.
