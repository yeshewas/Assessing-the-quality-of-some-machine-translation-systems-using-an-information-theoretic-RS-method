## Translation: Love Unto Grave (Part 2)

When the student gathering heard this, he hesitated to ask, as if out of fear, and turned around to look at his friends. They too had interrupted their arguments and were listening. Because all of them were beginners who had not yet composed poetry ($ያልተቀኙ$), they feared to argue in front of Bezabeh. In the midst of this, a stone bell was struck repeatedly.

"What bell is that!?" asked Bezabeh.
"It is to assemble for the *Zerefa* (the poetic plundering/recital)," said one of the students.
"Who is it that plunders?"
"This week, it is Yeneta (the Master)."
"Are there other plunderers?"
"Yes, there are two, and those who make them recite are many. Let me see... I think they are seven, yes, they are seven."
"So, besides the master, it means there are nine teachers of Qene among them?" said Bezabeh.
"Yes, the seven have not yet reached the level to plunder; they only make others recite," he said and stood up. His friends had already gone ahead to the *Zerefa*.

"What if I come along?" said Bezabeh.
"Why not? Even the *Diacon* (deacon) and the *Debtera* (church scribe) of the parish spend their evenings here when this happens," said the student.
After this, they held onto each other and walked to the place where the rain of Qene pours.

When they arrived there, the courtyard beneath the upper floor was packed full of people. However, all those people were not moving, not breathing, standing silently as if a magic spell had been cast over them, staring up toward the balcony, looking like lifeless statues.

In the midst of this, the clapping of hands was heard from the balcony. At this moment, movement and whispering spread from one corner of the courtyard to the other. When they clapped again, everyone became silent once more.

"*A'emere* (He knew)!" said a voice from the balcony. It was Alaka Kinfu.
"«*A'emere*»," responded the receiving student from the ground.
"*Ya'amr, Ya'emr, Ya'emr, A'emari, A'emarit, A'emariyan, A'emariyat, A'emro*," recited Alaka Kinfu, and the student received it. After this, as an example, they pulled two verbs from each grammatical house and began the *Zerefa*.

Starting from *Gubaye Kana* and reaching *Emewedis*, as always, they grew warmed up and passionate; they stood up like warriors boasting, pacing back and forth, and began to bring down the storm of poetry. When they finished the *Mewedis*, they said again, "Receive!" and began anew from *Gubaye Kana*. However, because the receiving student was exhausted, he sat down. While waiting for another to replace him, Alaka Kinfu poured out the *Gubaye Kana* and the *Ze'amlakuye* without a receiver. By the time he reached *Sellassie*, a receiver arrived and took it all the way to *Mewedis*. Alaka Kinfu, with his sweat dripping, started *Gubaye Kana* again, saying "Receive!", but the other teachers went up to the balcony, begged him, took him to his bed, and laid him down to sleep.

If a mediator had not arrived, he would have plundered until he collapsed from exhaustion. One day, in this manner, he plundered twelve full houses from *Gubaye Kana* to *Mewedis*, collapsed from exhaustion, fell ill for many days, and then recovered. After that, at most, they never let him plunder more than three full houses. This was because people, out of fear for his health, prevented him, not because he found it difficult. Unless Alaka Kinfu's mind itself untangled from the warp and weft of Qene, it was unthinkable that his poetry would ever run dry. What was most surprising was that, unlike other teachers whose poetry grew lighter and thinner the more they plundered, his mystery grew deeper and his wording grew more beautiful, as if he had chosen and matched them through great labor.

Alaka Kinfu was not like those who composed by calculating, nor like other teachers who plundered by thinking and stressing; when Qene intoxicated him, he forgot his existence in this world. It seemed to him that he was conversing in another world with the righteous, the martyrs, the faithful, and the angels. At that time, the storm of poetry he brought down without knowing or feeling it left him named as a true disciple of Tawanay. It gave him an immortal, unforgettable name in the house of Qene. Alaka Kinfu's rank in books, chants, *Zimare*, *Mewasit*, and *Aquaquam* was among the great masters; but in Qene, his rank was with Tawanay, the creator of Qene itself.

When Bezabeh heard the two full houses of Qene that Alaka Kinfu had plundered, he realized that he was still a mere beginner from square one. He who had thought he would be able to plunder within three months now admitted that he needed six months just to study poetry under Alaka Kinfu. Counting the time he spent at the Washera Qene school as wasted in vain, he returned to the hut with his hosts in sorrow. Even though the students who hosted him were all beginners, he admitted that they surpassed him in unraveling the plundered poetry of that day. He did not wish to discuss or argue over the interpretations or the history as was customary. Upon returning, he said, "I am tired," and lay down. However, he only pretended to sleep; he spent the evening listening to the others read and solve the plundered Qene one by one and narrate its history. Even after his hosts fell asleep, he stayed awake for a long time, looking for a way to correct what he had boastfully talked about, thinking he knew Qene and had mastered it.

### Tekle Alpha

On the next day, an attempt was made for Bezabeh to meet Alaka Kinfu, but because the master had allocated his entire time to those reciting Qene, those continuing books, and those practicing *Zimare* and *Mewasit*, and also because the previous night's *Zerefa* was his, it was inconvenient. An appointment was set for them to meet on the third day. At the appointed time, guided by one of the teachers, walking one in front and one behind, they climbed an old ladder whose era of construction was forgotten, leading to the balcony where Alaka Kinfu was. The abundance of shouting from the students studying was enough to wake someone sleeping outside the compound wall, let alone on the balcony. Having climbed the ladder and reached a small back door, the guide stayed behind, pushed Bezabeh inside, and turned back. The small back room was completely lined from edge to edge with goat skins spread out. In one corner, Alaka Kinfu sat wearing a thick white *Gabi*, holding his white flywhisk, with a large book spread open before him on a lectern (*Atrons*), gazing into it.

Bezabeh entered and stood. Alaka Kinfu, without seeing him, kept looking at his book, furrowing and loosening his brow, and occasionally laughing, which made Bezabeh want to laugh too. However, managing to suppress his laughter, he thought to himself, *What kind of wonder is this man?* When the master turned a parchment page and looked up, he saw him standing before him.

"What do you seek, brother?" he asked him.
"I am a guest who came for education. They told me you gave me an appointment to meet today, and that is why I have come now."
"Oh, look at me, I completely forgot it! I have kept you standing for long while conversing with this book?" said Alaka Kinfu.
"No, Yeneta; I have not stood for long."
"Wonderful, wonderful! From where did you come?"
"My home country is Mankusa, but now I came from Debre Work."
"Why did you go to Debre Work?"
"To continue studying books."
"Were you successful?"
"Yes, I completed the New Testament, the Old Testament, and the Book of Monks (*Metsahafe Menokosat*)."
"Excellent, excellent. And where did you study Qene?"
"At Washera."
"Under Ebui Nere?"
"Under Alaka Nere."
"Yes, we call him Ebui Nere (Nere the Proud)."
"Why?"
"Because he estimates his own worth as high and others as low, that is a name given to him since his time in the student school."
"Oh, today he does not possess such a character," said Bezabeh.
"That is because you saw him after hardship counseled him."
"What kind of hardship?"
"In the past, whenever students who had graduated from other Qene schools went to him, he troubled them by telling them to start over from *Gubaye Kana*. Therefore, all we teachers united, and whenever students who studied under him came to us, we made them start from *Gubaye Kana*. Because of this, students heading to him disappeared, and he was left alone. You see? The mud he kneaded to ruin the names of others became his own burden. But now, knowing it would not carry him far, he relented, and we forgave him."
"But then, in the midst of the competition you both waged to protect your pride, the ones who were harmed were the students," Bezabeh said, laughing.
"It is just the customary way, my son. When two powerful kings fight to protect their personal honor, who gets hurt? Is it not their followers?"
"Yes."
"This, too, is just like that."

Bezabeh smiled and fell silent. Alaka Kinfu remained quiet for a moment, then asked:
"What is it that you wish to learn?"
"I wish to become a master of Qene," said Bezabeh.
"Good, good!! In that case, on your part, a chair will be given to you to help teach books, and you shall diligently study your Qene. I believe it will not take you long to master the *Zerefa*," said Alaka Kinfu. Bezabeh bowed and went out.

After this, he settled down, teaching the book on his part and learning Qene on his own. In the evenings, when the students returned from begging for scraps of bread, they would contribute and give him at least half a leather pouch (*Kofeda*) of bread; this was without counting the boiled grains (*Nifro*) and roasted barley (*Kolo*). Furthermore, a straw mat woven for him by one student sold for one block of salt (*Amole Chew*), and a straw umbrella woven for him by a second student sold for one and a half blocks of salt. With these two and a half blocks of salt, he purchased flaxseed and began feeding himself flaxseed-stew with injera. The students washed his clothes for him and took turns washing his feet. Having a hut to himself, he began to live a high standard of life when measured by student standards. In his third month, he was able to plunder Qene. However, instead of going to Addis Ababa as he had initially planned upon mastering the *Zerefa*, he decided to add another year at Dima to strengthen his Qene. In the midst of this, Tekle Alpha arrived.

Tekle Alpha is the annual festival of Dima, celebrated on the 22nd day of the month of Tahsas. The number of people who come from near and far to see the celebration is beyond counting. On the morning of the eve of the festival, Alaka Kinfu sent for him and said:
"Tonight is the Eve, and tomorrow is *Ettane Mogor*; you are the one to compose the Qene, so think upon it."
"While there are teachers who have stayed here for a long time, if you give this honor to me who arrived just yesterday, they might be offended. It would be better if it were given to one of those who arrived earlier, Yeneta," said Bezabeh.
"Bless you, my son; you are full of humility. However, all those who are here arrived one after the other; they did not all arrive on the same day. Therefore, if I give it to one, the others will surely be offended," Alaka Kinfu said, laughing.
"There is a medicine that will not offend anyone," said Bezabeh.
"What is it?"
"If you compose the Qene yourself."
"You have found a good medicine indeed! An excellent medicine. Well then, if I compose it, you shall lead the chanting (*Merahi*)."
"Yes, Yeneta," said Bezabeh.

In the evening, when Alaka Kinfu plundered and recited the Eve Qene according to his custom, and Bezabeh led the chanting, everyone was amazed more by the beauty of Bezabeh's voice than by the poetry itself. That day, even the priests—who rarely praise one another—praised Bezabeh.

When the service concluded, as was custom, the priests who came from outside and the students from other regions living in Dima were guided to various houses for hospitality. Bezabeh and six other student-priests were guided to the house of a great nobleman named Fitawrari Meshesha.

The relatives called to Fitawrari Meshesha's house and the guests gathered by the festival packed the wide hall full. Food and drink were served with great generosity throughout the evening. After the feast, while drinking continued, the young men on one side began to sing folk songs, and the priests on their part began to chant the *Mahlete Genbo* verses. At first, other priests took turns leading the verses while Bezabeh stood with the responders, participating. Later, when he began to lead the chant, the noise inside the hall gradually grew quiet. The young men who were singing became silent, and everyone began to listen to the priests' chant.

"How God has perfectly fulfilled his gift, matching the melody to the poem and the poem to the melody, please look?" said Woizero Truaynet, the mistress of the house. When he finished one verse and the responders took it up, all the people filling the hall clapped completely.

> *He poured the Tella, sweet as wine,*
> *Sing it again, again, entwine!*
> *Mistress Truaynet, blessed be your hand,*
> *The Tella you made repeatedly across the land,*
> *Is thoroughly mixed with honey grand.*
> *May sickness never touch your hand, Truye,*
> *Who made this Tella and Tej today,*
> *All of you who drank this Tella and Tej,*
> *Say Truaynet, Truaynet, a wondrous washing of grace!*

As he brought down this chant, Woizero Truaynet sat to the left of her husband, her joy written and readable upon her beautiful face. Fitawrari Meshesha also saw his wife’s state, called the master of ceremonies (*Agafari*), and told him: "Tell the priests to rest and drink so they do not tire." At that moment, though Woizero Truaynet managed not to stammer lest people call her mad, she was on the verge of saying, "He is jealous because the boy praised only me." However, she managed somehow to cool her boiling blood for the moment.

While the priests softened their throats, dried by the abundance of chanting, with sweet *Tej*, the young men began to sing. Then, from among Fitawrari Meshesha’s servants, warriors and boasters arose one by one, listing deeds of bravery—both those their master had done and those he hadn't—and asserting their own loyalty unto the grave. After boasting, they sat down, receiving promises of rewards.

After this, the priests began to chant their *Mahlete Genbo* again. First, two students took turns leading, and then Bezabeh received it and chanted:

> *Searching through Mecha, Damot, and Nemay,*
> *At Dima was found a shelter (*Meshesha*) for the poor today.*
> *For the hungry, the thirsty, the one in stride,*
> *God gave you, Meshesha, for He is a kind guide.*

Before it could be known whether he would continue the poem or stop right there, Fitawrari Meshesha, unable to contain his joy, stood up himself, called his wardrobe keeper, and said, "Take, take! Reward this handsome student with a full suit of clothes and bring him to me." Immediately, Bezabeh returned wearing *Merdufa* trousers, a tree-patterned tunic, and a fine *Tbeb* toga (*Kuta*). When he kissed the knees of the master and the mistress and returned to his place, many of those present blessed him, saying, "You deserve it, it fits you! May He bring you to the cape, the cloak, and the double vestment." Only his friends hated him for it. After that, they could not even open their mouths for chanting or for drinking. Soon, as time had passed, the master and mistress arose and entered their inner private quarters (*Elfign*).

### Seble Wengel

During the week of the festival, Bezabeh remained a close favorite of Fitawrari Meshesha, never separating from him. When the festival concluded and he was taking his leave to return to the student school:
"It was a commotion, and we did not meet alone to speak. Where is your country?" Fitawrari asked him.
"My country is Mankusa, my lord," said Bezabeh.
"Do you plan to stay here long?"
"I plan to stay for one year, but the fulfiller is God."
"During the time you stay here, our house is your house; do not separate from us, do not feel estranged. We are enough not just for you, but for many," said Fitawrari Meshesha, stroking his long beard downward with his left hand. Bezabeh bowed, thanked him for the reward and the hospitality, and went to his student school. From that time on, he became a household member to Fitawrari Meshesha, Woizero Truaynet, and the entire family. If not for lunch, then for dinner; if not for dinner, then for lunch, he was never missing from Fitawrari Meshesha's house. Sometimes, if he spent a day and night without coming, they would worry as if for their own child and send an enquirer all the way to the student school.

Fitawrari Meshesha was an officer who took immense pride in his nobility. If he sat down and began reciting his lineage to the kings of Tigray, Shoa, and Gondar like the *Wudassie Maram* prayer, he never realized that the listener would get bored. According to his narrations, even if kingship eluded him in Tigray, Shoa, or Gondar, he considered the giving of the next highest position to another as the ultimate injustice. But what could be done? It is a thing found by victory, not by struggle! "I, Meshesha, I, the master of the brave—anyone knows that whether by lineage or by skill, there is none who surpasses me or equals me. But what of it! The time when a man is chosen for his lineage and his shield has passed, and behold, men like us are deployed here in the countryside with peasants and oxen!" when he spoke thus as if in anger, his flatterers would say to him, "Never mind, master, time passes; we will not remain like this," and he would immediately be comforted.

In counting lineage, his wife, Woizero Truaynet, was not lagging behind her husband at all. Fitawrari Meshesha and Woizero Truaynet had only one daughter, named Seble Wengel.

Seble Wengel was neither too tall nor too short, neither too thin nor too fat; she was crafted in perfect proportion, adorned by beauty and a radiant complexion working together, a most beautiful girl. Furthermore, because she was deeply blessed with character and humility, and hated natural arrogance, she was completely different from her parents.

By her fourteenth year, in the custom of noble children, Seble Wengel knew the Psalms (*Dawit*), *Wudassie Maram*, *Melke Maram*, *Melke Iyesus*, and *Melke Giorgis*. Leaving the pride and vanity to Woizero Truaynet, she thoroughly learned her mother's housekeeping and all other fine skills, making her a complete woman of whom it could not be said she lacked anything. Therefore, all the nobles in Dima and its surroundings who knew her or heard rumors of her, beginning from when she was only eleven years old, those who had sons courted her for their sons, and those who didn't have sons courted her for their relatives. However, her parents surrounded her like a thorny fence, defending her, and while all the young men drawn by her beauty circled the edges longingly, the season for eating that beautiful, ripe fruit of paradise passed by.

When any man whom Fitawrari Meshesha and his wife Woizero Truaynet did not consider an equal or match in lineage asked for their daughter in marriage, far from giving her, they considered the mere asking as a severe insult and grieved deeply.

"Oh, what a wonder! Does the ear ever refuse to hear, you people?" Fitawrari Meshesha would say when entering from outside.
"What did you hear today again, my lord?" his wife Woizero Truaynet would ask.
"Kanyazmach Someone begged for Seble Wengel for his son."
"Kanyazmach Someone is whose son?"
"Even his father doesn't know him, let alone me."
"Great insolence!" Woizero Truaynet would say.
"Gerazmach Someone told me, 'Give me your daughter,' Truye."
"Whose son is he, my lord?"
"His father is not a man of standing; he is a peasant."
"Would that I had died before hearing this!"
"Fitawrari Someone begged for my daughter for his son."
"Whose son is he?"
"His father was a merchant who handled pack-straps."
"Why did she have to be born, only to cause us to be insulted?"
"Balambaras Someone sent matchmakers to me saying 'Give me your daughter,' ha... ha... ha... Oh, what times!"
"Whose son is he?"
"His father was a vulgar countryman who had many cows. When he died, he left him all his cows. Now, while drinking his whey from a gourd and his belly swells, he thinks he has become equal to men of standing, and here he begins to beg for our daughter!"
"Let it be, oh let it be, it serves us right, it serves us right; as if we are tied down so we cannot go to where real people are, failing to leave this countryside, we have become the plaything of peasants," Woizero Truaynet would say, choked with tears.

"Oh, look at you, talking about 'where real people are.' In these days when the choice harvest of humanity is depleted and only the chaff remains, what real people are there? Wherever you go, do you think you will find a man who has a grandfather of standing, let alone a father?"
"How so?"
"How else? As you know, one sells his grain, another his cotton, another his butter, another his hides, and by giving bribes they are called Balambaras, Gerazmach, Kanyazmach, Fitawrari; and then his son is called the son of Balambaras Someone, Gerazmach Someone, Kanyazmach Someone, Fitawrari Someone. It's just a game of play-titles, as you well know! It is just like the children of Nesetegn and Areru playing in our compound, calling each other Fitawrari, Kanyazmach, Serazmach! A Fitawrariship and Kanyazmachship not inherited from bone and blood, but bought with cotton and hides. Ho, ho! Oh, what times have come upon us? Behold, it is the children of these people who ask to marry our daughter! Such children, long live hides, long live cotton, their fathers have been named Gerazmach, Kanyazmach, and some even Fitawrari like us; but they have no grandfathers. So, should I give my daughter to these? Should I give the child of Eleni, of Gelawdewos, of Susenyos to a peasant, to a merchant? Even if they cannot see or hear, would their bones not accuse me? Would they not reproach me?" Fitawrari Meshesha would say in anger, his blood boiling as he shook his head.

"Even if you say 'let me give her,' before the grave weighs upon me, my daughter will not marry a broken-boned man of no lineage! In fact, rather than marrying an unequal match and ruining the lineage, if it is said she remained unmarried because she lacked one who equaled or matched her, it will increase her honor, not decrease it."
"You speak the truth," Fitawrari would say.

While Fitawrari Meshesha and Woizero Truaynet conversed thus, saying "you are right, you are right" to one another, their daughter Seble Wengel reached twenty-four years of age without marrying. Until she turned sixteen, the suitors had denied her father and mother rest. After that, however, she was gradually forgotten. Even if a few suitors came far and few between, she was counted among those left behind on the shelf, as sometimes happens; suitors came to ask for her out of convenience as a divorcee/widow (*Fet*) rather than through the proper custom of a maiden (*Lijagered*).

Because her time passed without marrying a husband, the gossip and slander of the village people made her feel ashamed to lift her neck and look at people wherever she went. One day, while standing with her mother in church during Mass:

"Please, please look at the master's daughter for me; she has become just like her mother," said Debritu, one of the village slanderers.
"Why do you say she has become like her mother? Say she has surpassed her mother! Don't you see her hips and her chest? She looks like one who has given birth to ten children," said her friend Sinedu.
"What are these parents of hers planning to do with her, keeping her locked up and fattening her? Do they think they will slaughter and eat her? Why don't they give her away to someone?"
"Now to whom can they give her when suitors are missing? Having spent their time chasing away everyone who asked, saying 'you do not equal us, you do not match us,' now they lack suitors."
"Oh, they say Fitawrari Assege asked for her," Debritu said, laughing a mocking laugh.
"Which Assege?"
"The Fitawrari Assege of Bichena."
"What children does he have?"
"He asked for himself, what children does he have?"
"For himself! Oh, my Father of Dima! Would she not be his granddaughter?"
"In fact, let it surprise you! It is said they might part ways because he refused to marry her except under the title of a maiden."
"Oh, if they part, what does it matter? Rather than becoming the collector of an old man whom death forgot while she is a young piece of fruit, what does she lack sitting in her father's house!?"

"They say the life she lives there is not to be envied; it is torture. However, even if it looks like a grave, the torture she lives in is better than carrying a dying old man."
"Oh please, she is a master's child; what torture could she have?"
"They say the torture cannot be described. That girl Habtish-Yimer is my child's friend; every time she comes to our house and tells us, we are amazed! When I hear her story, I thank the Creator who created me poor."
"How so?"
"How else... look, except for female servants, if any men are seen near the house where she lives, woe to them and woe to her. As you see her now, she is not allowed to go out and see the face of man unless it is to church or to her relatives' houses; even for that, she is always surrounded by guards like a prisoner. Even when she walks inside the compound fence after leaving her house, if her father or mother sees her, they humiliate her even in front of people, saying 'Who called you? Whom are you waiting for?' So, while she sees her age-mates living as they please, she does not eat, she does not drink, it is always just weeping. Oh, how much can I tell you? If only you met Habtish-Yimer one day and she talked to you! Oh—oh—oh—oh, poverty is so much sweeter, sister!" said Debritu, as if aggrieved, shaking her head, blocking her mouth with her left hand so her talk wouldn't be heard from afar.

"Why do they cause their only daughter such trouble?" said Sinedu.
"To protect their honor, so it won't be said 'the daughter of so-and-so became like this.' Do you think they are sorrowful for her? If they felt sorrow for her, they would have given her to some young man before the flower of her beauty withered!"
"So it means she became the price-payer for their honor!"
"Of course!"
"It is amazing! It turns out that being born to nobility, when looked at from one side, is a bondage of honor! Do you know? There is one thing I fear," said Sinedu.
"What?"
"Lest what happened to Fitawrari Filate happens to these masters."
"Who is Fitawrari Filate?"
"Fitawrari Filate is a great nobleman of Debre Work. When I married my first husband and lived in Debre Work for two years, because our house was near their compound, we knew everything that was licked and tasted morning and night, let alone what the town heard. You know how a house with servants is? For servants, telling what their masters said and did before anyone else is a sign of favor and greatness! So, look, let us return to the main point. This Fitawrari Filate had a daughter who was beautiful and grown. Just like these masters, they locked that girl in the inner chamber, troubling her and making her weep, asking 'Whom did you see? Whom did you speak to?' In the end, she was found pregnant by their servant. They beat and imprisoned him, and sent her secretly to relatives they had in Mecha. But as the proverb says, 'It is easier not to commit a wicked deed than to hide it after committing it,' the matter was immediately revealed, and the people of Debre Work sang about it for a whole year."

"Oh... what a disgrace, what a disgrace! How ashamed her father and mother must be, please? I do not judge the girl; she wanted to eat bread with stew because she was hungry, and lacking it, she stole the roasted grain (*Kolo*) she found near her, ha-ha-ha-ha," laughed Debritu. Seble Wengel turned, looked at the two mocking women, and turned back.

Debritu and Sinedu were startled, remained quiet for a moment, and then Sinedu asked, "Do you think she heard us, please?"
"I don't know; judging by her look, I don't think she missed us," said Debritu.

In truth, even though Seble Wengel tried to listen to the Mass, her ears triumphed and listened to what Sinedu and Debritu were saying, so not a single word spoken by the two women escaped her. However, because a shameful thing—like anything else—ceases to cause shame after it is repeated and becomes familiar, every time she heard such mockery, instead of feeling ashamed and holding a grudge against the gossips as she did the first time, her grievance was toward her parents and her fate.

That day, upon returning from church, as breakfast was eaten in the women's quarters according to custom, even though Seble Wengel had no desire for food, she spent the time breaking the injera and bringing it to her mouth and returning it just to appear as if she were eating, lest her father and mother find an excuse to scold her. After the table was removed and her father went to the men's quarters to receive the people who came to visit them, she also went to her room, changed her clothes, sat on a stool outside her quarters warming herself in the sun, and began to look at the compound which the autumn (*Tseday*) had made beautiful. The season was autumn, just the beginning of the month of Tikimt.

In the wide field that Fitawrari Meshesha called his compound, the grass that they protected from cattle—growing mixed together from *Serdo*, *Akirma*, *Gudey*, and *Warat*—that which grew earlier had borne fruit, and that which grew later had blossomed with yellow, blue, white, and red flowers. Looking down from where Seble Wengel sat, that wide compound looked like a beautiful, multi-colored carpet stretched from edge to edge to adorn it. As the sun grew warmer, the paradise-like fragrance that the grass spread over that wide compound and the fruit trees planted around the houses blew together in the air; it was like a pool overflowing with perfume, making one never wish to leave it. The bees that moved from flower to flower chanting harmoniously in thick and thin tones, and the decorative butterflies that came only in autumn, dancing around the fruit trees at one time and over the flower carpet in the field at another, moving through their quarters—all seemed to hurry, understanding that this autumn made of greenery, fragrance, beauty, and grace, this autumn that did not let the cold of winter or the heat of summer be felt, would pass in a short time, saying, "Let us adorn ourselves before it passes, let us rejoice before it passes, let us work before it passes."

"Oh, you compound! Why can you not live always blossomed and beautiful like this?! Why could your beauty not be something that the summer sun does not dry and the winter mud does not soil! But this beauty of yours will pass now! This flower of yours will drop now! After a short time, these butterflies that dance drawn by your fragrance and grace, and the bees that chant, will not live here! They will go in search of another flowered and beautiful place," said Seble Wengel, after staying silent and observing the compound adorned with flowers and fruit stretched around her, and the creatures rejoicing in it. Immediately, the similarity between human life and the sprouts that change with time occurred to her: sprout, flower, fruit, then drying! Then being harvested! Then remaining as fruitless chaff and dust! Only that which has fruit living alive forever through its seed!

"Human life too, in its stages of age, is just like this! Infancy, youth, full adulthood, old age, then death! Only he who has a lineage living alive through his seed! But he who has no seed remaining like the grass and sprout that does not bear fruit, becoming dust or mud! Oh wonder! How similar man and plant are!" said Seble Wengel in her thoughts. Then, the similarity between the flowering time of the plant and the youth of human life occurred to her. "A plant in its season of greenery and flowers—these butterflies that dance around it and the bees that chant leave it and vanish when its greenery withers and its flowers drop; likewise, when man's time of youth passes, all who surrounded and admired him leave him and vanish!" she said, and especially remembered her world as it was from her fourteenth to her sixteenth year.

She remembered the abundance of young men who, in those days, whenever she went out escorted, would push and compete outside the guard fence just to see her once and be seen once. The joy and pride she felt upon returning then stood visibly compared with the sorrow and shame that met her upon returning today. Then, everything that Debritu and Sinedu had said in the morning at the church came back to her one by one:

* "What are these parents of hers planning to do with her, keeping her locked up and fattening her? Do they think they will slaughter and eat her?"
* "Now to whom can they give her when suitors are missing?"
* "They say Fitawrari Assege asked for her."
* "The Father of Dima! His granddaughter?"
* "Oh, in fact, they say he said 'I will not marry her unless she is a maiden.'"
* "The torture she lives in is better than carrying a dying old man whom death forgot like a grave."

The things they said to one another and their mocking laughter were heard loudly again in her mind. The mockers seemed to stand physically before her again, making fun of her.

Immediately, this mockery, this laughter, and this insult, which had accumulated over a long time without her realizing it, stifled and suppressed in her heart, ignited the hidden mockery, laughter, insult, shame, and disgrace, raising a raging fire within her and burning her! It scorched her. Her entire body felt as if a hellish sea was boiling within it; she was stifled, she was distressed. Finally, the heat that boiled inside her, like a fierce volcano shattering its barrier, erupted; while steam poured out of her mouth and nose, boiling tears flooded her eyes and began to pour down, ruining that face adorned with beauty and radiance. Her nose and lips, scorched by the steam, swelled; her eyes, flooded by boiling tears, grew inflamed; her cheeks were washed of their grace and radiance. To the extent that the hellish sea boiling within her shook and convulsed her, and to the extent that the steam rising from the heat choked and distressed her—like a man struggling to free his neck from a hanging rope—her mouth was wide open, her teeth bared, her eyes bulging, short of breath as she wrestled with agony, she looked like agony itself to anyone who might look upon her. Seble Wengel, whom they used to call the model of beauty, looked like something placed there to terrify and disgust. It is amazing! It turns out that radiance and grace are but shadows of emotion that follow emotion! Fortunately, the law of creation is built upon alternating. If light did not follow darkness, rest follow pain, silence follow turmoil, and joy follow sorrow, living would not have been possible. Because good and bad are alternating, and because often the lifespan of a bad thing is short, living is possible.

If Seble Wengel had remained longer than she did while burning in the raging fire that arose within her, and while cooking in the hellish sea created by her melting inner body, and while shaking and convulsing in the volcano that erupted within her, it might perhaps have been the end of her life. However, like the law of all creation, the fire that burned her cooled, the heat that choked and distressed her grew cold, and she too, little by little, began to become quiet.

After a few minutes, when she began to master her thoughts and know her body, the tears that flooded her eyes, the mucus that ran down her nose, and the saliva that scattered from her mouth had dried and tightened her skin; when she brought a mirror from the house and looked, she found her face looking like a dog that had overturned a container of dough, smeared with batter. Then, thinking of the calamity that would come upon her if her mother or father arrived and saw her in that state, she quickly brought water and, dipping her veil (*Netela*), washed and wiped her face thoroughly. Only the swelling of her eyes and lips could not be wiped away for the time being.

After that, she became still. As if the hellish sea that was boiling within her a short time ago, flooding and pouring through her eyes, had washed, wiped, and carried away her feelings of shame, disgrace, and heart pain, she found complete peace, complete silence, and complete rest for the time being. Because tears are the washing agent of anger, frustration, and sorrow, they calmed her anger, frustration, and turmoil for the time being; she found peace for the moment.

After she remained quiet for a short while, she thought she heard a sound, like the humming of many bees heard from afar, a low rumbling sound. Pulling her neck up and turning one ear toward the direction she heard the sound, she listened, but it disappeared. Saying "it is nothing," as she gathered her neck back and returned her ear from where she had turned it, it came again; just as she was about to catch it, it disappeared again. Thus, moving along with the shifting waves of the air, the sound came and went, came and went. She spent time turning her right ear at one time and her left ear at another to grasp and keep it, and when it eluded her, it finally stopped going away and continued coming closer. Seble Wengel was very pleased, as if she had chased and caught that mischievous sound with her ear from where it was fleeing. That face of hers which, a short time ago, had been dark and blackened like charcoal, cleared up like the morning sky. She caught it, she captured it; she hurried to know what it was. It drew closer little by little, growing louder, and finally she knew what it was: the *Andir* (flute) of Gebre!

Gebre had herded the cattle down below the cliff to the grass, and was returning while playing his *Andir*. Gebre came with his stick tucked under his left armpit, not looking whether what he stepped on was an obstacle, a ditch, a thorn, or a sharp twig, guided only by the *Andir*, following the *Andir*. As soon as he entered the fence, he went toward the kitchen, sat on a log that lay outside, and continued his play. To the extent that Seble Wengel heard Gebre’s *Andir* from afar, the bitterness of her mouth turned sweet, and her entire emotion began to change from sorrow to joy. Her face, which the flood of tears had ruined, began to be painted with beauty and radiance little by little; she was happy. Her heart, which a short time ago had been burned and wounded, began to soften. She began to forget the storm that had battered her a short time ago and, even if for a short time, began to live in the peaceful world that Gebre’s *Andir* had created for her.

The *Andir* of Gebre was different from all other *Andirs*; it seemed as if a power from the Creator was given to it to rule, command, and comfort the hearts of men! When Gebre’s breath came out blessing that three-jointed reed through its seven holes, it seemed given a power from the Creator to show the world as God had initially created it—free from agony, from hardship, from hunger, from thirst, from sickness, and from exhaustion—to show the world as a place of peace, of joy, of love, and of memory! Gebre’s *Andir* seemed given a power to give strength to the weak, courage to the fearful, comfort to the grieving, and medicine to the sick, freeing the hearts of men from malice, revenge, wickedness, and mischief, making man look like a companion and member of the angels' society! Would that the dominion Gebre’s *Andir* ruled over the hearts of men for a short time were for always! Would that the world Gebre’s *Andir* created for a short time were for always!

While Gebre continued playing the melody he brought when he arrived, and Seble Wengel listened from afar, swimming in a sea of joy, the servant-girl Habtish-Yimer came out of the kitchen, sat on a stone right in front of him, and began to speak. Her voice could be heard, but because the kitchen was somewhat far from where Seble Wengel was, she could not distinguish what she was saying. For a short time, Gebre, as if he did not hear what Habtish-Yimer was saying, continued his play without changing the melody. Later, he stopped and, slapping the log he sat on upon his left side, began to speak loudly too. After they exchanged words that sounded like a short argument, Habtish-Yimer arose from the stone and sat on the log on Gebre’s left side; Seble Wengel guessed that the argument had been about "Sit near me—I will not sit."

When Habtish-Yimer sat on his left side and leaned her head upon his shoulder like a child upon whom a heavy sleep had fallen, Gebre changed the melody of his play from a lamentation to a song, playing the new song that was popular in those days of their youth:

> *If the night, growing short, troubles us so,*
> *You are mine, I am yours, as we know,*
> *Let us be buried before we die, to live sleeping below,*

and while playing this, they both began to sway as they sat. Gebre put down his *Andir* and, as he rose to catch her, she ran, and he followed her. Then, running through the grass like a hunted gazelle, sometimes leaping, while he followed her, her laughter ringing out repeatedly like a church bell, she ran and he followed her. Just as he reached her and said "I have caught you," she eluded him. The tree from above and the grass from below shielded them.
