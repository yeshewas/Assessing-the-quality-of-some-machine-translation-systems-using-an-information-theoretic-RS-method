Seven

Dragon Boat Festival arrived. Her grandfather and Cui Cui had already agreed three days in advance that her grandfather would guard the boat, and Cui Cui and the yellow dog would go to Shunshun's stilt house to watch the excitement. Cui Cui initially refused, but later agreed. However, after a day, Cui Cui changed her mind again, thinking that if they were to watch, both should go, and if they were to guard the boat, both should guard it. Her grandfather understood the meaning, which was the result of Cui Cui's playfulness and love conflicting. Because of her grandfather's ties, she couldn't go to play even though she should, which was unacceptable! Her grandfather smiled and said, "Cui Cui, why are you doing this? You agreed and then changed your mind, which is not in line with the usual character of Chaodong people. We should mean what we say, no wavering. My memory isn't so bad that I'd immediately forget what you promised me!" Although her grandfather said that, it was very clear that he agreed with Cui Cui's plan. But she was too well-behaved, and her grandfather was a little sad. Seeing that her grandfather stopped talking, Cui Cui said, "If I go, who will keep you company?"

Her grandfather said, "If you go, the boat will keep me company."

Cui Cui frowned and smiled bitterly, "The boat will keep you company, hey, hey, the boat will keep you company. Grandpa, you really are..."

Her grandfather thought, "You will leave one day." But he dared not mention it. Her grandfather was speechless for a moment, so he walked to the small garden under the pagoda behind the house to look at the green onions, and Cui Cui followed.

"Grandpa, I've decided not to go. If the boat goes, I'll stay with you instead of the boat!"

"Good, Cui Cui, if you don't go, I'll go. I'll even wear a red flower and pretend to be Grandma Liu going to the city to see the world!"

Both laughed for a long time at this remark.

Her grandfather trimmed the green onions, while Cui Cui picked a large one and blew on it, making a "woo woo" sound. Someone on the east bank called for the ferry. Cui Cui didn't let her grandfather go first, so she hurried down, jumped onto the ferry, and pulled the transverse cable to pull the boat across the stream to pick up the person. While pulling the boat, she called out to her grandfather:

"Grandpa, sing, sing!"

Her grandfather didn't sing, but just stood on the high rock, looking at Cui Cui, waving his hand, not saying a word.

Her grandfather had something on his mind. He was preoccupied, Cui Cui was growing up.

Cui Cui was growing bigger day by day,She would blush when something was mentioned unintentionally. Time was maturing her, seemingly urging her on, making her responsible for something else. She loved watching new brides with powdered faces, loved talking about stories of new brides, loved putting wildflowers in her hair, and loved listening to people sing. She could already appreciate the lingering quality of the Cha Dong people's songs. Sometimes she seemed a little lonely, loving to sit on a rock and gaze at a cloud or a star in the sky. If her grandfather asked, "Cui Cui, what are you thinking about?" she would say with a touch of shyness, "I'm watching the wild ducks fight!" According to local custom, this meant "Cui Cui isn't thinking about anything." But in her heart, she would also ask herself, "Cui Cui, what are you really thinking about?" And in her heart, she would answer, "I'm thinking far and wide, many things. But I don't know what I'm thinking." She was indeed thinking, and indeed didn't even know herself what she was thinking. Her body was fully developed, and a "miracle" that naturally came with age, arriving monthly, also brought her more contemplation and more dreams.

Her grandfather understood the impact of such matters on a young woman, and his mood also changed somewhat. Her grandfather was a man who had lived in nature for seventy years, but when it came to natural phenomena in human affairs, there were some things he couldn't arrange. Cui Cui's growth reminded her grandfather of old memories, bringing back things from stories buried in a heap of time.

Cui Cui's mother, at one point, was just like Cui Cui. Long eyebrows, big eyes, rosy skin. She was also endearing and lovable—she also knew how to make the elders in the family happy with small gestures, a glance, a raised eyebrow. It seemed she would never be separated from this family member. But then misfortune struck, and she met that soldier. In the end, she abandoned the old and the young, and died with that soldier. According to the old boatman, no one was to blame for these things; only "heaven" should be held responsible. Cui Cui's grandfather didn't complain to heaven, but his heart couldn't fully agree with this unfortunate arrangement. The share that fell to him was truly unfair! He said he had let it go, but it was precisely something he couldn't let go of, a helpless tolerance!

At that time, there was still Cui Cui. Now, if Cui Cui were to be like her mother again, could the old boatman, at his age, raise another fledgling? People might wish it, but God might not agree! The man was too old, he should rest. All the toil and misfortune that a good countryman should endure, he had endured. If there was a God above, and this God had hands to control everything, it would be a very clear and fair solution to take the grandfather back first, and then let the young one receive her due, whether good or bad, in her new life. Only then would it be reasonable.

But her grandfather didn't think that way. He worried about Cui Cui. Sometimes he would lie on the rock outside the door, gazing at the stars and pondering his worries. He believed that death should be approaching soon, precisely because Cui Cui had grown up, proving that he himself was truly old. No matter what, Cui Cui had to have a place to go. Since Cui Cui was entrusted to him by her poor mother, now that Cui Cui was grown, he had to entrust Cui Cui to someone else, and only then would his task be complete! To whom? What kind of person would not wrong her?

A few days ago, when Tian Bao, the eldest son of the Shun Shun family, crossed the stream, he spoke with her grandfather. The straightforward young man's first words were:

"Old uncle, your Cui Cui is truly beautiful, like a Guanyin. In another two years, if I have free time to stay in Cha Dong and take care of things, instead of flying around like a crow, I will definitely come to this stream every night to sing for Cui Cui."

Her grandfather rewarded this confession with a smile. While pulling the boat, he looked at the eldest son with his small eyes.

Then the eldest son said:
"Cui Cui is too delicate. I worry that she is only suited to listening to the songs of the Cha Dong people, and cannot do all the serious things a Cha Dong woman does as a wife. I want a lover who can listen to me sing, but I also cannot do without a wife who can take care of the household. 'You want the horse not to eat grass, and you want the horse to run well,' alas, these two sayings were made by the ancients for me!"

Her grandfather slowly turned the boat around, letting the stern approach the bank, and said:

"Eldest son, such things do happen! You'll see." As for what exactly he meant, her grandfather didn't explain further. After the young man left, her grandfather reviewed those sincere words from a man's mouth, feeling both worried and happy.
