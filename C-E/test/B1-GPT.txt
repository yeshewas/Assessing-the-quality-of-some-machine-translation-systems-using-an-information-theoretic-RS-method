Section [4]: Chapter 3

Seven

The Dragon Boat Festival arrived. Three days earlier, grandfather and Cuicui had already agreed: grandfather would guard the boat; Cuicui and the yellow dog would go to Shunshun’s stilted house to watch the festivities. Cuicui at first did not agree but later consented. However, after a day, Cuicui changed her mind again, thinking that if two people went to watch, then two people should guard the boat.

Her grandfather understood this meant the conflict between Cuicui’s playful heart and her love. Because of her grandfather’s attachment, she could not fully play or fully guard—it wouldn’t do! Her grandfather smiled and said: “Cuicui, what’s this about? You agreed and now you’re going back on your word. That’s unlike the usual character of the tea village people. We should say what we mean and not be indecisive. My memory is not so bad that I forget immediately once you agree!”

Though he said this, it was clear her grandfather approved of Cuicui’s plans. But being too well-behaved made him a little stiff and unhappy.

Seeing that her grandfather was silent, Cuicui said, “I am leaving. Who will keep you company?”

Her grandfather said, “If you go, the boat will keep me company.”

Cuicui frowned and gave a bitter smile. “The boat keeps you company? Ha, ha, the boat keeps you company. Grandpa, you really are…”

Her grandfather thought, “You will leave one day.” But did not dare to say it. Having nothing to say, he walked over to the small garden behind the tower to look at the green onions, and Cuicui followed.

“Grandpa, I’ve decided not to go. If you go, I will let the boat accompany you!”

“All right, Cuicui, if you don’t go, I will. I will even wear a red flower and dress up as Old Liu going to the city to see the world!”

They both laughed for a long time at this.

Her grandfather tending onions, Cuicui plucked a big green onion and blew on it. Someone on the east bank called for the ferry. Cuicui wouldn’t let her grandfather go first, so she hurried down and jumped on the ferry, pulling the cross-stream cable to bring the boat across.

As she pulled the boat, she called to her grandfather:

“Grandpa, sing, sing!”

Her grandfather did not sing but stood on the high rock watching Cuicui, waving his hand, saying nothing.

Her grandfather was preoccupied with thoughts. Thoughts weighed heavily—Cuicui was growing up.

Cuicui grew day by day.She blushed whenever she accidentally mentioned something. Time was growing her, seeming to urge her on, making her take some responsibility for another matter. She happily watched the newlywed with a face full of powder, happily talked about stories of newlyweds, happily wore wildflowers on her head, and happily listened to people singing. The singing of the people in Tea Stream, she had already tasted its lingering emotions. Sometimes she seemed a bit lonely, liking to sit on the rocks, staring intently at a cloud and a star together in the sky. When her grandfather asked, "Cuicui, what are you thinking about?" she would shyly say softly, "Watching the water ducks fight!" According to local custom, this meant "Cuicui is not thinking about anything." But in her heart, she simultaneously asked herself: "Cuicui, what are you really thinking about?" And she also answered quietly, "I'm thinking far away, a lot. But I don’t know what exactly." She was indeed thinking, yet even she didn’t know what she was thinking about. This girl’s body had developed fully, and because of the naturally occurring ‘strange event’ at her age, when her period came, she had more thoughts and more dreams.

Her grandfather understood the effect this kind of thing had on a girl, and his mood changed as well. He was a man who had lived seventy years in nature, but natural phenomena in human affairs were beyond his control. Because Cuicui had grown up, her grandfather recalled some old stories and retrieved something buried deep in a pile of time.

Cuicui’s mother, at one time, looked just like Cuicui. Long eyebrows, big eyes, red skin. She was so obedient that people loved her—and she knew in small ways how to please the elders at home, arousing joy with a glance or a movement of her eyebrows. It seemed she would never be separated from her family. But misfortune struck: she met that soldier. In the end, she abandoned the old and the young and went to die with that soldier. According to the old boatman’s story, no one was at fault—only “heaven” should be blamed. Cuicui’s grandfather did not blame heaven in words but could not fully accept this unfortunate fate in his heart. The portion assigned to himself was truly unfair! Saying he let go was really just the helpless tolerance of something he could not let go of.

At that time, there was also a Cuicui. Now if Cuicui were to be like her mother, at the old boatman’s age, could he still raise a little chick? People wished, but the gods did not agree! People were too old and should rest. Any good countryside person had received all the hardship and misfortune they deserved. If there was a god at a higher place with a pair of hands controlling everything, the just and reasonable thing to do would be to take the grandfather back first and then let the young one accept whatever happiness or misfortune in a new life.

But her grandfather did not think that way. He worried about Cuicui. Sometimes he would lie on the rocks outside the door, facing the stars, thinking about his worries. He felt death should be near because Cuicui had grown up, proving he was truly old. No matter what, Cuicui must have a place to settle. Since Cuicui was entrusted to him by her poor mother, now that she was grown, he had to hand Cuicui over to someone for his own matter to be complete. To whom? What kind of person would not wrong her?

A few days ago, when the elder Tianbao from Shunshun’s family crossed the stream, he talked with her grandfather. This straightforward young man said first:

“Old uncle, your Cuicui is really beautiful, like a Guanyin statue. In two years, if I have the time to stay in Tea Stream to take care of things, instead of flying around like an old crow, I’ll surely come to this stream every night to sing for Cuicui.”

Her grandfather rewarded this confession with a smile. While pulling the boat, he kept his small eyes fixed on the elder.

Then the elder said again:

“Cuicui is too delicate. I worry she’s only fit to listen to Tea Stream people's songs, not to do all the proper things a Tea Stream girl does as a daughter-in-law. I want a lover who can listen to me sing, but even more, I can’t be without a wife to manage the household. ‘Wanting the horse to not eat grass, yet to run well’—ah, these two phrases were perfectly said by the ancients for me!”

Her grandfather leisurely turned the boat around, bringing the stern to the shore, and said:

“Elder, there are such things! Just you watch.” What exactly these things were, the grandfather did not clearly say. After the youth left, her grandfather reviewed the honest words from a man, feeling both worried and happy. If Cuicui must be entrusted to someone, was that person suitable to take care of her? If really handed over, would Cuicui agree?

On the fifth day early in the morning, light drizzle fell, the upstream water rose a bit called "dragon boat water," turning the river green as beans. Grandfather went to the city to buy festival goods, wearing a leaf cloak made from zongzi leaves, carrying a basket and a large gourd for wine, with a pouch hung on his shoulder containing six hundred qian in cash, and he left. Because it was a festival, many from small villages and hamlets brought copper coins and goods to the city, starting very early. After her grandfather left, the yellow dog accompanied Cuicui to guard the boat. Cuicui wore a brand-new cloak on her head, ferrying people back and forth across the stream. The yellow dog sat at the bow of the boat, always jumping ashore first to grab the rope when the boat came alongside, amusing every passenger. Some villagers also brought dogs to the city; as the saying goes, “A dog cannot leave the house.” Once away from home, even being near its owner, the dog would be very obedient. When ferrying, Cuicui’s dog always went over to sniff the other dog, got a glance from Cuicui’s side, as if understanding Cuicui’s meaning, and dared not make a move. After going ashore and finishing the rope pulling, it watched the strange dog go up the hill and usually followed. It either softly barked at the dog's owner or chased after the strange dog. Cuicui would scold a bit with a teasing shout: “Dog, dog, why are you so wild? You still have work to do; get back!” Then the yellow dog quickly ran back to the boat and kept sniffing all over. Cuicui said, “What’s this wild behavior! Who taught you! Go sit properly over there!” The dog seemed very smart and immediately went back to its place, only occasionally giving a light bark as if recalling something.

The rain fell incessantly, the surface of the stream smoky. When Cuicui had nothing to do on the boat, she would calculate the old boatman’s journey. She knew what places he would reach and whom he would meet, what conversations he’d have, what the scene by the city gate would be, and what the river street might look like. “In her heart was a volume,” which was as clear as if she had seen all this with her own eyes. She also knew her grandfather’s temper: whenever he met familiar grain merchants in the city, whether horsemen or firemen, he would inevitably say festive greetings. On one side, they’d say, “Manager, have a full and happy holiday!” The other would respond, “Boatman, have a full and happy holiday!” If said on one side, the other side might reply, “What can make you full? Four taels of meat, two bowls of wine, you won’t be full or drunk!” Then her grandfather would sincerely invite his acquaintance to come to Bixi Reef to drink enough. If someone wanted a sip from her grandfather’s gourd immediately, the old boatman was never stingy and would quickly pass the gourd over. After drinking, the soldier in the camp would roll his tongue and lick his lips, praising the wine, then be compelled to drink a second cup. When the wine ran low in this situation, her grandfather went back to the shop to refill it. Cuicui also knew her grandfather would go to the dock to talk with sailors who had just come ashore a day or two before, inquire about the price of rice and salt downstream, sometimes bending over to enter the cabins with smells of kelp, squid, oil, vinegar, and smoke. The sailors would take a handful of red dates from a small jar and give them to her grandfather. After a while, when her grandfather returned home and was reproached by Cuicui, these red dates would become the tokens of reconciliation. Once the grandfather arrived at the riverside market street, many merchants would send him zongzi and other things to show respect for this dutiful boatman. Although her grandfather grumbled, “I’m carrying such a big load; it will break my old bones,” no matter what, he always accepted these gifts. When he went to the butcher’s stall wanting to buy meat, the seller often refused to take money. If one butcher refused, he preferred going to another rather than accept favors. The butcher said, “Grandpa, why are you so strict? It’s not like you have to plow fields with a plowshare!” But no, he thought it was blood money, unlike other matters. If they refused to take money, he would pre-count it, then suddenly throw the money into the big long money tube, take the meat, and leave. The butcher understood this attitude, so when weighing the meat, he would choose the best parts and intentionally give more. When seeing this, the old boatman would say: “Hey, boss, I don’t want your favors! The leg meat is for city folks frying squid strips, don’t joke with me! I want meat from the shoulder, rich and sticky. I’m a boatman; I’ll stew it with radish and wine!” After getting the meat and handing over the money, he would count it once himself and then ask the butcher to count again. The butcher usually ignored him, throwing the money into the long bamboo tube, and he would walk away with a charming smile. The butcher and other meat buyers would laugh heartily upon seeing his demeanor...

Cuicui also knew that her grandfather would definitely go to Shunshun’s house on the river street.

Cuicui reviewed everything she had seen and heard during the two festivals, feeling very happy, as if there was something now—just like the fiddlehead flowers she saw with her eyes closed that morning in bed—that was very clear yet vague, right in front of her but impossible to grip.

Cuicui thought, “Does White Chicken Pass really have tigers?” She didn’t know why she suddenly thought of White Chicken Pass, a place in the middle of You River, more than two hundred miles from Tea Stream!

Then she thought again, “Thirty-two people row six oars, hoisting a large canopy when going upstream, a hundred white cloth sheets spread out, first crossing Dongting Lake on such a big boat, how ridiculous...” She didn’t understand how big Dongting Lake was and had never seen such a big boat. What was even more ridiculous was that she didn’t know why she suddenly thought of this question!

A group of ferry passengers arrived, carrying loads, some on official errands, and a mother and daughter. The mother wore newly washed and starched blue cloth clothes, and the girl’s face was painted with two circles of red, wearing new clothes that didn’t fit well, going to the city to visit relatives and watch the dragon boat. After the group boarded and settled, Cuicui looked at the little girl while pulling the boat across the stream. The girl seemed about thirteen or fourteen years old, but was delicate and proud, as if she had never been apart from her mother. She wore new pointed nails shoes, slightly stained with yellow mud. Her trousers were made of purplish green chive cloth. Seeing how Cuicui kept looking at her, the girl also looked back, her eyes bright like two crystal balls. Slightly shy and uneasy, with a hint of indescribable charm. The motherly woman asked Cuicui her age. Cuicui smiled but was reluctant to answer, instead asking the girl how old she was. Hearing that she was thirteen, Cuicui couldn’t help but laugh. The mother and daughter clearly were the wife and daughter of a wealthy family, as could be seen from their bearing. Cuicui noticed the girl was wearing a pair of braided silver bracelets, shining white and bright, and felt a little envy. After the boat stopped by the shore, people disembarked one by one. The woman took a copper coin from her pocket and slipped it into Cuicui’s hand before leaving. Cuicui actually forgot her grandfather’s rule, neither thanked nor returned the money, only staring dumbstruck at the girl in the group. As the group climbed over a small hill, Cuicui hurriedly caught up and returned the money to the woman on the hilltop. The woman said, “This is for you!” Cuicui said nothing, only smiled and shook her head, and before the woman could say more, she quickly ran back to her ferry.

Upon arriving at the ferry, there were more people calling from the other side. Cuicui pulled the boat back again. The second crossing had seven people, including two girls, who also changed into clean clothes especially to watch the dragon boat, but their appearance was not very pretty, making Cuicui unable to forget the earlier one.

Today’s ferry passengers were especially many, with more girls than usual. Cuicui, pulling the rope and ferrying, saw all kinds of interesting and strange people, well-behaved ones, those with red-rimmed eyes—all left an impression in her memory. When no one was on the ferry and her grandfather hadn’t come, she would repeatedly review the expressions of these girls. She would softly and casually sing:

“Tigers come out from White Chicken Pass biting people, not biting others, the troop leader’s miss sends the first. ... Big sister wears a pair of golden hairpins, second sister wears a pair of silver bracelets, only I, the third sister, have nothing to wear, wearing a sprout of peas on my ear year-round.”

Someone from the city visiting the countryside once saw the old ferry operator in front of a tavern on river street, handing the gourd mouth to a young sailor and inviting him to drink the newly bought white liquor. Cuicui asked about this, and the city person told her what they’d seen. Cuicui laughed at her grandfather’s generosity being out of time and place. The ferry passengers had left,Cuicui was softly humming on the boat a song that the wizard sang in December when people fulfill vows and welcome the gods——

　　Oh great immortal, oh mighty god, open your eyes and look at the people here!

　　They are honest, young, and free of illness.

　　Their adults can drink, work, and sleep;

　　Their children can grow up, endure hunger, and bear the cold;

　　Their oxen are willing to plow, their goats willing to give birth, chickens and ducks willing to incubate eggs;

　　Their women can raise sons, sing, and find the lovers they cherish!

　　Oh mighty god, oh great immortal, come forth and stand on both sides.

　　Master Guan rides the Red Hare horse,

　　General Yuchi holds a big iron whip!

　　Oh great immortal, oh mighty god, descend slowly through the clouds!

　　Zhang Guolao must sit steadily on his donkey,

　　Iron Crutch Li must be careful underfoot!

　　Blessings and prosperity are the grace of the gods,

　　Gentle wind and rain show the gods’ kind hearts,

　　Good wine and good food present on the front line,

　　Fat pigs and sheep are roasted over the fire!

　　Hong Xiuquan, Li Hongzhang,

　　When alive you were overlords,

　　Killing, arson, all loyal in your own ways,

　　Coming now to this feast is no trouble!

　　Eat slowly, drink slowly,

　　A moonlit and breezy night is good for crossing the river.

　　When drunk, hold hands and go home together,

　　I will sing for you again!

That song’s voice was extremely gentle, joyful yet tinged with melancholy. After finishing the song, Cuicui felt a trace of sadness in her heart. She thought of the firelight and drum sounds during the harvest festival vows in late autumn.

The distant drumbeats had already started, and she knew the dragon boat painted with vermilion long lines had now entered the river for this season. The fine rain still fell without stopping, and the creek surface was all mist.

Grandfather was heading home, probably close to the usual breakfast time, carrying everything on his shoulders and hands. Upon reaching the small hilltop, he called out to Cuicui, asking her to pull the boat across the creek to meet him. Cuicui saw so many people all entering the city that she felt helpless on the boat. Hearing her grandfather’s voice, her energy revived, and she answered sharply: "Grandpa, Grandpa, I’m coming!" The old boatman boarded the ferry from the pier, put the things he carried onto the bow of the boat, helped Cuicui pull the boat, and smiled at her like a child, full of humility and shyness. "Cuicui, you were worried, weren’t you?" Cuicui was supposed to scold her grandfather but instead answered, "Grandpa, I know you were having fun persuading people to drink on the river street." Cuicui knew her grandfather was very happy to go play on river street, but if she said that, it would make him even more embarrassed, so she kept the words back.

Cuicui carefully looked at the items placed on the bow but didn’t see the wine gourd. She sneered.

"Grandpa, you’re so generous, inviting the deputy master and those on the boat to drink, you even drank the whole gourd yourself!"

Grandfather laughed and hurriedly explained:

"No, no, my gourd was taken by Uncle Shunshun. When he saw me treating people to drinks on river street, he said: ‘Hey, hey, Zhang Heng the ferryman, this won’t do. You don’t run a distillery, how could you behave like this! Put your gourd down and let me drink it all.’ He really said those words, ‘Let me drink it all.’ So I put my gourd down. But I guessed he was just joking with me. Does he lack strong liquor at home? Cuicui, what do you think...?"

"Grandpa, do you think people really wanted to drink your wine, or just joked with you?"

"How is that?"

"Don’t worry, they must have kept your gourd because you invited guests in the wrong place, so they wouldn’t let you treat everyone to drink it all. It’ll be sent back to you soon. You don’t understand, really!——"

"Ah, it really might be like that!"

As he spoke, the boat reached the shore. Cuicui rushed to help grandfather carry things but ended up only taking the fish and that floral cloth bag; the money inside the bag had been used up, but there was a pack of white sugar and a pack of sesame cakes. Just as the two of them carried the newly bought things home, someone shouted from the creek to ferry over. Grandfather told Cuicui to watch the meat and vegetables so the wild cats wouldn’t drag them away, then hurried to the creek to work. Soon, he came back with the ferryman shouting. It turned out this person was the one who delivered the wine gourd. Grandfather said, "Cuicui, you guessed right. They really brought the wine gourd!"

Cuicui didn’t have time to go to the stove side. Grandfather and a broad-shouldered man with a young face entered the house.

Cuicui and the guest both smiled, encouraging grandfather to continue speaking. The guest also looked at Cuicui and smiled. Cuicui seemed to understand why she was being looked at and felt a bit shy, so she went to the stove to make fire. Someone shouted again for the ferry on the creek side, so Cuicui hurried out and pulled the boat to ferry the person across. Just then, yet another person crossed the creek. Although it was drizzling, there were especially many ferrymen, three times in a row. While working on the boat, Cuicui thought about her grandfather’s amusing behavior. Somehow, the one sent from the city to deliver the wine gourd seemed like someone familiar. But while the eyes looked familiar, she couldn’t recall where they had met before. Yet she didn’t want to think of that person in any particular way, so she couldn’t guess the visitor’s identity.

Grandfather shouted from the hillside: "Cuicui, Cuicui, come up and rest, keep the guest company!" Originally, when no one was crossing the creek, she intended to go ashore to light a fire, but after grandfather’s shout, she stayed on the boat.

The guest asked grandfather, “Are you going into the city to watch the boats?” The old ferryman said, “I should watch the ferry.” They talked about other things for a while. Later, the guest returned to the main topic:

“Uncle, your Cuicui looks like a grown-up now, and very pretty!”

The ferryman laughed. “Her tone is just like her brother’s, straightforward and pleasant.” Thinking about it, he said: “Sirs, the only ones here deserving praise are you. People say you’re good-looking! ‘The leopard of Bamianshan, the pheasant of Didi Creek,’ all lines specially praising your virtues!”

“But that’s unfair.”

“Not at all! I heard from the boatmen that last time when you were escorting the boat, there was an accident below Sanmen, at Baijiguan Beach. You rescued three people from the swift rapids. You stayed overnight on the sandbar, and the village women sang by your shed all night. Was it true?”

“It wasn’t women singing all night, but wolves howling. That place is famous for many wolves, only waiting for a chance to eat us! We built a big fire to scare them away, or else we’d have been eaten!”

The old ferryman laughed. “That’s even more wonderful! People’s words are right. Wolves only eat maidens, kids, and eighteen-year-old fine youths. They wouldn’t eat old bones like me!”

The two elders said: “Uncle, in the twenty thousand days you’ve been here, everyone says this place’s feng shui is good and produces great people. But for some reason, it stopped producing great people recently?”

“Are you saying good feng shui should produce people with famous names? I think such people aren’t born in our small place, and it doesn’t matter. We have smart, honest, brave, and hardworking young people. That’s enough. People like you, father and sons, have already brought lots of glory to the place!”

“Uncle, I think so too. The place doesn’t produce bad people but good people, like you. Though old, you’re still sturdy like a nanmu tree, living steadily on this land, respectable and generous—something rare.”

“I’m old bones now; what can I say? Sun, rain, walking long roads, carrying heavy loads, eating big meals, starving, enduring cold—I’ve taken my share. Soon I’ll lie in this cold earth to feed the worms. This world has all that your young folks deserve. Work hard, and don’t let the sun down; and don’t let the sun down either!”

“Uncle, seeing you so diligent, we young people dare not disappoint the sun!”

After a while, the two elders wanted to leave. The old ferryman stood at the door and called to Cuicui, asking her to come inside to boil water and cook rice while he watched the boat. Cuicui refused to go ashore. The guest was already off the boat. As Cuicui pulled the boat away, grandfather pretended to scold in a joking manner:

“Cuicui, if you don’t come up, do you want me to be a daughter-in-law cooking at home?”

Cuicui glanced sideways at the guest, saw the guest staring at her, then turned her face away, pursed her lips confidently, and slowly pulled the horizontal rope, drawing the boat to the opposite bank. The guest stood at the bow talking with Cuicui:

“Cuicui, after eating, want to go watch the rowing competition with your grandfather?”

Cuicui felt shy and didn’t want to speak, so she said: “Grandpa says no, there’d be no one to watch the boat if we go!”

“And you?”

“If grandpa doesn’t go, I won’t go either.”

“You’ll watch the boat too?”

“I’ll accompany my grandpa.”

“I’ll come alone to replace you for watching the ferry, how about that?”

With a bang, the bow crashed on the soil mound, and the boat landed. The two elders jumped ashore and stood on the slope, saying:

“Cuicui, you worked hard!… When I go back, I’ll send someone to replace you. Eat quickly and come to my house to watch the boats. Today it’ll be lively and crowded!”

Cuicui didn’t understand why the stranger was so kind or why she had to go to his house to watch the boats. She pursed her lips and smiled slightly, then pulled the boat back. When they reached home behind the creek bank, she saw that man still standing on the small hill opposite the creek, seemingly waiting for something, not leaving immediately. Cuicui went back inside to the stove, using some slightly damp grass to tend the fire, while asking her grandfather, who was testing the gourd of wine delivered with the guests:

“Grandpa, that man said he’d send someone to replace you, and wants us both to go watch boats. Are you going?”

“Would you like to go?”

“I’d be happy to go together. That person is very nice. I feel like I know him. Who is he?”

Grandfather thought: “That’s right, people think highly of you too!” He smiled and said:

“Cuicui, don’t you remember that person by the big river two years ago who said big fish would bite you?”

Cuicui understood but pretended not to and asked: “Who is he?”

“Think about it and guess.”

“There are so many people in the Hundred Family Surnames book, I can’t guess if he’s Zhang or Li.”

“He’s one of the elders from Shunshun’s boat family, he knows you but you don’t know him!” He took a sip of wine, as if praising both wine and people, and said softly: “Good, wonderful, this is rare.”

The ferry man was calling from outside under the door step, and grandfather was still saying “Good, wonderful...” before hurrying off the boat to work.

Section [5]: Chapter 4

Ten

During the meal, someone shouted for the ferry across the creek. Cuicui rushed down the boat and only then realized the ferryman was a sailor sent by Shunshun’s boat family as a substitute. Upon seeing Cuicui, he said: “The elders want you to go after the meal; he’s already on the river.” Seeing grandfather, he repeated: “The elders want you to go after the meal; he’s already on the river.”

Hearing the drumbeats, one could tell the rhythm was now tighter. From the drumbeats, one could imagine those extremely narrow boats, drawing beautiful long lines straight ahead in the long pool!

The newcomer didn’t even drink tea but stood firmly at the bow. While Cuicui and grandfather ate, they invited him to have a drink, but he shook his head and declined. Grandfather said:

“Cuicui, I’m not going. Would you like to go with the puppy?”

“No, I don’t want to go either!”

“If I go?”

“I didn’t want to go at first, but I’d like to accompany you.”

Grandfather smiled, “Cuicui, Cuicui, you’ll come with me, good, you’ll come with me!”

When grandfather and Cuicui reached the big river by the city, the riverside was already crowded. The drizzle had stopped, but the ground was still damp. Grandfather wanted Cuicui to go to the boat family’s stilted building on River Street to watch the boats, but Cuicui thought it better to stand by the river. They stood there for a short while before Shunshun sent someone to fetch them. The stilted building was already full of people. That morning when Cuicui had noticed the local gentry’s wives and daughters, they were entertained by the Shunshun family and occupied the best windows. Upon seeing Cuicui, the girl said, “Come, come!” Cuicui walked over shyly and sat on a bench behind them, and grandfather walked away.

Grandfather didn’t watch the dragon boat race but went with an acquaintance half a mile upstream to see a new rice mill’s water wheel. The old boatman was always very interested in water wheels. Nestled beside the mountain and water was a small thatch hut with a round stone wheel fixed on a horizontal axle, slanting inside a stone trough. When the sluice gate is lifted, the water rushes and spins the hidden wheel underneath, turning the stone wheel on top. The owner manages it, pouring raw grain into the trough, scooping the milled rice into a sieve placed in a corner, and then sifting out the bran. The ground was covered with bran; the owner wore a white cloth tied on the head, with bran on both head and shoulders. On good days, they grow radishes, greens, garlic, and scallions in the yard in front and behind the mill. If the water ditch breaks, they take off their trousers and go into the river to build stone piles to repair the drainage. If the water wheel dam is well-built, a small fish trap can be installed. When the water rises slightly, fish come onto the trap naturally—no effort needed!Managing a mill by the river is more varied and interesting than managing a ferry. The situation becomes clear at a glance. But for a ferryman to have a mill—that’s simply an impossible wish. Mills usually belong to the local small wealthy landowners. When the acquaintance brought the old ferryman to the mill, he told him who the mill owner was. The two of them inspected various parts while talking.

The acquaintance kicked the new millstone with his foot and said:

“The people of Zhongzhai sit atop the high mountain fortress, yet they like to come down to the big river to set up businesses; this belongs to Wang Tuanzong of Zhongzhai, a big deal costing 700 qian!”

The old ferryman turned his small eyes around, admiring everything with envy, estimating all, nodding his head, and politely commenting on each item inside the mill. Later, the two sat down on the unfinished white wooden bench. The acquaintance talked about the mill’s future, as if it were the dowry for Tuanzong’s daughter. This reminded the ferryman of Cuirui, recalling what the elder had asked him to do, so he asked:

“Uncle, how old is your Cuirui this year?”

“Just turned fourteen, almost fifteen.” After saying this, the old ferryman continued calculating the years in his mind.

“Over fourteen and quite capable! Whoever gets her will be truly lucky!”

“What luck? No mill as dowry, just a single person.”

“Don’t say a single person, a useful person’s two hands are worth five mills! The Luoyang Bridge was also built by Luban’s two hands!…” Speaking this and that, later the man laughed.

The old ferryman laughed too, thinking, “Cuirui has two hands and might go build the Luoyang Bridge someday, what a novelty!”
