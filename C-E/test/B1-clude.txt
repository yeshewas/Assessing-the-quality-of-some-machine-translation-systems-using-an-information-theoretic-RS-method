Chapter 【4】: Chapter 3

　　Seven

　　The Dragon Boat Festival arrived. Three days earlier, her grandfather and Cuicui had already made an agreement: her grandfather would mind the ferry, while Cuicui and the yellow dog would go to Shunshun's stilted building to watch the festivities. At first Cuicui refused, then she agreed. But after a day had passed, Cuicui changed her mind again, saying that if they went they should both go, and if they minded the ferry they should both mind it. Her grandfather understood what she meant — it was the result of a struggle between Cuicui's desire to play and her affection for him. Because of her grandfather's ties on her, she couldn't go and enjoy herself even when she should. That wouldn't do! Her grandfather said with a smile, "Cuicui, what's this about? You agreed and then went back on your word — that's not in keeping with the character of Chadong people. We should mean what we say and not be of two minds. My memory isn't so bad that I'd instantly forget what you promised me!" Though he said this, it was perfectly clear that her grandfather approved of Cuicui's plan. But the girl was being too well-behaved, and her grandfather felt a little uneasy. Seeing that her grandfather had stopped talking, Cuicui said, "If I go, who will keep you company?"

　　Her grandfather said, "If you go, the boat will keep me company."

　　Cuicui knitted her brows and gave a pained smile. "The boat will keep you company, ha, ha, the boat will keep you company. Grandpa, you really are..."

　　Her grandfather thought to himself, "You'll have to leave someday." But he dared not bring it up. Having nothing to say for the moment, he went around to the small garden behind the house by the pagoda to check on the scallions, and Cuicui followed.

　　"Grandpa, I've decided not to go. If anyone goes, let the boat go — I'll stay and keep you company in the boat's place!"

　　"Fine, Cuicui, if you won't go then I'll go. I'll put a red flower in my hair and go into town to see the world like Old Lady Liu!"

　　Both of them laughed at this for a long time.

　　Her grandfather tended the scallions while Cuicui pulled out a large one and blew on it, making a humming sound. Someone on the east bank called for the ferry. Cuicui wouldn't let her grandfather get there first, so she hurried down, jumped onto the ferry, grabbed the crossline and pulled the boat across the stream to pick up the passenger. As she pulled the boat she called out to her grandfather,

　　"Grandpa, sing! Sing!"

　　Her grandfather did not sing, but just stood on the high bank watching Cuicui, waving his hand without a word.

　　Her grandfather had something on his mind. Something weighing heavily on his mind — Cuicui had grown up.

　　Cuicui was growing up more and more with each passing day,When something was accidentally mentioned, she would blush. Time was growing her up, seemingly urging her to take on a bit of responsibility in another matter. She loved watching brides with powder-dusted faces, loved talking about stories of brides, loved tucking wildflowers into her hair, and loved listening to people sing. The lingering melodies of Chadong people's songs she had already come to appreciate. Sometimes she seemed a little lonely, and liked to sit on the rocks, gazing up at a cloud or a star in the sky. If her grandfather asked, "Cuicui, what are you thinking about?" she would answer with a touch of shyness, softly saying, "I'm watching the wild ducks fight!" By local custom, this meant "Cuicui isn't thinking about anything." But in her heart she would also ask herself, "Cuicui, what are you really thinking about?" And she would answer herself inwardly, "I'm thinking about things far away, so many things. But I don't know what I'm thinking about." She was indeed thinking, yet truly even she herself didn't know what she was thinking about. The girl's body had developed fully, and a certain "strange thing" that came naturally with age arrived each month, bringing her more thoughts and more dreams.

Grandfather understood the effect such things had on a young woman, and his mood had shifted somewhat too. Grandfather was a man who had lived seventy years in nature, but when it came to the natural phenomena of human affairs, there were things beyond his power to arrange. Because Cuicui had grown up, grandfather was reminded of old matters, and from stories buried under a great heap of time, he retrieved certain things anew.

Cuicui's mother, at one time, had been just like Cuicui. Long eyebrows, large eyes, rosy skin. So well-behaved she made people fond of her — she also knew how, in small ways, to glance and raise her eyebrows to make the elders of the family happy. It seemed as though she would never be parted from this one person in the family. But a small misfortune came, and she met that soldier. In the end she left the old and the young behind, and died alongside that soldier. As the old ferryman told it, no one was to blame — only "Heaven" should be held responsible. Cuicui's grandfather did not blame Heaven with his lips, yet in his heart he could not fully accept this unhappy arrangement. The share that had fallen to him personally was, to speak plainly, deeply unfair! To say he had let it go was precisely the kind of helpless resignation one endures when one cannot let go at all!

At that time there had still been little Cuicui. Now, if Cuicui were to follow in her mother's footsteps, would the old ferryman, at his age, still be able to raise another fledgling? A man may be willing, but fate is not! A man grows too old and should rest. All the toil and misfortune that any good country person ought to receive, he had received in full. If somewhere up above there were a God, a God with two hands to govern all things, then the obvious thing, the perfectly fair arrangement, would be to take grandfather back first, and then let that young one receive in a new life whatever happiness or unhappiness was her due — only that would make sense.

Yet grandfather did not think that way. He worried about Cuicui. Sometimes he would lie on the rocks outside the door, gazing at the stars and turning his thoughts over in his mind. He felt that death ought to be coming soon, precisely because Cuicui had grown up, proving that he himself had truly grown old. No matter what, he had to see Cuicui settled. Since Cuicui had been entrusted to him by her poor mother, now that Cuicui was grown, he too had to entrust Cuicui to someone — only then would his work be done! Entrust her to whom? What kind of person would be worthy of her?

A few days ago, when Shunshun's eldest son Tianbao crossed the river, he had a conversation with grandfather. This straightforward and outspoken young man said as his very first words,

"Uncle, your Cuicui has grown up so beautiful, like a Guanyin. In another two years, if I have the leisure to stay in Chadong and look after things, instead of flying around everywhere like a crow, I'll definitely come to this riverbank every night to sing for Cuicui."

Grandfather rewarded this confession with a smile. While pulling the boat along, he fixed his small eyes on the eldest son.

Then the eldest son said,

"Cuicui is too delicate. I worry she's only suited to listening to the songs of Chadong people, and can't do all the proper work a Chadong woman does as a daughter-in-law. I want a sweetheart who will listen to me sing, but I also can't do without a wife who looks after the household. 'You want the horse not to eat grass, yet you want the horse to run well,' — ah, those two lines were written by the ancients just for me!"

Grandfather slowly turned the boat around, let the stern rest against the bank, and said,

"Eldest son, there are such things! You just wait and see." Exactly what things, grandfather did not go on to explain clearly. After the young man left, grandfather turned over those honest words spoken by a man, and felt both worried and glad. If Cuicui was to be entrusted to someone, was this person suited to look after Cuicui? If she were truly entrusted to him, would Cuicui be willing?

Early on the morning of the fifth day, a light drizzle fell, and upstream the river had risen a little with the "dragon boat water," turning the whole river a bean-green color. Grandfather went into town to buy things for the festival, wearing a bamboo-leaf rain cape shaped like a "dou peng," carrying a basket, a large gourd filled with wine, a cloth satchel slung over his shoulder containing six hundred copper coins, and off he went. Because it was a festival day, a great many people from small villages and hamlets had come into town carrying copper coins and goods to buy and sell, and they had set out very early, so after grandfather left, the yellow dog stayed with Cuicui to watch the ferry. Cuicui wore a brand-new rain cape on her head and ferried passengers back and forth across the river. The yellow dog sat at the bow of the boat, and each time the boat reached the bank it would jump ashore first to pick up the mooring rope in its mouth, delighting every passenger. Some of the country folk crossing the river also brought their dogs into town, and as the saying goes, "a dog can't leave its home" — once away from their own house, even with their owners beside them, they became very well-behaved. When it came time to cross, Cuicui's dog would go over and sniff them, and after catching a glance from Cuicui, seeming to understand her meaning, it would not dare make any move. But once ashore, after finishing the job of pulling the rope, when it saw the unfamiliar dog heading up the hill, it would follow and give chase. Sometimes it would bark softly at the dog's owner, sometimes it would chase after the strange dog, until Cuicui called out with a touch of annoyance, "Dog, dog, what are you going wild about? There's still work to do, and you just run off!" Then the yellow dog would quickly run back onto the boat, and still go on sniffing all around the boat without stopping. Cuicui said, "What kind of reckless behavior is this! Who did you learn it from! Go sit properly over there!" The dog seemed to understand perfectly, and immediately returned to its original spot, only occasionally, as if remembering something, letting out a few soft barks.

The rain kept falling without stop, and a mist rose over the surface of the stream. When Cuicui had nothing to do on the boat, she would trace grandfather's journey in her mind. She knew where he should be by now, who he would run into, what they would talk about, what the city gate area should look like that day, what the riverside street should look like — she had "a book in her heart," and it was all as clear to her as if she were seeing it with her own eyes. She also knew grandfather's temperament — the moment he spotted familiar soldiers in town, whether horse grooms or stokers, he would always offer the festival greetings that were due. He would say on his side, "Deputy sir, eat and drink your fill this festival!" And the other would reply, "Ferryman, eat and drink your fill!" If he said something like that and the other replied, "What is there to eat and drink your fill on? Four ounces of meat, two bowls of wine — not enough to fill you up or get you drunk!" then grandfather would sincerely invite this acquaintance to come to Bixi Ju and drink to his heart's content. If someone wanted a sip of the wine in grandfather's gourd right then and there, the old ferryman was never stingy, and would quickly pass the gourd over. After drinking, the soldier would curl his tongue and lick his lips, praising the wine, and would then be pressed to take a second sip. When the wine ran low this way, grandfather would run back to the original shop and have it filled up again. Cuicui also knew that grandfather would go to the docks to chat with the upstream boat sailors who had just arrived one or two days ago, asking about the price of rice and salt downriver, and sometimes he would bend down and duck into the boat cabins that smelled of kelp and squid, along with other smells of oil, vinegar, and wood smoke, where the sailors would reach into a small jar and pull out a handful of red dates to hand to the old ferryman. Later, when grandfather came home and was scolded by Cuicui, these red dates would become the thing that made peace between them. Once grandfather reached the riverside street, many shopkeepers would certainly give him rice dumplings and other things as a token of respect for this dutiful ferryman. Grandfather would grumble, "I'm carrying such a big load, it'll break these old bones on the way back," but no matter what, he would always accept at least a little of their goodwill. When he went to the butcher's counter wanting to buy meat, the butcher would refuse to take his money, but if the butcher wouldn't take the money, he would rather go to another stall — he would never think of taking advantage. The butcher would say, "Old man, why are you so stubborn? It's not like we're asking you to plow the fields!" But it was no use — he considered this blood money, unlike other things, and if you wouldn't take the money he would count it out in advance, then suddenly fling the coins into the long money tube and grab the meat and walk off. The butcher knew his temperament, and when weighing his meat would always choose the best cut and deliberately add a little extra. When he noticed, he would say, "Hey hey, boss, I don't want your favors! The leg meat is what city people use to stir-fry squid and shredded pork — don't joke with me! I want the neck meat, I want something rich and tender, I'm a ferryman, I want something to stew with carrots and drink wine with!" Once he had the meat and handed over the money, he would count it himself first, then tell the butcher to count it again. The butcher, as usual, would pay no attention, and with one hand would toss the coins clattering into the mouth of the long bamboo tube. He would then walk away with what could only be described as a charming smile. The butcher and the other customers, seeing him like this, would laugh without stopping…

Cuicui also knew that grandfather would certainly go to Shunshun's place on the riverside street.

Cuicui went over in her mind everything she had seen and heard during the two festival days, and felt very happy inside, as if there were something before her now — like the elusive yellow mallow flowers she saw with her eyes closed in bed in the early morning — something that seemed bright and clear before her eyes, yet she couldn't see it clearly or grasp it.

Cuicui thought, "Does Baiji Pass really have tigers?" She didn't know why she had suddenly thought of Baiji Pass. Baiji Pass was a place name in the middle reaches of the You River, more than two hundred li from Chadong!

Then she thought again, "Thirty-two men rowing six oars, raising a great sail when going upstream with the wind, a thing made of a hundred bolts of white cloth spread out, first crossing Dongting Lake on such a big boat — how funny…" She didn't know how large Dongting Lake was, had never seen such a big boat, and what was even funnier was that she herself didn't know why she had thought of this at all!

A group of passengers came to cross, with carrying poles, people who looked like they were running official errands, and also a mother and daughter. The mother wore blue cloth clothes that had been freshly starched stiff, and the girl's face was daubed with two patches of red, wearing new clothes that didn't quite fit, going into town to visit relatives and watch the dragon boats for the festival. After everyone had settled on the boat, Cuicui pulled the boat across the stream while watching the little girl. From Cuicui's estimate the child was about thirteen or fourteen years old, but her manner was very delicate, as if she had never been away from her mother. On her feet she wore a pair of pointed new oiled nail shoes, smudged with some yellow mud. Her trousers were made of a purplish scallion-green cloth. Seeing that Cuicui kept looking at her, she looked back at Cuicui too, her eyes bright and clear like two crystal balls. A little shy, a little ill at ease, and at the same time with an indescribable charm. The woman who appeared to be her mother asked Cuicui how old she was. Cuicui smiled and didn't feel like answering, but instead asked the little girl how old she was. When the mother said thirteen, Cuicui couldn't help laughing. The mother and daughter were clearly from a wealthy family — you could tell just from their manner. Cuicui looked closely at the girl and noticed she was also wearing a pair of twisted silver bracelets on her wrists, gleaming with a bright white light, and felt a little envious. After the boat reached the bank and the passengers went ashore one by one, the woman reached into her clothes and pulled out a copper coin, pressed it into Cuicui's hand, and left. Cuicui at that moment forgot grandfather's rule entirely — she neither said thank you nor returned the money, but only stared after the girl in that group of people in a daze. Just as the group was about to go over the small hill, Cuicui suddenly hurried after them, and at the hilltop returned the money to the woman. The woman said, "This is for you!" Cuicui said nothing, only smiled and shook her head repeatedly, and without waiting for the woman to say a second word, ran quickly back to her ferry.

Back on the ferry, someone on the other side of the stream called out for a crossing, and Cuicui pulled the boat back. The second crossing had seven people, including two more girls who had also changed into clean clothes especially to watch the dragon boats, though they were not particularly good-looking, which made Cuicui all the more unable to forget the first one.

There were especially many people crossing today, with more girls than usual among them. Since Cuicui was on the boat pulling the cable and ferrying people, whenever she saw someone pretty, peculiar, well-behaved, or with reddened eyes, she left an impression of them in her memory. When there was no one to ferry, waiting for grandfather who still hadn't come, she would go over and over the manner of these girls in her mind. And she softly, carelessly sang,

"Baiji Pass has tigers that bite people, they don't bite others, the militia chief's daughter ranks first… Elder sister wears a gold hairpin, second sister wears a silver bracelet, only I the third sister have nothing to wear, ears wearing a bean sprout year-round."

Someone who had come down from the city to the countryside had seen the old ferryman at a wine shop on the riverside street, pushing the gourd's mouth toward a young sailor and inviting the sailor to drink the white spirits he had just bought. When Cuicui asked about it, the person from town told her what he had seen. Cuicui laughed at grandfather's generosity being ill-timed and out of place. The passenger left,Cuicui hummed softly on the boat, singing the shaman's song for fulfilling vows and welcoming the gods in the twelfth month—

　　You great immortal, you great god, open your eyes and look upon the people here!

　　They are honest, and young, and free from illness.

　　Their grown-ups can drink, can work, can sleep;

　　Their children can grow tall, can endure hunger, can endure cold;

　　Their bulls will plow the fields, their goats will bear young, their chickens and ducks will hatch their eggs;

　　You great god, you great immortal, come forward and stand on either side.

　　Lord Guan rides his Red Hare,

　　Yuchi Gong holds his great iron whip!

　　You great immortal, you great god, descend from the clouds and walk slowly down!

　　Fat pigs and fat sheep roasting over the fire!

　　In life you were both overlords,

　　Each with your own way of killing and burning, loyal and devoted to the end,

　　What harm in coming now to take a seat at the feast!

　　Eat slowly, drink slowly,

　　With moonlight bright and breeze cool, it's good to cross the river.

　　When drunk, let us take hands and go home together,

　　And I shall sing for you once more!

　　The song was extremely gentle in tone, joyful yet tinged with a faint melancholy. When the song was done, Cuicui felt a thread of sadness in her heart. She thought of the torchlight and the drums and horns at the autumn thanksgiving ceremonies.

　　In the distance the drumbeats had already started. She knew that the dragon boats painted with long crimson lines had by now been launched onto the river. The fine rain still fell without cease, and a mist rose over the surface of the stream. Nine

　　By the time grandfather returned home, it was nearly the usual hour for breakfast. His shoulders and hands were laden with things. As soon as he reached the top of the little hill he called out to Cuicui, asking her to pull the boat across the small stream to meet him. Cuicui had been watching helplessly on the boat as so many people passed into the city, growing more and more anxious. When she heard her grandfather's voice her spirits lifted at once, and she called back in a bright, clear voice: "Grandpa, grandpa, I'm coming!" After the old ferryman boarded the ferry from the dock, he set down everything from his shoulders and hands at the bow, and while helping Cuicui pull the boat he smiled at her like a child, his expression full of humility and shyness. "Cuicui, you must have been worried sick, weren't you?" Cuicui should have scolded her grandfather, but instead she said: "Grandpa, I knew you were on River Street talking people into drinking with you, having a grand time." Cuicui also knew that grandfather loved going to River Street to enjoy himself, but saying so would only make him flustered and embarrassed, so she held the words back just as they reached her lips.

　　Cuicui looked over the things set at the bow one by one, and noticed the wine gourd was missing. Cuicui let out a little laugh.

　　"Grandpa, how generous of you — treating the deputy and the boatmen to wine until even the gourd itself got swallowed up!"

　　Grandfather laughed and quickly explained:

　　"Not at all, not at all — my gourd was confiscated by Uncle Shunshun. He saw me treating people to wine on River Street and said: 'Hey, hey, Zhang Heng the ferryman, this won't do. You don't run a distillery, so how can you carry on like this! Put that thing down and let me drink it all.' He really said that — 'let me drink it all.' So I put the gourd down. But I reckon he was just teasing me. Does his household lack for liquor? Cuicui, what do you think…"

　　"Grandpa, do you think that just because someone really wants to drink your wine, they must be joking with you?"

　　"Then what is it?"

　　"Don't worry — they must have taken your gourd because you were treating people in the wrong place, and didn't want you to let everyone drink it all up there. They'll send it back to you shortly. You really don't understand, do you! ——"

　　As they spoke the boat had already reached the bank. Cuicui rushed to help grandfather carry the things, but in the end she only took the fish and the embroidered bag. The money in the bag was all spent, but there was a packet of white sugar and a packet of small sesame cakes. The two of them had just carried the newly bought things into the house when someone on the other side of the stream called for the ferry. Grandfather told Cuicui to watch over the meat and vegetables so the wild cats wouldn't drag them away, and hurried down to the stream to work. Before long, he came back to the house with the person who had crossed, both of them talking loudly. It turned out this person had come to return the wine gourd. All that could be heard was grandfather saying: "Cuicui, you guessed right. They really did send the wine gourd back!"

　　Before Cuicui could make her way to the stove, grandfather and a young, dark-faced, broad-shouldered figure had already stepped inside.

　　Both Cuicui and the guest were smiling, letting grandfather go on with his story. The guest looked over at Cuicui and smiled. Cuicui seemed to sense why she was being looked at, and feeling a little self-conscious, she turned away and walked to the stove to tend the fire. Someone called for the ferry from across the stream again, and Cuicui hurried out the door and onto the boat, ferrying the person across. Just then another person needed to cross. Though a light rain was falling, there were unusually many people crossing — three times in a row. On the boat, working as she went, Cuicui thought about how amusing grandfather was. She couldn't quite say why, but the person who had been sent from the city to return the wine gourd felt somehow familiar to her. His face seemed like someone she knew, yet she couldn't place where she had seen him before. But it was also as though she was unwilling to connect this person to any particular thought, which was why she couldn't figure out who he was.

　　Grandfather called from the rocky bank: "Cuicui, Cuicui, come up and rest a while, keep the guest company!" She had been meaning to go ashore to tend the fire when no one needed crossing, but the moment grandfather called out, she found herself unwilling to go up after all.

　　The guest asked grandfather whether he was "going into the city to watch the boats," and the old ferryman said he "ought to stay and mind the ferry." The two talked about a few other things. Then at last the guest came to the point:

　　"Uncle, your Cuicui looks all grown up now — she's very pretty!"

　　The ferryman smiled. "He speaks just like his brother — straightforward." Thinking this, he said aloud: "Second Young Master, the only one in these parts who deserves to be praised is you — everyone says you're the handsome one! 'The leopard of Bamianshanshan, the golden pheasant of Didixixi' — those sayings were made up just to sing your praises!"

You spent the night on the bank, and the women from the village spotted you and sang outside your shelter all night long — is that true?"

　　"It wasn't women singing all night — it was wolves howling. That place is famous for wolves, and they were just waiting for a chance to eat us! We built a big fire and scared them off, otherwise we'd have been eaten!"

As long as we have young people who are clever, upright, brave, and hardworking, that's enough. Your family — father and sons and brothers — have already brought a great deal of honor to this place!"

Sun and rain, long roads, heavy loads to carry, eating and drinking heartily, going hungry and cold — I've had my share of everything that was mine to have. Before long I'll be lying in this cold earth feeding the worms. Everything in this world that belongs to you young fellows is still ahead of you — work hard at it. The sun won't let you down, and don't you let the sun down either!"

　　After talking for a while, the second young master made to leave. The old ferryman went to the doorway and called to Cuicui, asking her to come inside to boil water and cook, so he could go down and mind the ferry himself. Cuicui refused to come ashore, but the guest had already stepped onto the boat. As Cuicui pulled the boat along, grandfather put on a deliberately aggrieved expression and said:

　　"Cuicui, if you won't come up, do you expect me to stay home and cook like a housewife?"

　　Cuicui cast a sidelong glance at the guest and saw him staring at her. She turned her face away, pressed her lips together with a certain quiet pride, and pulled the rope. The boat slowly crossed to the other bank. The guest stood at the bow and spoke to Cuicui:

　　"Cuicui, after you've eaten, come watch the boat races with your grandfather, won't you?"

　　"And you?"

　　"If grandpa doesn't go, I won't go either."

　　"I'm keeping grandpa company."

　　"Cuicui, thank you! … I'll go right now and send someone to take over for you both — hurry and eat, then come to our place to watch the boats. There'll be lots of people today, very lively!"

　　Cuicui didn't understand this stranger's kindness, nor why she should have to go to his home to watch the boats. She pressed her lips into a small smile and pulled the boat back. After returning to her side of the bank, she could see that the person was still standing on the little hill across the stream, as if waiting for something, in no hurry to leave. Cuicui went back inside, went to the stove to tend the fire, and while stuffing slightly damp grass into the firebox, she asked her grandfather, who was sampling the gourd of wine the guest had brought back:

　　"Grandpa, that man said he'd send someone right away to take over for you, and wants us both to go watch the boats — are you going?"

　　"Would you like to go?"

　　"I'd be happy to go if we both go. That man is very nice — I feel like I know him. Who is he?"

　　Grandfather thought to himself: "That's just right — he thinks well of you too!" Grandfather smiled and said:

　　"Cuicui, don't you remember, the year before last by the big river, there was someone who said he'd let a big fish bite you?"

　　Cuicui understood, but still pretended not to and asked: "Who is he?"

　　"Think about it, take a guess."

　　"There are so many people in the Hundred Family Names — I can't guess whether he's Zhang San or Li Si."

　　"Shunshun the boat master's second young master — he knows you even if you don't know him!" He took a small sip of wine, as if praising the wine and the person at once, and said softly: "Good, wonderful — this is something rare."

　　Someone outside at the foot of the bank was calling for the ferry. The old grandfather was still murmuring "good, wonderful…" as he hurried down to the boat to work.

　　While they were eating, someone across the stream called for the ferry. Cuicui rushed down to the boat, and when she reached the other side she found that the person crossing was a boatman sent by boat master Shunshun's household to take over the ferry. The moment he saw Cuicui he said: "Second Young Master wants you to come as soon as you've eaten — he's already gone down to the river." When he saw grandfather he said again: "Second Young Master wants you to come as soon as you've eaten — he's already gone down to the river."
