Seeing him fall silent, Cuicui asked, "If I go, who will keep you company?"

Her grandfather said, "If you go, the boat will keep me company."

Cuicui frowned and gave a bitter smile. "The boat keeps you company? Ha, the boat keeps you company. Grandpa, you really are..."

Her grandfather thought, "One day you'll leave." But he didn't dare mention it. At a loss for words, he went to the small garden behind the pagoda to check on the scallions, and Cuicui followed.

"Grandpa, I've decided not to go. If anyone goes, let the boat go. I'll keep you company instead!"

"Fine, Cuicui. If you don't go, I will. I'll put on a red flower and dress up like Old Liu going to town to see the world!"

They both laughed at this for a long time.

While her grandfather tended the scallions, Cuicui picked a large one and blew on it, making a buzzing sound. Someone called for the ferry from the east bank. Cuicui wouldn't let her grandfather go first; she rushed down, jumped onto the ferry, and pulled the boat across the stream to pick them up. As she pulled, she called to her grandfather:

"Grandpa, sing! Sing!"

Her grandfather didn't sing. He just stood on the high rock, watching Cuicui, waving his hand without a word.

He had something on his mind, heavy with thought. Cuicui was growing up.

She likes to watch brides covered in face powder, likes to talk about stories concerning brides, likes to wear wildflowers in her hair, and also likes to listen to people sing. If Grandpa asks, "Cuicui, what are you thinking?" she replies with a hint of shyness, softly, "Watching the water ducks fight!" In local custom, that means "Cuicui isn't thinking about anything." But in her heart, she simultaneously asks herself, "Cuicui, what are you really thinking?" And in her own mind, she answers, "I'm thinking far away, about many things. This girl's body has fully developed, and a natural "wonder" that comes with age arrives monthly, giving her more to ponder and more dreams.

Grandpa understood the impact such things could have on a woman, and his mood changed as well. A man who had lived seventy years in nature, yet when faced with the natural phenomena of human affairs, found himself at a loss. Because Cuicui was growing up, Grandpa recalled old memories, retrieving something from stories buried under a heap of time.

Cuicui's mother, at a certain time, was just like Cuicui. Long eyebrows, big eyes, red skin. After getting the meat, he would count the money himself, then ask the butcher to recount, but the butcher would ignore him and toss the coins into the bamboo tube with a clatter. Then Grandpa would walk off with a almost flirtatious smile. The butcher and other customers would laugh endlessly at this sight...

Cuicui also knew that Grandpa would definitely go to Shunshun's house on the river street.

Cuicui reviewed everything she had seen and heard on the two festival days, feeling very happy. It was as if there was something in front of her, like the elusive yellow sunflower she saw with her eyes closed in bed that morning—something clear before her, yet impossible to see clearly or grasp.

Then she thought, "Thirty-two people rowing six oars, hoisting a big sail when the wind blows upstream, a spread of a hundred pieces of white cloth crossing Dongting Lake in such a big boat—how ridiculous..." She didn't know how big Dongting Lake was, nor had she ever seen such a big boat. What was even more ridiculous was that she herself didn't know why she was thinking of this!

The miller managed this, pouring raw grain into the trough, then collecting the milled rice in a sieve in a corner to shake off the chaff. The floor was covered in chaff and ash. Fourteen

The old ferryman, tired from work, fell asleep, and Cuicui, exhausted from crying, also slept. Cuicui could not forget what her grandfather had said. To pick tiger-ear plants! During the day, while pulling the boat, she had often looked up at the plump tiger-ear plants on the cliff, grown quite familiar with them. The cliff wall was thirty to fifty feet high, usually out of reach, but tonight she could pick the largest leaves to use as an umbrella.

Everything felt like a story Grandfather had told. Cuicui lay vaguely on the straw mat under the coarse linen bed curtain, thinking the dream was wonderfully beautiful and sweet. Since Cuicui had cried herself to sleep and was resting soundly, he did not disturb her.

Fifteen

The eldest son took the new oil boat and headed downstream, leaving Nuosong, the second son, at home. Gradually, he moved from stories to singing, speaking most skillfully. Finally, he asked:

"Cuicui, a song in a dream could let you climb a high cliff to pick tiger-ear plants. If someone really sang for you on the cliff across the stream, what would you do?" Grandfather said this as a joke.

"And if I do understand their heart, then what?"

Grandfather slapped his own leg hard with his fist, smiling. "Cuicui, you're clever, but I'm clumsy and my words aren't gentle—don't be angry. I'm rambling, telling you a joke. Take it as a joke. Cuicui was startled and lowered her head. She wasn't sure how much of this joke was real, or who had made it up.

Cuicui smiled faintly and said, with a hint of pleading, "Grandfather, don't tell this joke anymore." She stood up.

"What if I'm telling the truth?"

"Grandfather, you really are..." Cuicui said, walking out.
