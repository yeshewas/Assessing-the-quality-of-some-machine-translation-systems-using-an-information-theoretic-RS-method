Section [1]: Preface to "Border Town"

I harbor an unspeakable gentle affection for farmers and soldiers, a feeling that can be seen everywhere in all my works. I have never concealed this emotion. I grew up in the kind of small rural town depicted in my writings. My grandfather, father, and brothers all served in the military: those who died did so in the line of duty, and those who survived will inevitably spend their entire lives in military service. Based on the side of the world I have encountered, I narrate their loves, hates, joys, and sorrows. Even if my pen is clumsy, it may not stray too far from the subject. Because they are upright and honest, some aspects of their lives are extremely great, while others are extremely ordinary; some aspects of their temperaments are extremely beautiful, while others are extremely trivial. When I set out to write about them, to make them more human and more relatable, I naturally wrote truthfully and plainly. But because of this, this work might inevitably become a kind of futile endeavor.

According to current trends, literary theorists, critics, and most readers are very likely to develop unpleasant feelings toward such works. The former claim to "not lag behind," telling people that China does not need this kind of work, while the latter are "too worried about lagging behind" and are unwilling to read such works at present. This is certainly true. What is "lagging behind"? A person with some backbone may never understand it, but who among the majority isn't afraid of "lagging behind"? I have something to say: "This book of mine is not written for that majority." Those who have read three or five foreign books on literary theory and criticism, or also a pile of classics and modern world masterpieces, often find that their life experiences do not allow them, beyond their "erudition," to know a little about another place and another kind of thing in China. Therefore, even if this work aligns with some literary theory and critics heap praise on it, such criticism still inevitably becomes an insult to the author. Since they don't want to understand the genuine loves, hates, joys, and sorrows of this nation, they cannot explain the merits and demerits of this work—this book is not written for them. As for literary enthusiasts, they might be college students or high school students, scattered in the more densely populated cities across the country, often honestly and naively spending a considerable amount of precious time reading newly published domestic literary books. They are controlled and dominated by a habit created through the concerted efforts of theorists, critics, clever publishers, and literary gossipers accustomed to lying and fabricating stories. Their lives are also far removed from the world mentioned in this work. They don't need this kind of work, and this book doesn't expect to gain them either. Theorists have literary theories from various countries' publications to reference, so they're never at a loss for words; critics have writers and works they owe a bit of petty gratitude or grievance to, enough for them to praise or condemn for a lifetime. Most readers, regardless of their tastes or beliefs, have works to read. Isn't it precisely because they care about the reading public that many people, supposedly for the sake of the reading public, are forever spinning like tops? Even if the publication of this book is not rejected by the leading majority of theorists and critics, and the led majority of readers do not completely abandon it, the author of this book has long since decided to abandon this "majority."

This book is only intended for those who "have already left school, or have never had the chance to approach school, can still read some Chinese characters, are engaged in duties beyond the reach of literary theory, literary criticism, and lying and fabricating news, live in that society, and are deeply concerned about all the good and bad of the entire nation in time and space." They truly know what the current countryside is like and want to know what the past countryside was like. They will surely be willing to learn from this book a little about the countryside and soldiers in a small corner of the world. Even if the world I write about is entirely unfamiliar to them, their tolerance and their enthusiasm for seeking comfort and knowledge from a book will surely enable them to read this book calmly. I do not stop there; I also intend to provide them with an opportunity for comparison. In another work, I will mention the two decades of civil war, how some peasants at the forefront, whose characters and souls were crushed by great force, lost their original patterns of simplicity, diligence, peace, and integrity, and what new thing they have become. How poor and lazy they have become under the poison of exorbitant taxes and opium! I will give a simple account of the worries of some small figures in the midst of change, and the concepts and desires of "surviving" and "how to survive" arising from malnutrition, as the nation is propelled by history toward an unknown fate. My readers should be rational, and this rationality is based on a concern for changes in contemporary Chinese society, an understanding of the nation's past greatness and current degeneration, and those who are working in solitude for the great cause of national rejuvenation. This work may only give them a touch of nostalgic sentiment, or only a bitter smile, or perhaps a nightmare. But at the same time, who knows, it might also give them some courage and confidence!

Section [2]: Chapter 1

1

Going from Sichuan to Hunan, there is an official road to the east. When this official road nears the border of western Hunan and reaches a small mountain town called "Chadong," there is a stream, with a small white pagoda by its side, and under the pagoda lives a solitary household. This household consists of only an old man, a young girl, and a yellow dog.

The stream flows down, winding around the mountains, and after about three li it merges into the great river of Chadong. If one crosses the stream and goes over the small hill, it's only one li to the edge of Chadong town. The stream is like a bowstring, the mountain path like a bow, so there's a slight difference in distance. The stream is about twenty zhang wide, with a streambed made of large rocks. Even if the still water is too deep for a pole to touch bottom, it remains clear and transparent, and the fish swimming in the river can be counted. Since the stream is a thoroughfare between Sichuan and Hunan, the water often rises and falls. Limited by finances and unable to build a bridge, a square-nosed ferry boat was arranged. This ferry can carry about twenty passengers with their horses across the river at a time, going back and forth when there are more people. A small bamboo pole is erected at the bow of the ferry, hanging with a movable iron ring. A waste cable is stretched across the water channels on both banks of the stream. When someone needs to cross, the iron ring is hooked onto the waste cable, and the person on the boat pulls on the cable with their hands, slowly pulling the boat to the opposite shore. When the boat is about to dock, the ferryman, while shouting "Slow down, slow down," leaps ashore, pulls the iron ring, and then people, goods, cattle, and horses all disembark, disappear over the small hill. The ferry landing is publicly owned, so those crossing do not need to pay. If someone feels uneasy and throws a handful of coins onto the boat deck, the ferryman will pick them up one by one and shove them back into that person's hand, with a serious look like he's arguing: "I have my ration, three pecks of rice, seven hundred coins, enough. Who wants this!"

But it doesn't work that way. Everyone seeks peace of mind. Who would feel comfortable accepting labor without pay? No matter what, some people still give money. The ferryman, unable to refuse, and for his own peace of mind, entrusts this money to someone to buy tea and tobacco in Chadong. He ties the high-quality tobacco produced in Chadong in bundles around his belt, generously giving it to anyone crossing who needs it. Sometimes, if he judges from someone's expression that the traveler is quite interested in the tobacco he carries, he ties a small bundle to that person's bundle, saying, "Don't you smoke this? It's good, it's fine, the flavor is excellent, and it's suitable as a gift!" As for tea, he puts it in a large vat in June, brews it with boiling water, and gives it to passersby to quench their thirst.

The one managing this ferry is the old man living under the pagoda. He is seventy years old. Since he was twenty, he has stayed by this stream. For fifty years, he has ferried countless people across. Though so old, and should be resting, heaven does not permit him to rest. It's as if he cannot part from this life. He never ponders the meaning of his occupation to himself, just quietly and faithfully living on there. Replacing heaven, making him feel the strength of life when the sun rises, and preventing him from thinking of dying along with the sun when it sets, is the girl by his side. His only friends are a ferry boat and a yellow dog, and his only relative is that girl.

The girl's mother, the old ferryman's only daughter, fifteen years ago secretly had an affair with a soldier from Chadong, behind the back of her honest father. After she became pregnant, this garrison soldier wanted to elope with her downstream. But judging from the act of elopement, one would violate the duty of a soldier, and the other would have to leave her lonely father. After some consideration, the soldier, seeing that she lacked the courage to go far and not wanting to ruin his own reputation as a soldier, thought: since they couldn't be together in life, no one could stop them from dying together. He took poison first. The woman, concerned about the child in her womb, couldn't bear it and couldn't make a decision. The matter had already come to the knowledge of her ferryman father, yet the father didn't say a single weighty word, just pretended not to have heard of it, continuing his days peacefully. The daughter, filled with shame and pity, stayed by her father's side. After the child in her womb was born, she went to the stream, drank a lot of cold water, and died. In a near miracle, this orphan grew up, and in the blink of an eye, she was thirteen. Because the mountains on both sides of their home were full of bamboo, their greenness overwhelming, the old ferryman casually picked a name close at hand for this poor little orphan, calling her "Cuicui."

Cuicui grew up in the wind and sun, her skin turned dark. What met her eyes were green mountains and blue waters, and her eyes were clear as crystal. Nature nurtured and also educated her, making her naive, lively, and everywhere like a little wild animal. She was also so gentle, like a yellow muntjac on the hill, never thinking of cruel things, never worrying, never getting angry. Usually, when on the ferry she met a stranger who took notice of her, she would stare at the stranger with bright eyes, looking as if she might at any moment run off into the deep mountains. But once she realized the person had no ill intent, she would leisurely play by the water again.

The old ferryman, whether in fair weather or rain, would stay at the boat's bow. When someone came to cross, he would slightly bend his waist, pulling the bamboo cable with both hands, and ferry the boat across the stream. Sometimes, when tired, he would lie on a large rock by the stream and fall asleep. If someone on the opposite bank waved and called out to cross, Cuicui wouldn't let her grandfather get up. She would jump into the boat, quickly ferry the passerby across for her grandfather, doing everything smoothly and skillfully, never causing any trouble. Sometimes, she would be on the boat with her grandfather and the yellow dog. When crossing, she would work together with her grandfather. As the boat neared the shore, and her grandfather was greeting the passengers, "Slow down, slow down," the yellow dog would grab the rope with its mouth, leap ashore first, and, as if understanding its duty, pull the boat tight to the shore.

On clear and mild days, when no one was crossing, the day long and leisurely, grandfather and Cuicui would sit on the large rock in front of the house, basking in the sun. Sometimes they would throw a piece of wood from high up into the water, urging the yellow dog beside them to leap from the high rock and retrieve the wood. Sometimes Cuicui and the yellow dog would both prick up their ears, listening to grandfather tell war stories from the town many years ago. Sometimes grandfather and Cuicui would each put a small bamboo vertical flute to their lips and play tunes for welcoming brides or seeing off daughters. When a ferry passenger came, the old ferryman would put down his bamboo flute, go alone to the boat, and ferry them across the stream. The one on the rock, seeing the boat move, would shout sharply:

"Grandpa, Grandpa, listen to me play, you sing!"

Grandpa, in the middle of the stream, would start singing joyfully. His hoarse voice and the sound of the bamboo flute vibrated in the still air, making the stream seem a bit livelier. (In truth, the echoing of the song made everything even quieter.)

Sometimes, what crossed were small cattle from eastern Sichuan to Chadong, flocks of sheep, or the bridal sedan of a newlywed. Cuicui would insist on being the ferryman, standing at the bow, lazily pulling the cable, letting the boat cross slowly. After the cattle, sheep, or sedan had disembarked, Cuicui would follow, stand on the small hill, watch these things disappear into the distance, then return to the boat and pull it near the shore by their home. She would also, alone, softly imitate the call of a lamb, the moo of a cow, or pick a handful of wildflowers to tie around her head, pretending to be a bride herself.

The mountain town of Chadong was only one li away from the ferry landing. When she needed to buy oil or salt, or when her grandfather wanted a drink during holidays, grandpa wouldn't go to town; the yellow dog would accompany Cuicui into town to get supplies. At the general store, there were large bundles of vermicelli, big vats of white sugar, firecrackers, and red candles, all leaving a deep impression on Cuicui. When she returned to her grandfather, she would talk about these things for a long time. There, by the river, were many upstream boats, with hundreds of boatmen busy loading and unloading various goods. These boats were much larger and more interesting than the ferry boat.Cuicui was not easily forgotten either.

The town of Chadong was built against the hills by the river. On the side near the mountains, the city wall crawled along like a long snake. On the side facing the water, space was left outside the city wall along the riverbank to set up docks, sheltering small awning boats. Boats going downstream carried tung oil, rock salt, and nutgalls for dyeing. Those going upstream transported cotton, cotton yarn, cloth, groceries, and seafood. Connecting the docks was a river street. Most houses had half their foundations on land and half over the water, because space was limited, so none of these houses lacked stilted buildings. When spring floods rose in the river and the water gradually entered the street, the families on the river street would each use long ladders, with one end on the eaves and the other on the city wall. Everyone, cursing and shouting, would carry their bundles, bedding, and rice urns up the ladders into the city. Only when the water receded would they come out of the city gate again. If the floodwaters were particularly fierce in a certain year, one or two of the stilted buildings along the river would inevitably be washed away, and everyone would stare blankly from atop the city wall. Those who suffered losses also stared blankly, seemingly speechless about their losses, much like when facing other irreparable misfortunes under the arrangement of nature. During the flood, from the city wall one could also see the suddenly widened river surface. The vast, surging waters carried houses, oxen, sheep, and large trees floating down from the upstream mountains. Then, in areas where the current was gentler, in front of the customs revenue vessel, people would often row small sampans. As soon as they saw an animal, a piece of wood, or an empty boat floating in the river's center, with the crying voice of a woman or child on board, they would urgently row their oars, meet the target downstream, tie it with a long rope, and then row back to the shore. These honest and brave people, like other locals, were both profit-loving and righteous. Whether rescuing people or objects, they acted swiftly and courageously in a kind of pleasurable adventure, earning admiration from onlookers.

That river was the historically renowned Youshui, with its new name being the Baihe River. After the Baihe River flowed downstream to Chenzhou and merged with the Yuanshui, it became slightly turbid, as if it were a mountain stream. If one traced it upstream, three-zhang or five-zhang deep pools were clear to the bottom. Illuminated by the sun, the small white pebbles and patterned agate stones at the bottom of the deep pools were clearly visible. The fish swimming back and forth in the water seemed to float in the air. On both banks were high mountains, covered with thin bamboo suitable for papermaking, which remained a deep, eye-catching green all year round. Homes near the water were mostly nestled among peach and apricot blossoms. In spring, one only needed to pay attention: wherever there were peach blossoms, there were people, and wherever there were people, one could buy wine. In summer, the dazzling purple cloth clothes and trousers drying in the sun could serve as markers of people's dwellings. When autumn and winter arrived, houses on the cliffs or by the water were all clearly visible. The yellow mud walls and black tiles were always perfectly positioned and harmonized extremely well with the surroundings, leaving a very pleasant first impression. A traveler with a slight interest in poetry or painting, curled up in a small boat on this river for a thirty-day journey, would surely not grow bored, because there were wonders everywhere; the boldness and delicacy of nature were captivating and enchanting in every aspect.

The source of the Baihe River came from the border of Sichuan. Small boats traveling upstream on the Baihe River could, during the spring floods, reach Xiushan in Sichuan territory. But within Hunan's borders, Chadong was the last water dock. The river surface at Chadong, though about half a li wide, narrowed to less than twenty zhang of flowing water during the autumn and winter dry season, with the rest being a stretch of blue stones. Since small boats could go no further upstream after reaching this point, all import and export goods from eastern Sichuan were loaded and unloaded here. Export goods were carried and lifted by porters using fir carrying poles on their shoulders, while import goods were also moved here in bundles and loads by human labor.

In this town, a single battalion of garrison soldiers, reorganized from former Green Standard Army屯丁, was stationed, along with about five hundred households. (Among these households, apart from some small capitalists who owned some mountain fields and oil mills, or engaged in lending out tung oil, rice, and cotton yarn, most were families with military registration who had been stationed here since the old days.) There was also a likin tax bureau here, whose office was in a small temple beneath the river street outside the city, often flying a long pennant. The director lived in the city. A battalion of soldiers was stationed in the old garrison commander's yamen. Except for the bugler who went up the city wall every day to play his bugle, reminding people that troops were still stationed here, the rest of the soldiers seemed not to exist. On winter days, walking into the city, one would see clothes and green vegetables drying in front of every household. Sweet potatoes, often with their vines still attached, hung under the eaves. Bags made of palm bark, filled with chestnuts, hazelnuts, and other hard-shelled fruits, also hung under the eaves. Chickens of all sizes played and clucked in the corners of houses. Occasionally, a man could be seen sawing wood on his doorstep or splitting wood with an axe, stacking the split firewood in the open yard like pagodas. Or one might see several middle-aged women, dressed in blue cloth clothes starched very stiff, with white cloth embroidered aprons hanging from their chests, bending over in the sunlight, talking while working. Everything was always so quiet; all the people spent every day in this simple solitude. A bit of silence increased one's power to ponder "human affairs" and increased dreams. For those living in this small town, each person must have spent their allotted days harboring inevitable expectations of love and hate in human affairs. But what were these people thinking? Who knew. Living in the higher parts of the city, standing at the door, one could overlook the scenery across the river and on the river itself. When boats arrived, countless trackers could be seen from afar on the beach across the river. Those trackers sometimes brought items like fine pastries and foreign sugar from downstream, which they would take into the city to exchange for money when they came ashore. When a boat came, children's imagination would focus on the trackers. As for adults, hatching a brood of chicks, raising two pigs, asking a downstream boatman to bring back a pair of gold earrings, two bolts of official blue cloth, or a jar of good soy sauce, or a double-thick Meifu lamp shade, occupied most of a housewife's mind.

Although this small town was so peaceful and quiet, because it was a commercial hub for eastern Sichuan, the small river street outside the city was a bit different. There were inns for merchants to stay in, and barbershops that sat firmly in place. Besides these, restaurants, grocery stores, oil firms, salt depots, and cotton cloth shops each had their place, decorating this river street. There were also shops selling檀木 pulleys, bamboo cables, and pots and pans for boats, as well as establishments specializing in introducing sailors and living off the docks. In front of small restaurants, on long tables, there were often fried golden carp with tofu, decorated with red chili shreds, lying in shallow bowls. Beside the bowls, large bundles of red chopsticks were stuck in big bamboo tubes. Anyone willing to spend a little money could sit down by the long table in front of the shop, pull out a pair of chopsticks, and a woman with plucked, delicate eyebrows and powdered face would come over and ask: "Big brother, officer, sweet wine or liquor?" A man with a bit more fire, being playful and perhaps interested in the landlady, would pretend to be angry and say: "Sweet wine? What, am I a child, asking if I want sweet wine?" Then, strong, dark liquor would be ladled from a large urn with a bamboo tube into an earthenware bowl, and it would promptly appear on the table before him. The grocery store sold Meifu oil and oil lamps, as well as incense, candles, and paper. The oil firm stored tung oil. The salt depot piled up green salt from the Fire Well. The cotton cloth shop sold white cotton yarn, coarse cloth, cotton, and black crepe silk for head wraps. The shop selling boat supplies had everything on display, lacking nothing, and sometimes even had iron anchors weighing over a hundred jin placed on the roadside outside, waiting for customers to inquire about the price. The establishments specializing in introducing sailors and living off the docks had their doors wide open all day in their homes on the river street. Boat owners in black serge mandarin jackets and rough-and-tumble sailors were always coming and going. The place was like a teahouse but didn't sell tea, and wasn't a smokehouse but allowed smoking. Although what people talked about here were the business matters of the boats, the going up and down of boats, the rowing and towing, mostly had fixed rules, so there was no need for numerical discussions. Most people came here for "socializing." Centered around the "leading dragon-head manager," they discussed local current events, the commercial situation in the two provinces, and "news from downstream." Meetings for inviting associations or collecting funds mostly took place here, and throwing dice to see the points and rotate the meeting head also often happened here. Two things frequently became their business talk: buying and selling boats, and buying and selling wives.

With the commercial development of big cities came certain parasites. Due to the needs of merchants and sailors, even this small border town's river street had a group of such people, gathered in some stilted buildings. These women were either brought from nearby villages or were women who followed the Sichuan army and drifted into Hunan. They wore fake foreign silk clothes and printed calico trousers, plucked their eyebrows into a thin line, and applied heavily fragrant, vulgar oil on large hair buns. During the day, when idle, they sat at the door making shoes, embroidering double phoenixes on the toes with red and green silk threads, or embroidering flower waist pouches for their sailor lovers, while watching passersby to pass the long hours. Or they leaned by the window overlooking the river, watching sailors stow goods and listening to them sing while climbing the masts. At night, they took turns receiving merchants and sailors, earnestly fulfilling a prostitute's duties.

Due to the simple and honest customs of the border area, even prostitutes were always so straightforward. When dealing with strangers, they had to collect payment first before closing the door to do business. Once they got to know someone, payment became optional. These prostitutes mostly relied on Sichuan merchants for a living, but their emotional attachments were mostly with the sailors. When feelings were strong, they would bite each other's lips and necks, swearing oaths and agreeing "not to fool around after parting." For forty or fifty days, the one floating on the boat and the one left on the shore would both numbly get through these days, tightly binding their hearts to a distant person. Especially the women, whose emotions were so genuine and infatuated beyond description. If the man didn't return by the agreed time, in her dreams, she would often dream of the boat docking, and the man swaying unsteadily from the gangplank onto the shore, running straight towards her. Or if she had suspicions during the day, she would dream of the man singing in the mast towards another direction, ignoring her. The weaker ones would then dream of drowning themselves in the river or swallowing opium; the stronger ones would grab a kitchen knife and rush straight at the sailor. Although their lives were so alienated from general society, tears and joy, intertwined with the gains and losses of love and hate, seeped into these people's lives, just like in other lands with other young lives, their whole bodies and minds soaked through with that love and hate, feeling hot and cold, forgetting everything. If there was any difference, it was merely that these people were more genuine, and also a bit more foolish. Short-term contracts, long-term marriages, temporary closures—these transactions concerning a woman's body, due to the simplicity of local customs, those involved did not find them vile or shameful, and onlookers never used the concepts of a scholar to criticize or despise them. These people valued righteousness and disdained profit, and could keep promises and self-discipline. Even as prostitutes, they were often more trustworthy than the moral and shame-conscious city folk.

The man in charge of the docks was named Shunshun, a figure who had muddled along in the military camps during the late Qing. During the revolution, he served as a squad leader in the famous 49th Standard of the Army. Other squad leaders had become great men and celebrities through the revolution, or had been beheaded and dismembered. He, however, brought back a case of beriberi, contracted from his youthful adventures, to his hometown. He used his meager savings to buy a six-oared white wooden boat, which he rented to a poor boat owner to carry goods between Chadong and Chenzhou. With good luck, the boat didn't encounter trouble for half a year, so from his earnings, he married a young widow with fair skin and black hair, who had a bit of property. A few years later, on this river, he owned four boats of various sizes, a shop, and two sons.

But this generous and unrestrained man, although his career was very smooth, could not make a huge fortune like the oil merchants, because he loved making friends, was generous, and was always ready to help those in need. Having muddled through life in the army himself, he understood the hardships of travelers and the feelings of the disappointed. Therefore, he would do his utmost to help any boat owner bankrupted by a shipwreck, passing retired soldiers, or traveling scholars who came to this place seeking assistance based on his reputation. The money he earned from the water was thus lavishly scattered. Although he had a slight limp and could still swim, and walked unsteadily, he was fair and selfless. Matters on the river were originally very simple, all governed by custom. If one boat bumped into another, or one boat harmed the interests of another person or boat, there were customary methods to resolve it. However, applying these customary rules to settle everything required a central figure of advanced age and high virtue. One autumn, when the original manager died, Shunshun became his replacement. He was only fifty then, but being sensible, reasonable, upright, peaceful, and not greedy for money, no one questioned his age.

To this day, his eldest son is eighteen and the youngest is sixteen. Both young men are as sturdy as young bulls, capable of sailing boats, swimming, and walking long distances. They did everything that young people from small towns could do, and did it all expertly. The elder resembled his father, being bold, open-minded, and not bound by formalities. The younger took after his fair-skinned, black-haired mother, being quiet but with an exceptionally handsome and refined appearance.At a glance, one could tell he was intelligent and full of emotion.

Since the two brothers had grown up and needed training in various aspects of life, their father took turns sending the two children on trips to different places. When traveling downstream by boat, they often served as crew on their own vessels, sharing hardships with others. They took the heaviest oars when rowing, pulled the lead ropes when towing, ate dried fish, chili, and sour pickled vegetables, and slept on the hard cabin planks. When traveling upstream by land, they followed Sichuan-bound merchants, doing business through Xiushan, Longtan, and Youyang, trudging in straw sandals from post to post regardless of weather, be it cold or hot, rain or snow. They carried short knives, and when forced to act, they would swiftly draw them, step into an open space, and face their opponent, settling matters with bare flesh. The guild’s ethos was, "To deal with enemies, you must use the knife; to bond with friends, you must also use the knife," so when a knife was needed, they never missed the chance. Learning trade, social graces, how to live in a new place, and how to use a knife to protect themselves and their honor, the purpose of their education seemed to be to instill courage and loyalty in the two boys. The result of this education made them as strong as tigers, yet gentle and approachable, not arrogant, frivolous, or bullying, so whenever the father and sons were mentioned in the borderlands of Chadong, everyone spoke their name with respect.

When the sons were still young, their father realized the elder was very much like him, but he couldn’t help doting a little more on the younger. Due to this unconscious bias, he named the elder son Tianbao and the younger Nuosong. The meaning was that what heaven protects might still face conflicts in human affairs, but what the Nuo gods send, according to local custom, could not be taken lightly. Nuosong was very handsome, and the boat people of Chadong, clumsy at praising such beauty, could only give him the nickname "Yue Yun." Though no one had actually seen Yue Yun, their general impression came from the stage character, a young warrior with a similar bearing.

In the border region where three provinces meet, the military authorities who had controlled the area for over a decade focused on maintaining peace and stability, handling matters properly, and no upheavals occurred. Water and land commerce were neither halted by war nor affected by bandits; everything was orderly, and the people were content with their lot. Except for when a cow died, a boat capsized, or some other major misfortune struck, bringing deep sorrow, the struggles and suffering elsewhere in China seemed never to reach the people of this border town.

The busiest days of the year in the border town were the Dragon Boat Festival, the Mid-Autumn Festival, and the New Year. The excitement these three festivals brought to the locals fifty years ago remains unchanged to this day, still standing as the most meaningful days for the residents.

On the day of the Dragon Boat Festival, women and children all wore new clothes and painted the character "king" on their foreheads with realgar wine. Every household was sure to eat fish and meat that day. Around eleven in the morning, everyone in Chadong had lunch, and after eating, those living in town all locked their doors and headed out of town to the riverbank to watch the boat races. Those with acquaintances on River Street could watch from the stilted houses; otherwise, they stood at the customs office or by the docks. The dragon boats raced from a spot in the Long Pool to the customs office. This day, military officers, tax officials, and local dignitaries all gathered at the customs office to watch the excitement. The boat races had been prepared days in advance, with groups and teams selecting sturdy, agile young men to practice advancing and retreating in the pool. The boats were very different from ordinary wooden ones, uniformly long and narrow, with high, upturned ends, painted with vermilion lines. Normally, they were stored in dry caves by the river, and when needed, they were dragged into the water. Each boat could hold twelve to eighteen oarsmen, a leader, a drummer, and a gong player. Each oarsman held a short oar, rowing forward to the rhythm of the drum, which regulated the pace. The leader, sitting at the bow with a red cloth tied around his head, waved two small command flags left and right to direct the boat. The drummers and gong players, usually seated in the middle, beat out a simple, rhythmic sound to set the oarsmen’s stroke. A boat’s speed depended entirely on the drum, so when two boats raced fiercely, the drumming thundered like rolling thunder. With the cheers of the crowd on both banks, it reminded one of Liang Hongyu drumming during the water battle at Laoguan River or Niu Gao capturing Yang Yao in a water fight. Any boat that pulled ahead would receive a reward at the customs office—a red cloth and a small silver medal—which, hung on anyone’s head, symbolized the team’s cooperative glory. Enthusiastic soldiers, whenever a boat won, would set off five-hundred-firecracker strings at the water’s edge to celebrate the victory.

After the boat races, the garrison commander, to share the joy with the people and enhance the holiday’s pleasure, released thirty green-headed, long-necked drakes with red cloth strips tied around their necks into the river. Soldiers and civilians who were good swimmers jumped into the water to chase them. Whoever caught a duck became its owner. Thus, the Long Pool took on a new look, with ducks everywhere and people chasing them everywhere.

The boat races and the duck-chasing contests lasted until evening.

Shunshun, the "boss of the docks," had been an excellent swimmer in his youth, never missing a duck when he dove into the water to chase them. But once his younger son, Nuosong, turned twelve and could dive underwater to reach the ducks, then suddenly pop up to catch them, the father said with self-deprecation, "Well, with you handling these things, I don’t need to go in the water anymore." And so he truly stopped competing in duck-catching. Rescuing people from the water, however, was a different matter. Helping others escape danger, even jumping into fire, remained his inescapable duty, even at eighty!

Tianbao and Nuosong were both top swimmers and rowers in the area.

The Dragon Boat Festival was approaching again. On the fifth, the boat races were to be held, and on the first, a meeting on River Street decided which boat assigned to that street would enter the water that day. Tianbao had to travel upstream that day with a land merchant to deliver festival goods to Longtan in eastern Sichuan, so only Nuosong participated. Sixteen strong, calf-like young men brought incense, candles, firecrackers, and a high-legged drum covered in raw cowhide and painted with a vermilion Taiji symbol to the cave upstream where the boat was stored. After burning incense and candles, they dragged the boat into the water, boarded it, lit the firecrackers, and beat the drum. The boat shot like an arrow downstream toward the Long Pool.

It was still morning; by the afternoon, the fishermen’s dragon boat from across the river had also entered the water, and the two boats began practicing various racing methods. The first sound of drums on the water brought joy to many, signaling the festival’s approach. Those living in stilted houses by the river, waiting and hoping for distant loved ones, were also reminded of them by the drumming. During this festival, many boats would rush home, while others would celebrate halfway. Amid it all, there were joys and sorrows unseen, playing out on River Street in this small mountain town, making some smile and others frown.

When the booming drumbeat crossed the water and hills to reach the ferry dock, the yellow dog was the first to notice. It barked loudly, ran around the house in alarm, and when someone crossed the ferry, it followed the boat to the eastern bank, where it ran to a small hilltop, barking toward the town.

Cuicui was sitting on a large rock outside the door, weaving grasshoppers and centipedes from palm leaves for fun. She saw the yellow dog sleeping in the sun, then suddenly wake and run around frantically, crossing the river and returning. She scolded it: "Dog, dog, what are you doing? Stop that!"

But soon, she heard the sound. She ran around the house too, crossed the stream with the yellow dog, stood on the small hilltop for a long time listening, letting the enchanting drumbeat carry her back to a past festival.

This happened two years ago. On the fifth day of the fifth month, the ferry’s grandfather found a substitute, then took the yellow dog and Cuicui to town to watch the boat races by the river. The riverbank was packed with people. Four vermilion long boats glided in the pool. The dragon boat water had just risen, the river water was pea-green, and the weather was so clear. The drums boomed. Cuicui pursed her lips and said nothing, her heart filled with unspeakable joy. There were too many people by the river; everyone was staring at the water. Before long, though the yellow dog was still by her side, Grandfather had been squeezed out of sight.

Cuicui watched the boats but thought, "Grandfather will find me soon enough." But after a long time, he still hadn’t come, and she began to feel a little uneasy. The day before they went to town with the yellow dog, Grandfather had asked Cuicui, "Tomorrow, there’ll be boat races in town. If you go alone, are you afraid of the crowds?" Cuicui replied, "I’m not afraid of crowds, but it’s no fun by myself." So Grandfather thought for a while, then remembered an old acquaintance in town. He went into town that night to ask the old man to tend the ferry for a day, while he accompanied Cuicui to town for fun. Because that old man was even lonelier than the ferryman—no family, not even a dog—they agreed he’d come to the house in the morning for a meal and a cup of realgar wine. The next day, the man came, ate, and took over the duties. Cuicui and the others then headed to town. On the road, Grandfather, as if remembering something, asked Cuicui again, "Cuicui, Cuicui, with so many people and so much excitement, do you dare to watch the dragon boats by the river alone?" Cuicui said, "Why wouldn’t I? But what’s the fun in being alone?" At the riverbank, the four vermilion boats in the Long Pool completely captured her attention, and Grandfather seemed almost irrelevant. Grandfather thought, "It’s still early; there’s at least three hours until the end. That friend by the stream should also come see the young people’s fun. If I go back and swap places with him, I’ll make it in time." So he asked Cuicui, "There are too many people. Stand here and watch, don’t move. I have some business elsewhere, but I’ll definitely be back to walk you home." Cuicui, absorbed in the two boats racing side by side, agreed without a second thought. Grandfather knew the yellow dog by Cuicui’s side might be more reliable than him, so he went back to check on the ferry.

When Grandfather reached the ferry dock, he saw the old friend substituting for him standing under the white pagoda, listening to the distant drumbeats.

Grandfather called to him, asking him to pull the boat over. They crossed the stream and stood again under the white pagoda. The man asked why the old ferryman had come back. Grandfather said he wanted to relieve him for a while, so he had left Cuicui by the river and hurried back, allowing the man to go watch the festivities. He added, "If you enjoy it, no need to come back. Just find Cuicui and tell her; she can go home on her own. If the little girl’s afraid to go back, walk with her!" But the substitute had lost interest in watching the dragon boats and preferred to share a few cups of liquor with the old ferryman on the big rock by the stream. The old ferryman was delighted, took out his gourd, and pushed it toward the man from town. They chatted about old Dragon Boat Festival stories and drank. Soon, the man got drunk and collapsed on the rock.

With the man passed out, he couldn’t go to town, and Grandfather, bound by duty, couldn’t leave the ferry. Cuicui, left alone by the river, was bound to worry.

After the boat races ended with a final winner, the town’s officer had sent a small boat to release a flock of ducks into the pool. Still, Grandfather hadn’t appeared. Cuicui feared he might be waiting for her somewhere, so she squeezed through the crowds with the yellow dog, searching for him, but found no trace. As dusk approached, soldiers who had carried benches out of town to watch the excitement started carrying them back home. Only three or five ducks remained in the pool, and the chasers were dwindling. The setting sun fell toward Cuicui’s home upstream, and twilight veiled the river with a thin mist. Seeing this scene, a frightening thought suddenly struck Cuicui: "What if Grandfather is dead?"

She remembered his instruction not to leave the spot, so she dismissed the thought, reasoning that he must have gone into town or to an acquaintance’s place, been held up by drink, and couldn’t come right away. Since that was possible, she didn’t want to rush home with the yellow dog before dark, so she stayed by the stone dock, waiting for Grandfather.

A little while later, the two long boats across the river had moored in the side stream and vanished, and almost all the spectators had dispersed. In the stilted houses where courtesans lived, lamps were lit, and someone was playing a small drum and a moon guitar, singing songs. In other houses, there was the clamor of drinking games. On the boats moored beneath the stilted houses, people were preparing food, pouring greens and radishes into hot oil with a sizzling sound. The river surface grew hazy, and it looked as if only a single white object floated in the pool.There was only one person still chasing the duck.

  Cuicui still didn’t leave the dock, always believing her grandfather would come find her and take her home.
