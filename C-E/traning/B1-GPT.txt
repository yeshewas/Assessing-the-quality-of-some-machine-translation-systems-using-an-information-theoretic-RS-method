Section 【1】: “Border Town” Preface

For peasants and soldiers, I harbor an unspeakable warmth, a feeling evident throughout all my works. I never hide this sentiment. I grew up in the kind of small town written about in my works. My grandfather, father, and brothers all served in the military: those who died did so in service, and those alive will inevitably spend their lives in duty. From the side of the world I know, to narrate their loves, hates, joys, and sorrows—even if this pen is clumsy or somewhat off-topic—still feels appropriate. For they are upright, honest; their lives have aspects of great nobility and others quite ordinary, their characters some parts beautiful, some trivial—as I write about them, to make them more human and relatable, I naturally write quietly and sincerely. But thus, this work might inevitably become a kind of futile labor.

According to current trends, literary theorists, critics, and most readers are likely to feel unpleasant about this kind of work. The former claim “it’s outdated,” saying China doesn’t need works like this. The latter, “too worried about being outdated,” currently don’t want to read such works. This is indeed true. What is “outdated”? A somewhat silly person might never understand, but who doesn’t fear being “outdated”? I want to say: “This book is not written for that majority.” Those who have read three or five foreign books on literary theory and criticism, or also a large number of classical and modern world masterpieces, often do not permit themselves, beyond being “erudite,” to know a little of another place and another matter in China. So even if this work fits some literary theory and critics praise it variously, such criticism still inadvertently insults the author. For they neither want to understand this nation’s real loves, hates, joys, and sorrows, nor can explain this work’s gains and losses—this book is not written for them. As for literary enthusiasts, whether university or high school students, mostly scattered in densely populated cities at home, often very honestly and naively spend some precious time reading recently published domestic literature. They are controlled and dominated by a habit created by some theorists, critics, smart publishers, and gossipers in the literary world working together. Their lives are truly far removed from the world this work mentions. They don’t need this kind of work, and this book does not wish to gain them. Theorists have literary theories from publications worldwide to reference—no fear of having nothing to say; critics have their owed grudges or favors from writers and works enough to vilify or praise for a lifetime. Most readers, regardless of taste or belief, always have works to read. Precisely because of concern for the general reader public, are there not many who, supposedly for the common reader, endlessly spin like tops? Even if this book’s publication is not wholly abandoned by leading theorists and critics, and the led majority of readers do not completely give it up, the author has long consciously abandoned that “majority.”

This book is only prepared for some “who have left school or never approached it, yet know some Chinese writing, are positioned in jobs where literary theory, criticism, and fabricated rumors do not reach, living in that society, and deeply concerned with all the benefits and drawbacks of the entire nation in space and time.” They truly know what the current countryside is, want to know what past rural life was like; they would also willingly learn from this book a little about a tiny corner of the rural world and military personnel. The world I describe, though entirely unfamiliar to them, their tolerance and eagerness to seek comfort and knowledge from a book will surely allow them to read it smoothly. I do not stop here; I also prepare to give them a chance for contrast, mentioning in another work the civil war of the past twenty years, how some frontline peasants’ characters and souls were heavily oppressed, losing their original simplicity, thrift, peace, and honesty, becoming a new kind of creature. How they suffer from exactions and opium’s poison, becoming poor and lazy! I will simply narrate the worries small people face amid historical changes toward an unknown destiny, and the ideas and desires about “surviving” and “how to survive” caused by malnutrition. My readers should be rational, based on caring for China’s current social changes, recognizing the nation’s past greatness and present decline, people quietly working at the nation’s revival. This work may only give them some nostalgic sentiment, or a bitter smile, or even a nightmare, but perhaps also courage and confidence!

April 24, 1934

Section 【2】: Chapter 1

1

Passing from Sichuan to Hunan, eastward lies an official road. This official road approaches the western border of Xiang and reaches a small mountain town named “Cha Dong.” There is a small creek there, by which stands a white little tower, beneath which lives a lone family. This household has only an old man, a girl, and a yellow dog.

The creek flows around the mountain, and after about three li joins the great river of Cha Dong. If one crosses the creek and takes the little mountain path, it’s only one li to the edge of Cha Dong town. The creek flows like a bow’s back, the mountain road like its string, thus there are slight distance differences. The creek is about twenty zhang wide, its riverbed made of large stones. Still water, even when deep enough a pole cannot touch the bottom, remains clear and transparent; fish swimming in the river can be counted. Since the creek is a vital channel for Sichuan-Hunan traffic, its water level often rises and falls. Restricted by finances, no bridge was built; instead, a square-headed ferry was arranged. This ferry can carry about twenty passengers, people and horses included, over the river at once. When there are many people, it makes repeated trips. A small bamboo pole stands upright at the ferry’s front, hanging an adjustable iron ring. On both creek banks, some old cable stretches across in the water grooves. When crossing, people hang this iron ring on the cable; those on the boat then pull hand-over-hand along the cable, slowly hauling the boat to the opposite shore. When nearing shore, the ferry manager shouts “slow down, slow down” while swiftly jumping off to pull the iron ring. Then all people, goods, cattle, and horses disembark and cross the little mountain, disappearing. The ferry, owned by the government, does not charge crossing fees. If someone feels uneasy and throws some money on the boat’s board, the manager will pick it up one by one and return it to the person's palm with a serious face as if in an argument: “I have my measure: three dou of rice, seven hundred qian, enough. Who wants this?”

But it’s no use. For peace of mind, some people still give money for the effort. The ferry manager, unable to refuse, for peace of mind, takes the money to Cha Dong to buy tea and grass tobacco. Tying bundles of fine local grass tobacco from Cha Dong to his belt, he generously gives them to those crossing who need it. Sometimes, sensing that travelers from afar are quite interested in the tobacco, he ties a small bunch to their bundles, saying, “Don’t you smoke this? This is good, wonderful, tastes fine, also good for gifts!” As for tea, in June it is soaked in big jars with hot water and offered to travelers to quench their thirst.

The ferry manager is the old man living beneath the tower. He has lived seventy years and has guarded this creek since he was twenty. In fifty years, countless people have crossed his ferry. Though so old, he should have rested long ago, but the heavens do not allow it. He seems unable to part from this portion of life. He never contemplates the meaning of this duty to himself, simply living quietly and faithfully. Replacing heaven, he feels life’s strength when the sun rises, and when it sets, he is not burdened by thoughts of dying with it. The girl, who accompanies him, is his only companion. His only friend is the ferry and a yellow dog; his only kin is the girl.

The girl’s mother, the old ferryman’s only daughter, fifteen years ago, secretly had an ambiguous relationship with a Cha Dong soldier behind that honest father’s back. After having a child, the stationed soldier wanted to escape downstream with her. But in terms of behavior, one violated the soldier’s duty, the other had to leave a lonely father. After consideration, the soldier decided, as she lacked courage to flee far and he couldn’t ruin his soldier’s honor, to take poison together to die, since no one could stop that. The woman cared for the child in her belly and could not decide. The matter was discovered by the ferryman’s father, who didn’t add a word or acknowledge the event, living quietly as if it never happened. The daughter felt both shame and pity, staying by her father’s side. After giving birth, she went to the creek, drank much cold water, and died. In a near miracle, the orphan survived and grew up; in a flash, she was thirteen years old. The area by their home has many bamboo groves pressing in green. The old ferryman casually picked a close name for the poor orphan—“Cuicui.”

Cuicui grew up in wind and sunlight, her skin darkened, like the green mountains and waters around. Her eyes are as clear as crystal. Naturally, while raising and educating her, she was innocent and lively, seemingly like a little wild animal. So obedient, like a small yellow deer on the mountain, never thinking cruel thoughts, never worrying, never getting angry. Usually, when strangers on the ferry pay attention to her, she would gaze at them with bright eyes, ready to flee into the deep mountains anytime, but once understanding that people harbor no malice, she would leisurely play by the water again.

Rain or shine, the old ferryman guards the boat’s front. When someone crosses, he slightly bends over, pulling the bamboo cable with both hands to row the ferry across the creek. Sometimes tired, he lies on a big rock by the creek and falls asleep. When people ask for crossing from the opposite bank, Cuicui will not let her grandfather rise, jumping off the boat swiftly to help ferry travelers across; everything smooth and without delay. Sometimes she and her grandfather, along with the yellow dog, handle crossings together. When the ferry nears shore, the grandfather greets: “Slow down, slow down.” Then the yellow dog, holding the rope in its mouth, leaps first, seemingly understanding how to fulfill its duty, tightly gripping the boat’s rope to pull it ashore.

On clear and calm days with no crossings, the whole day is leisurely. The grandfather and Cuicui sit on a big rock at the door sunning themselves. They throw a piece of wood from a height into the water, enticing the yellow dog to leap from the rock to fetch the wood. Or Cuicui and the dog both listen as the grandfather tells old war stories from the town. Or the grandfather and Cuicui each play a small bamboo flute at their lips, blowing tunes for wedding processions or seeing off brides. When crossings come, the old ferryman puts down his flute and goes alone to the boatside, ferrying people across. One person on the rock, seeing the boat moving, shouts sharply:

“Grandpa, grandpa, listen to me play, you sing!”

At the creek’s center, grandpa happily sings along, his hoarse voice mixing with the bamboo flute in the quiet air, making the creek seem livelier. (In fact, the echo of the song makes everything even quieter.)

Sometimes crossing are young cattle from east Sichuan passing Cha Dong, or sheep flocks, or bridal sedan chairs. Cuicui insists on playing ferry manager, standing at the boat’s front, lazily pulling the cable to let the boat go slowly. After the cattle, sheep, and sedan chair land, Cuicui follows, standing on a little hill to watch them leave until far away, then returns to the boat to pull it close to the shore near home, softly mimicking lamb calls, cow calls, or picking a bunch of wildflowers to tie on her head, playing bride alone.

Cha Dong mountain town is only one li from the ferry. When buying oil and salt, or during festivals when the grandfather needs a drink, the grandfather stays home, while the yellow dog accompanies Cuicui into town to prepare things. At the grocery, heaps of vermicelli, big jars of sugar, firecrackers, red candles—all leave a deep impression on Cuicui, who retells all these things to her grandfather for half a day after returning. Along the river are many upstream boats, with dozens of boatmen busy loading and unloading goods. These boats are much larger and more interesting than the ferry, and Cuicui never forgets them.Two

The Cha Dong area builds its town relying on water and mountains. On the side close to the mountains, the city wall snakes up along the mountain like a long dragon. On the side facing the water, space is left outside the city near the riverbank to set up docks, where small covered boats are moored. Downstream, these boats transport tung oil, green salt, and dyed pallets. Upstream, they carry cotton, cotton yarn, cloth, general goods, and seafood. Connecting the various docks is a river street, where many houses are half on land and half over the water. Because space is limited, these houses all have stilted floors. When the spring flood rises and the water gradually flows into the street, those living along the river all use long ladders—one end resting on the eaves, the other on the city wall. Everyone curses and shouts as they carry bundles, bedding, and rice jars up the ladders into the city. When the water recedes, they come out through the city gates again. In years when the flood is especially fierce, one or two of these stilted houses along the river are surely washed away by the surge, and everyone watches helplessly from the city. Those who suffered losses also just stand and watch, seemingly speechless about the damage, as if resigned to nature’s arrangements and the sight of other inevitable misfortunes.

When the water rises, people on the city walls can watch the suddenly widened river surface, the immense current flowing violently. Along with the mountain water floating downstream come houses, cattle, sheep, and large trees. At slower currents, near the tax bureau’s barge, there are often people rowing small boats who spot something floating down the center of the river—a beast, a piece of wood, or an empty boat from which a woman or child cries out. They quickly row toward it, and some downstream meet these floating objects, tie them with long ropes, and row back to shore. These honest, brave people are both business-minded and righteous, much like the locals. Not only saving people and goods but also enjoying the adventure, they act swiftly and courageously, inspiring applause from onlookers.

That river is the historically famous You River, now called the Bai River. Downstream of the Bai River, after it merges with the Yuan River at Chenzhou, the water turns somewhat turbid, as if mixed with mountain spring water. Going upstream, the deep pools three to five zhang deep are crystal clear. The sunlight reflects off the surface, showing the small white stones and patterned agate stones clearly on the riverbed. Fish swim in the water as if floating in the air. Both banks have high mountains, covered with fine bamboo suitable for paper-making, which remains deep green year-round, dazzling to the eye. Houses near the water are mostly set amid peach and apricot blossoms. In spring, just notice that wherever there are peach blossoms, there is a home; wherever there is a home, wine can be bought. In summer, dazzling purple floral cloth drying in the sun serves as a banner of residence. In autumn and winter, houses on cliffs or by the water always delight the eye. Yellow mud walls, black tiles, their locations always so well placed and perfectly integrated with the surroundings, give a thoroughly pleasant impression. A traveler with even a slight affinity for poetry and painting, curled up in a small boat, traveling for thirty days along this small river, would never feel bored precisely because miracles are everywhere—natural boldness and exquisite details alike all inspire admiration and love.

The source of the Bai River comes from the Sichuan border. Boats traveling upstream on the Bai River in spring flood can reach Xiushan of Sichuan. Within Hunan territory, Cha Dong is the last water dock. Though the river surface at Cha Dong is about half a li wide, in autumn and winter when the water recedes, the riverbed’s flowing part is no more than twenty zhang, with the rest just a stretch of green stones. Beyond this point, boats cannot go further upstream, so all import and export goods from eastern Sichuan are unloaded and loaded here. Export goods are carried by porters using fir wood poles balanced on their shoulders, while imported goods are likewise bundled and carried by manual labor here.

Within this place, only one garrison camp is stationed, formed from what used to be Green Camp conscript soldiers, along with about five hundred households. (Among these households, some own small mountain fields and oil mills or act as small capitalists stocking tung oil, rice, or cotton yarn, but most are families with military registration from the conscript soldiers stationed here.) There is also a tax collection bureau operating in a small temple below the river street outside the city, often flying a long banner. The bureau chief lives inside the city. One camp of soldiers is stationed at the old veteran general’s office. Except for the bugler who sounds the horn daily on the city walls to let people know the troops are still here, the other soldiers seem almost nonexistent. On winter daylight hours, going into the city, everywhere in front of houses people hang clothes and greens to dry. Sweet potatoes with vines hang under eaves. Bags made of palm fiber filled with chestnuts, hazelnuts, and other hard-shelled fruits also hang under the eaves. Chickens of all sizes crow and play around corners. Occasionally, some men saw wood at their front doorsteps or chop firewood with axes, stacking the chopped wood in the yard like little towers. Or one might see middle-aged women wearing stiff blue cloth clothes, white cloth aprons with button decorations on their chests, bending over in the sunlight, chatting while working. Everything remains so quiet and serene; every person lives each day in such simple solitude. This quietness increases their reflection on "human affairs" and fuels their dreams. Those surviving in this small town certainly all harbor inevitable hopes and aversions about human relations in their own portions of day-to-day life. What these people think—who knows? Living at a higher spot inside the city, standing at the doorstep offers a clear view across the river and its scenery. When boats come, from the distant riverside you can see countless haulers. Some haulers come from downstream places carrying fine snacks and foreign sugar, which they bring into the city to exchange for money. When boats arrive, children’s imaginations are certainly with those haulers. As for the adults, their minds are mostly occupied with hatching a brood of chicks, raising two pigs, trusting the outbound boatmen to bring back a pair of gold earrings, two zhang of official blue cloth, or a jar of good soy sauce and a double-layered Mejia lampshade.

Though so quiet and peaceful, this small town is a junction of eastern Sichuan trade, so the small river street outside the city is somewhat livelier. There are inns where merchants stay, stationary barbershops, as well as restaurants, general stores, oil shops, salt depots, and colorful clothing shops, each holding their place and decorating the river street. There are also shops selling sandalwood pulleys, bamboo cables, and pots for use on boats, introducing the sailor profession, families who live at the docks. In front of small restaurants, the long tables often display carp cooked golden brown and tofu decorated with red chili threads, lying in shallow bowls. Beside the bowls, big bamboo containers hold bundles of red chopsticks, freely available to anyone wishing to spend a little money. Then a woman with finely drawn eyebrows and a face powdered white will come over and ask, "Big brother, sir, want sweet wine? Want distilled spirits?" Men with a fiery temper and a sense of humor and some rapport with the shopkeeper will pretend to be angry and say, "Eat sweet wine? I’m no child, why ask for sweet wine!" Then thick distilled spirits are scooped with bamboo tubes from large jars into earthen bowls and immediately brought to the table. The general stores sell Mejia oil and Western oil lamps that use Mejia oil, as well as incense paper. Oil shops stock tung oil. Salt depots pile green salt from fire wells. Colorful clothing shops offer white cotton yarn, big cloth, cotton, and black crepe silk from Baotou. Shops selling goods for boats display everything imaginable; sometimes heavy iron anchors over a hundred jin rest outside waiting for customers to inquire price. Those specializing in introducing sailors to eat dock meals have homes on river street with wide-open doors all day long. Shipowners dressed in blue satin mandarin jackets and scruffy sailors go in and out. The place is like a teahouse but doesn’t sell tea, not a tobacco shop but one can smoke. Visitors talk shop about business on the boats, but loading and unloading of boats, and rowing and hauling crews mostly follow certain rules, so there’s no need to argue numbers. Most come here just to “gather.” With the "dragon head boss" as the center, they discuss local news, commerce between the two provinces, and the “new things” downstream. Most gatherings for money collection also take place here, and when playing dice to see who will lead the next round of meetings, it often happens here. Their common business involves two things: buying and selling boats, and buying and selling brides.

Because of the developed commerce, a certain group of dependents arose in the big city—they formed because of merchants’ and sailors’ needs. This small border town river street also has a group of women gathered in some stilt houses. These women are either brought from nearby countryside or followed Sichuan troops to Hunan’s backward lands. They wear fake satin dresses, printed cloth pants, with eyebrows drawn into thin lines. Their large hair buns are smeared with strongly scented, vulgar oil-based products. In the daytime when not busy, they sit at doorsteps making shoes, embroidering phoenix patterns on shoe tips with red and green threads, or embroidering floral pouches for their sailor lovers, all while watching passersby to pass the long days. Or they lean on riverside windows watching sailors unloading goods and listen to sailors climbing the mast singing. At night, they take turns entertaining merchants and sailors, thoroughly fulfilling the obligations expected of a "prostitute."

Because border customs are simple and pure, even as prostitutes they remain blunt and sincere. With strangers, business must first be paid for before locking the door and indulging, but with acquaintances, money becomes optional. Prostitutes mostly live on Sichuan merchants’ support, but their affections are often towards sailors. Those with strong feelings bite each other’s lips and necks, swear oaths, and promise “after parting, no mischief allowed.” For forty or fifty days, the one afloat on the boat and the one on shore both spend their days wounded, tightly binding their hearts to the distant other. Especially the women’s feelings are sincere, to an indescribable degree. When the man fails to return on schedule, they often dream of the boat mooring, someone stumbling down the gangplank and running straight to them. Or, if doubts arise at midday, the dream shows the man singing aboard the mast to the other side, ignoring her. Those more fragile in character commit opium overdose by plunging into the river in dreams; those stronger wield a kitchen knife and rush toward the sailor. Though living so apart from general society, tears and joy, woven amidst love, hate, and loss, merge into their lives. They are like young lives in other lands, soaked through and through with love and hate, feeling heat and cold, forgetting all else. The differences are only that these people are more genuine and somewhat more confused. Short-term arrangements, long-term marriages, occasional lock-in, these are bodily transactions of women. Because of the pure folk customs, those involved do not find it obscene or shameful, nor do bystanders criticize or despise them from a scholarly perspective. These people value loyalty over profit, keep their promises, and even as prostitutes are often more trustworthy than city dwellers who preach morality and shame.

The dockmaster is named Shunshun, a man who spent his early days in military service during the late Qing. During the revolution, he served as a squad leader in the famous 49th Army Banner. Among former squad leaders, some became great men, some were executed, while he returned home with a youthful foot ailment from happy events. With some savings, he bought a six-oar white wooden boat, renting it to a poor boat owner and transporting goods between Cha Dong and Chenzhou. Luck was good; within half a year, the boat remained intact. He used the money earned to marry a small widow with a pale face and black hair who had some modest property. Several years later, he owned four boats, a shop, and two sons on this river.

But though this generous, straightforward man’s business went well, his fondness for making friends and generosity in helping others meant he could not amass wealth like oil merchants. Having experienced hard times himself, he understood the hardships of those traveling away from home and the feelings of disappointment. So any boatman bankrupt because of accidents, passing retired soldiers, wandering scholars seeking help—none who came here asking for aid failed to receive it. He earned money on the water while freely giving it away. Though he had a slight foot problem and could swim, walking evenly was difficult for him, yet he was so fair and selfless. All matters on the water were naturally simple, governed by established customs. Whenever boats collided or one boat hindered another’s interests, customary methods resolved the disputes. However, to apply these customs, a highly respected elder was necessary. One autumn, the original manager died, and Shunshun became the successor. At that time he was only fifty, sensible, fair, peaceful, and not fond of wealth, so no one doubted him because of his age.

By now, his older son is eighteen, the younger sixteen. Both young men are as strong as young bulls, capable of steering boats, swimming, and walking long distances. They do all the things young men from small towns and cities can do, all done thoroughly and expertly. The older one, like their father, is bold, open, and unconcerned with petty formalities. The younger one takes after their pale-faced, black-haired mother; quiet, with elegantly arched eyebrows revealing intelligence and great emotional depth at a glance.The two brothers, having grown up, had to be trained in various aspects of life. Their father took turns sending the two children on trips to different places. When going downstream by boat, they often accompanied their own crew, sharing hardships and joys alike. When rowing, they took the heaviest oar; when pulling the rope, they pulled the headrope or the secondary rope. They ate dried fish, chili, and sour pickled vegetables, and slept on the hard wooden planks of the cabin. When going upstream by land, they followed the Sichuan East passengers and freight, passing through Xiushan and Longtan, and conducting business in Youyang. Regardless of cold, heat, rain, or snow, they always wore straw sandals and walked from post to post. Moreover, they carried short knives. If absolutely necessary, they would draw their knives sharply, stand in an open space, and wait for the opponent, then settle the matter with close combat. The code of conduct in their group was that "to deal with enemies, a knife is necessary; to keep friends, a knife is also necessary," so whenever a knife was needed, they never missed the opportunity to use it. Learning trade, social skills, living in a new place, and learning to use a knife to protect their body and honor—the purpose of education seemed to be to teach the two children courage and loyalty as human beings. As a result of this education, both became strong like tigers, yet kind and familiar, not arrogant, lazy, frivolous, or tyrannical. Therefore, when the three—father and sons—were mentioned in the border area of Cha Dong, everyone respected the family name.

When the father was raising the two sons at a very young age, he realized that the elder son was very much like himself, but he tended to spoil the younger son slightly. Due to this unconscious bias, he named the elder son Tianbao and the younger Nuosong. The meaning was that those protected by heaven might still have some conflicts in human affairs, but those sent by the Nuo spirit, according to local customs, could not be taken lightly. Nuosong was very handsome. The Cha Dong boatmen were clumsy in praising such beauty and only knew to give him a nickname, "Yue Yun." Although no one had ever seen Yue Yun in person, the common impression came from the young scholar Yue Yun on the opera stage, resembling a similar aura.

At the junction of two provinces, the local military chief who had been in charge for more than ten years focused on maintaining security and order, handled matters properly, and no incidents occurred. Water and land commerce was neither interrupted by war nor affected by bandits. Everything was orderly, and the people lived peacefully and contentedly. Apart from unfortunate events like deaths of cattle, boat capsizes, or other fatalities in households, the local people seemed completely unaffected by the misfortunes occurring elsewhere in China.

The busiest days in the border town during the year were the Dragon Boat Festival, Mid-Autumn Festival, and New Year. How excited the local people were about these three festivals thirty to fifty years ago remains unchanged even now; these are still the most meaningful days for residents there.

On the Dragon Boat Festival, local women and children all wore new clothes, and used realgar mixed with wine to paint the character "Wang" on their foreheads. Every family could eat fish and meat on this day. Around eleven in the morning, all the people of Cha Dong had their lunch. After eating, those living in the town would lock their doors and take the whole family out of town to watch the boat races by the river. If they knew people at River Street, they would watch by the balcony doors on River Street; otherwise, they stood by the customs gate or various docks. The dragon boats started from a spot in the pond called Changtan and ended before the customs gate. It was a competitive race. Military officers, tax officials, and other influential locals would gather in front of the customs gate to enjoy the spectacle. Preparations for rowing started several days earlier. Teams were formed, selecting several strong and agile young men to practice advancing and retreating in the pond. The boats were very different from ordinary wooden boats. All were long and narrow, with both ends raised high, and the bodies painted with vermilion lines. Normally, they were stored in dry caves by the river and dragged into the water when needed. Each boat could seat twelve to eighteen rowers, one leader, one drummer, and one gong player. Each rower held a short oar and rowed in time with the drumbeat slowly or quickly depending on the rhythm to push the boat forward. The person sitting at the front of the boat wore a red cloth headband, waving two small signal flags to direct the boat's advance or retreat. The drummers and gong players sat in the middle of the boat, and once the boat moved, they beat the drums and gongs loudly and simply to coordinate the rowing rhythm for the rowers. The speed of the boat depended on the drumbeats. When two boats fiercely competed, the drum sounds were thunderous, combined with cheers from both banks, evoking memories of Liang Hongyu beating the drum in the old Guan River water battle or Niu Gao's water battle capture of Yang Yao. Any boat that got ahead could receive a prize before the customs gate—a red horse figurine or a small silver medal. No matter who among the crew wore it, it symbolized the glory of that boat’s teamwork. The enthusiastic soldiers celebrated each time a boat won by setting off five hundred firecrackers on the shore.

After the boat race, the local garrison commander, to blend with the people and enhance the festival’s joy, released thirty large male ducks with green heads and long necks into the river. Red cloth strips were tied around their necks. Military personnel and civilians skilled at swimming jumped into the water to chase the ducks. Whoever caught a duck became its owner. Thus, the Changtan transformed into a new spectacle, with ducks everywhere on the water and people chasing them all over.

The competition between boats and the chase of ducks continued until late evening.

The headman of the water dock, Shunshun, was once an expert swimmer in his youth. When chasing ducks in the water, he never failed. But when his younger son Nuosong turned twelve, he could dive underwater, swim close to ducks, and suddenly emerge to catch them. His father jokingly said, "Great, with you guys doing this, I don’t need to go into the water anymore." So indeed he stopped competing for ducks. But when it came to rescuing people in the water, that was another matter. Helping someone avoid danger, even at eighty years old, still remained an unavoidable responsibility!

Both Tianbao and Nuosong were excellent local swimmers and rowers.

The Dragon Boat Festival was approaching again. On the fifth day, boats would race. The first day of the month, a meeting had decided which boat from River Street would be launched that day. Tianbao happened to be going upstream that day and, travelling overland with merchants to Sichuan East Longtan to deliver festival goods, so only Nuosong participated. Sixteen strong young men, carrying incense, firecrackers, and a high-legged drum made of raw cowhide painted with a vermilion Taiji diagram, gathered by the river near the upstream cave where the boats were kept. After burning incense, they dragged the boat into the water, boarded it, lit firecrackers, and beat the drum, propelling the boat like an arrow swiftly downstream toward Changtan.

It was still morning at that time. By afternoon, the dragon boats of the rival fishermen had also entered the water, and the two dragon boats began practicing various competition techniques. The drumbeats were heard on the water for the first time. Many people felt the joy of the approaching festival from the drum sounds. Those living in the riverside stilt houses waiting for someone arriving from afar also thought of them upon hearing the drums. During this festival, many boats would rush back home, while many only stopped halfway to celebrate. Among these, there were unseen human joys and sorrows, making some people busy, and causing some to frown in this small mountain town on River Street.

When the loud drumbeats rushed over the water and across the mountain to the ferry pier, the first to notice was the yellow dog. The yellow dog barked fiercely, startled, running erratically around the houses.
