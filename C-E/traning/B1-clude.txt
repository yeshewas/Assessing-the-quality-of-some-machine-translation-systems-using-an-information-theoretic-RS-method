Section [1]: Preface to "Border Town"

For farmers and soldiers, I harbor an inexpressible tender affection, and this sentiment can be seen throughout all my works. I have never concealed this feeling. I grew up in the kind of small rural town depicted in my works. My grandfather, father, and brothers all served in the military: those who have passed away died in service, and those still living will inevitably end their lives in service. From the perspective of the world I have encountered, I narrate their loves and hates, joys and sorrows. Even if this pen is clumsy, it should not stray too far from the subject. Because they are upright and honest, their lives are in some ways extremely great and in others extremely ordinary, their temperaments in some ways extremely beautiful and in others extremely trivial. When I set out to write about them, in order to make them more human and closer to human nature, I simply wrote down faithfully what I observed. But as a result, this work may inevitably become something of no practical use.

According to current trends, literary theorists, critics, and most readers easily develop unpleasant feelings toward such works. The former claim to be "not falling behind," telling people that China does not need such works, while the latter, "too worried about falling behind," are also unwilling to read such works at present. This is certainly true. What is "falling behind"? A person with some rationality might never understand it, but who among the masses does not fear "falling behind"? I have something to say: "This book is not written for such a majority." Those who have read three or five foreign books on literary theory and literary criticism, or who have also read a large collection of classical and modern world masterpieces, often find that their life experience does not permit them, beyond their "erudition," to know anything about another place in China and another kind of matter. Therefore, even if this work conforms to some literary theory, critics may heap various praises upon it, yet such criticism is still essentially an insult to the author. Since they do not wish to understand the true loves and hates, joys and sorrows of this nation, they cannot explain the merits and faults of this work. This book is not written for them. As for literature enthusiasts, they are either university students or middle school students, scattered in densely populated cities throughout the country, often very sincerely and naively devoting a portion of extremely precious time to reading recently published literary works from within the country. They are controlled and dominated by a habit created through the joint efforts of theorists, critics, clever publishers, and gossip-mongers accustomed to lying and spreading rumors. Their lives are also actually far removed from the world mentioned in this work. They do not need such a work, and this book does not hope to gain their attention either. Theorists have literary theories from publications of various countries to consult and need not worry about having nothing to say; critics have writers and works to whom they owe small debts of gratitude, enough for them to praise or condemn for a lifetime. Most readers, regardless of taste or belief, have works to read. Is it not precisely because of concern for the reading public that many people, it is said, for the sake of the reading public, are constantly spinning like tops? Though the publication of this book may not be abandoned by the theorists and critics who lead the majority, and the majority of readers being led may not completely abandon it, the author of this book has long since made up his mind to abandon this "majority."

This book is only prepared for those "who have already left school or have never had access to school, who still recognize Chinese characters, who are engaged in work beyond the reach of literary theory, literary criticism, and lying gossip, who live in that society, and who are deeply concerned with all the good and bad of this entire nation across space and time." They truly know what the present countryside is, wish to know what the past countryside was, and would also be willing to learn from this book something about the countryside and soldiers in a small corner of the world. The world I have written about, even if it is entirely a strange world to them, their tolerance and their enthusiasm in seeking comfort and knowledge from a book will certainly enable them to read this book with ease and composure. I do not stop here; I also prepare for them an opportunity for comparison. In another work, I will mention the internal wars of the past twenty years, showing what kind of new thing some peasants who bore the brunt have become after their character and soul were pressed down by great force, losing their original simplicity, frugality, peace, and integrity. How they have become impoverished and lazy through excessive taxation and the poison of opium! I will make a simple narration of the worries of small people amid change as this nation is carried by history toward an unknowable fate, and the concepts and desires of "living on" and "how to live on" arising from malnutrition. My readers should be those with reason, and this reason is based on concern for the changes in contemporary Chinese society, recognition of both the past greatness and present decline of this nation, each quietly engaged in the great cause of national revival. This work may only give them a nostalgic melancholy, or only give them a bitter smile, or perhaps give them a nightmare, but at the same time, it may also give them courage and confidence!

Written on April 24, 1934

Section [2]: Chapter 1

One

From Sichuan to Hunan, there is an official road to the east. When this official road approaches the Xiangxi border and reaches a small mountain town named "Chadong," there is a small stream. By the stream stands a white pagoda, and below the pagoda lives a solitary household. This family consists of only an old man, a girl, and a yellow dog.

The small stream flows downward, winds around the mountain, and after about three miles merges into the great river of Chadong. If one crosses the stream and goes over the small mountain, it is only one mile to the edge of Chadong city. The stream curves like a bow's back, and the mountain path like a bowstring, so there is a small difference in distance. The stream is about twenty zhang wide, with a riverbed made of large stones. The quiet water, even if deep enough that a pole cannot touch bottom, remains clear and transparent, and the fish swimming in the river can be counted as they come and go. Since the small stream is a thoroughfare for traffic between Sichuan and Hunan, the water constantly rises and falls. Limited by financial means, a bridge cannot be built, so a flat-bottomed ferry boat has been arranged. This ferry boat can carry about twenty passengers along with their horses in one trip, and when there are more people, it makes repeated journeys. A small bamboo pole is erected at the ferry head, with a movable iron ring hanging from it. Cables are strung along the water troughs at both ends of the stream bank. When someone needs to cross, they hang the iron ring on the cable, and the people on the boat pull themselves along the cable, slowly hauling the boat to the opposite shore. As the boat approaches the bank, the person managing the ferry shouts "Slow, slow," then suddenly leaps ashore, pulls the iron ring, and all the people, cargo, cattle, and horses disembark and disappear over the small mountain. The ferry is public property, so passengers need not pay. When someone feels uneasy and throws a handful of money onto the boat deck, the ferry manager will pick it up piece by piece and stuff it back into that person's hand with the serious manner of someone quarreling: "I have my measure—three pecks of rice and seven hundred coins—that's enough. Who needs this!"

But it doesn't work out that way. Everyone seeks peace of mind and a clear conscience. Who would feel comfortable not being compensated for their labor? So people still give money. The ferry manager, unable to refuse and also seeking peace of mind, has someone take this money to Chadong to buy tea and tobacco. He hangs bundles of the finest local tobacco around his waist and generously gives it to anyone who needs it when crossing. Sometimes, judging from a traveler's manner that they have taken considerable notice of the tobacco at his side, he ties a small bundle of tobacco to their bundle, saying, "Don't you smoke this? This is good stuff, wonderful stuff, great flavor, and it's suitable for giving as gifts too!" The tea is steeped in large jars with boiling water in June and given to passing travelers to quench their thirst.

The person managing this ferry is the old man living below the pagoda. He has lived seventy years, and since he was twenty, he has kept watch by this small stream. For fifty years, he has ferried countless people across. Though he is so old and should have retired, heaven does not permit him to rest. He seems unable to separate himself from this life. He never ponders the meaning of his work for himself, but simply lives quietly and faithfully. What replaces heaven, making him feel the power of life when the sun rises and preventing him from thinking he should die when the sun sets, is the girl at his side. His only friends are the ferry boat and the yellow dog; his only family is this girl.

The girl's mother, the old ferryman's only daughter, fifteen years ago developed an ambiguous relationship with a Chadong soldier, kept very secret from her honest father. After having a child, this garrison soldier wanted to persuade her to flee downstream with him. But considering the act of fleeing, one would violate a soldier's duty, and the other would have to abandon her lonely father. After some deliberation, the soldier, seeing she lacked the courage to leave and unwilling to ruin his reputation as a soldier, thought: if they could not live together, they could at least die together without anyone to stop them. He took poison first. The woman, concerned for the child in her womb, could not bear it and could not make a decision. The matter became known to her father, the ferryman, but he did not add a single weighty word. He simply continued living his days very peacefully, as if he had never heard of this matter. The daughter, filled with both shame and pity, remained at her father's side. After the child was born, she drank much cold water by the stream and died. Through what seemed nearly miraculous, this orphan grew up, and in the blink of an eye, she was thirteen years old. Because the area around the dwelling was covered with bamboo groves, their verdant color pressing in, the old ferryman casually gave this pitiful orphan a familiar name: "Cuicui."

Cuicui grew up in wind and sunshine, her skin turning dark. With green mountains and clear waters before her eyes, her pair of eyes were as clear as crystal. Nature both raised and educated her. She was innocent and lively by nature, in every way like a small wild creature. She was so well-behaved, like a yellow deer on a hillside, never thinking of cruelty, never worrying, never getting angry. When strangers on the ferry boat paid her attention, she would stare at them with her bright eyes, appearing ready at any moment to flee into the deep mountains. But once she understood they meant no harm, she would play by the water with ease and composure.

The old ferryman, rain or shine, always kept watch at the ferry head. When someone needed to cross, he would bend slightly at the waist, guide the bamboo cable with both hands, and pull the boat across the stream. Sometimes, when tired, he would lie on a large stone by the stream and fall asleep. When people on the opposite bank waved and called for the ferry, Cuicui would not let her grandfather get up. Instead, she would jump into the boat and nimbly ferry the travelers across the stream for him, handling everything smoothly and never making mistakes. Sometimes she and her grandfather and the yellow dog would be on the boat together. When ferrying, she would work with her grandfather. As the boat approached the shore and her grandfather was calling out to the passengers, "Slow, slow," the yellow dog would bite the rope in its mouth and be the first to leap ashore, appearing to understand perfectly how to do its duty, holding the boat rope firmly and dragging the boat to shore.

On clear, pleasant days when no one needed to cross, and the day was long and idle, grandfather and Cuicui would sit on a large rock in front of the door and bask in the sun. Or they would throw a piece of wood from a height into the water and urge the yellow dog at their side to leap down from the high rock and retrieve the wood in its mouth. Or Cuicui and the yellow dog would both perk up their ears and listen to grandfather tell stories of wars in the city from years past. Or grandfather and Cuicui would each take a small bamboo flute, put it to their lips, and play tunes for welcoming and sending off brides. When passengers came to cross, the old ferryman would put down his bamboo pipe and go alone to the boat's edge to ferry people across the stream. The one on the rock would then call out sharply:

"Grandpa, grandpa, listen to me play, you sing!"

Grandpa, in the middle of the stream, would sing happily, his hoarse voice and the sound of the bamboo pipe vibrating in the quiet air, making the stream seem a bit more lively. (In fact, the echo of the song made everything even quieter.)

Sometimes those crossing were young cattle from eastern Sichuan, flocks of sheep, or a bride's sedan chair. Cuicui would insist on acting as the ferryman, standing at the boat's head, lazily pulling the cable and letting the boat pass slowly. After the cattle, sheep, and sedan chair reached the shore, Cuicui would follow, stand on the hilltop, and watch these things disappear into the distance before returning to the boat and pulling it close to the family's shore. She would softly imitate the bleating of lambs, the lowing of cows, or pick a handful of wildflowers and tie them on her head, dressing up as a bride by herself.

Chadong city was only one mile from the ferry. When buying oil or salt, or during holidays when grandfather wanted to have a drink, grandfather would not go to the city. Instead, the yellow dog would accompany Cuicui into the city to buy supplies. In the general store, there were large bundles of vermicelli, large jars of white sugar, firecrackers, and red candles—all of which left deep impressions on Cuicui. Back at her grandfather's side, she would talk about these things for half a day. By the river there were also many upstream boats, with hundreds of boatmen busy loading and unloading goods. These boats were much larger and more interesting than the ferry, and Cuicui did not easily forget them.II

The town of Chadong was built by the water and against the mountains. On the side near the mountains, the city wall wound like a long serpent up the slopes. On the water side, space was left outside the city along the riverbank for docks where small canopied boats were moored. When boats traveled downstream, they carried tung oil, rock salt, and dyed gallnuts. When traveling upstream, they carried cotton, cotton yarn, cloth, and miscellaneous goods along with seafood. Running through the various docks was a river street, where houses were half on land and half in water. Because space was limited, all these houses were built with stilted structures. When spring floods came and water gradually rose into the street, the residents of the river street would each use long ladders, one end resting on the eaves, the other on the city wall, cursing and shouting as they carried bundles, bedding, and rice jars up into the city. Only when the water receded would they leave through the city gates. In certain years when the floods came particularly fierce, one or two of the stilted houses along the river would be swept away by the waters, and everyone would stand on the city walls staring blankly. Those who suffered losses would stare blankly as well, seemingly having nothing to say about their misfortune, as if resigned to the inevitable disasters arranged by nature. During floods, one could see from the city walls the suddenly widened river surface, with water surging mightily, carrying along houses, cattle, sheep, and great trees from upstream. Then in places where the current was gentler, in front of the customs house barge, people would often pilot small sampans. Whenever they spotted livestock, a piece of wood, or an empty boat drifting in the river, or heard the cries of a woman or child from a boat, they would quickly paddle downstream to meet the object, secure it with a long rope, and then paddle back to shore. These honest and brave people loved profit and valued righteousness like the local people generally did. Whether rescuing people or objects, they performed with such agility and courage in this kind of thrilling adventure that one could not help but cheer them on.

That river was the historically famous You River, now called the White River. After the White River flows downstream to Chenzhou where it merges with the Yuan River, it becomes somewhat turbid, like water emerging from mountain springs. But if one travels upstream, the deep pools of thirty or fifty feet are all crystal clear. When illuminated by daylight, the small white pebbles at the bottom of the river and the patterned agate stones are all visible with perfect clarity. Fish swimming in the water seem to float in air. Both banks are lined with high mountains, and the mountains contain fine bamboo suitable for papermaking, maintaining a deep emerald color year-round that strikes the eye. Homes near the water are mostly nestled among peach and apricot blossoms. In spring, wherever there are peach blossoms there are homes, and wherever there are homes one can buy wine. In summer, purple calico clothes and trousers hung out to dry in the sunlight serve as flags marking where people live. When autumn and winter arrive, houses on cliffs and by the water all come clearly into view. Yellow mud walls, black tiles, positioned so perfectly and harmonizing so well with the surrounding environment that the impression one receives is truly most pleasant. A traveler with even slight interest in poetry and painting, curled up in a small boat on this little river for thirty days of travel, would not grow weary, precisely because there are wonders everywhere. Nature's boldness and delicacy in every place inspire longing and affection.

The White River originates from the Sichuan border. Small boats traveling upstream on the White River can reach Xiushan in Sichuan territory when spring floods occur. But within Hunan's territory, Chadong is the last water dock. Although the river surface at Chadong is about half a mile wide, when water levels drop in autumn and winter, the flowing water occupies less than twenty zhang, with the rest being blue stone beaches. After boats reach this point, they cannot travel further upstream, so all goods entering and leaving eastern Sichuan pass through this place. Outgoing goods are carried by porters using fir wood carrying poles pressed on their shoulders, while incoming goods are similarly transported away by human labor in bundles and loads.

In the city itself, only one garrison of soldiers, originally reformed from the old Green Camp militia, is stationed, along with about five hundred households. (Among these households, aside from a small number of capitalists who own some mountain fields and oil presses, or who lend money and store oil, rice, and cotton yarn, most are families of military households originally stationed here.) The town also has a customs office, with administrative operations in a small temple below the river street outside the city, where a long banner is permanently displayed. The bureau chief lives in the city. The garrison of soldiers is stationed at the old commander's yamen. Except for buglers who blow their horns daily on the city walls to remind people that troops are stationed here, the other soldiers seem scarcely to exist. On winter days in the city, one sees clothes and vegetables hung out to dry in front of every house. Sweet potatoes with vines hang from the eaves. Bags made of palm fiber, filled with chestnuts, hazelnuts, and other hard-shelled fruits, also hang from the eaves. In every corner of the yards, chickens of various sizes cluck and play. Occasionally one sees some men sitting on their doorsteps sawing wood or chopping trees with axes, stacking the split firewood in the open ground in piles like pagodas. Or one might see several middle-aged women wearing stiffly starched blue cloth clothes, with white cloth embroidered aprons hanging on their chests, bending at the waist in the sunlight, talking while working. Everything remains eternally quiet, and all the people pass each day in this simple solitude. The quietness increases people's contemplation of human affairs and increases their dreams. Those living in this small city must each be waiting in their allotted days with inevitable expectations regarding human affairs. But what do these people think about? Who knows. Those living in the higher parts of the city can stand at their doors and gaze across the river and its scenery. When boats arrive, one can see countless trackers from across the river beach from far away. These trackers sometimes bring delicacies and foreign candy from downstream, and when the boat pulls ashore, they bring these into the city to exchange for money. When boats arrive, children's imaginations focus on the boat pullers. As for adults, raising a brood of chicks, keeping two pigs, asking downstream boatmen to bring back a pair of gold earrings, two bolts of official blue cloth, or a jar of good soy sauce and a double-wick Mobil lamp shade occupies most of the housewife's attention.

Although this small city is so quiet and peaceful, since the place serves as a commercial trading junction for eastern Sichuan, the small river street outside the city presents a different scene. There are inns where merchants lodge, barber shops that stay put. Besides these are restaurants, general stores, oil shops, salt warehouses, cloth shops for patterned fabrics, all occupying their respective positions and adorning this river street. There are also shops selling wooden pulleys for boats, bamboo cables, and cooking pots, as well as people who introduce water workers and make their living from dock work. In front of small restaurants, long tables often display golden-fried carp and tofu, decorated with red chili pepper threads, resting in shallow bowls, with large bamboo tubes beside the bowls filled with red chopsticks. Whoever is willing to spend a little money can sit down by the long table in front, pull out a pair of chopsticks, and a woman with extremely thin eyebrows and white powder on her face will come over and ask: "Big brother, sir, would you like sweet wine? Would you like strong liquor?" Men with higher spirits, those with a sense of humor, or those with some interest in the proprietor will pretend to be angry and say: "Sweet wine? I'm not a child, why ask if I want sweet wine!" Then strong liquor, scooped from a large jar with a bamboo tube and poured into an earthen bowl, arrives at the table immediately. General stores sell Mobil oil and lamps that burn Mobil oil, along with incense, candles, and paper. Oil shops stock tung oil. Salt warehouses pile up rock salt from salt wells. Cloth shops sell white cotton yarn, coarse cloth, cotton, and black crepe silk for head wraps. Shops selling boat supplies display a hundred items, lacking nothing, and occasionally have iron anchors weighing over a hundred pounds resting outside on the road, waiting for customers to inquire about prices. Those whose business is introducing water workers and making their living from dock work keep their doors open all day in their homes on the river street. Boat captains in green silk jackets and rough-handed water workers constantly come and go. The place resembles a teahouse but doesn't sell tea; it's not a tobacco shop yet people can smoke there. Those who come here mostly discuss boat business, but the hiring of boats and trackers follows established customs and doesn't require numerical discussion. They come here mostly to "socialize." With the "dragon head manager" as the center, they discuss local affairs, commercial conditions between the two provinces, and "new happenings" downstream. When organizing gatherings and collecting funds, most often it's here. Rolling dice to determine who becomes the meeting organizer also frequently happens here. Two things commonly become their business: buying and selling boats, buying and selling wives.

Certain parasitic classes that arise in large cities with commercial development, due to the needs of merchants and water workers, have also gathered in this small border town's river street in homes with stilted structures. These women are either brought from nearby villages or are women left stranded after coming with Sichuan troops to Hunan. They wear fake silk clothes and printed cloth trousers, their eyebrows plucked into thin lines, their large buns coated with heavily scented, vulgar oils. During the day with nothing to do, they sit at their doors making shoes, embroidering double phoenixes on shoe tips with red and green silk threads, or embroidering flower pouches for their water worker lovers, watching passersby and whiling away the long days. Or they lean against windows overlooking the river, watching water workers unload cargo and listening to them sing while climbing the masts. In the evening, they take turns entertaining merchants and water workers, faithfully fulfilling the duties of a courtesan.

Due to the simple customs of the border region, even as courtesans, these women remain so genuine and sincere. With unfamiliar people, they must be paid first before closing the door and having fun, but after becoming acquainted, money becomes optional. The courtesans mostly depend on Sichuan merchants for their livelihood, but their emotional attachments are usually with water workers. When feelings are strong, they bite each other's lips and necks, swear oaths, and agree that "after parting, neither shall act recklessly." For forty or fifty days, the one floating on the boat and the one left on shore both pass the time with their hearts tightly bound to the distant person. Especially when the woman's feelings are genuine, her infatuation becomes indescribable. If the man doesn't return by the agreed time, in her dreams she constantly sees the boat pulling ashore, a figure swaying down the gangplank and running toward her. Or if doubt arises during the day, she dreams of the man singing on the mast while ignoring her. Those with weaker temperaments then dream of throwing themselves into the river or swallowing opium; those with stronger temperaments grab a kitchen knife and rush at the water worker. Though their lives are so removed from general society, tears and joy, mixed into their lives through love and loss, make them like young lives on another land, their entire being saturated by these emotions, seeing cold as heat, forgetting everything. If there are differences, it's only that these people are more genuine and somewhat more confused. Short-term contracts, long-term marriages, temporary closures—these transactions concerning a woman's body, due to the simplicity of local customs, are not considered shameful by those involved, nor are they criticized or despised by observers using the standards of educated people. Since these people value righteousness over profit and can keep faith with themselves, even courtesans are often more trustworthy than moral, shame-conscious city people.

The man in charge of the water dock is named Shunshun, someone who mixed through military life in the Qing dynasty, serving as a sergeant in the famous 49th Army Regiment during the revolution. Among those who served as sergeants, some became great men and celebrities through the revolution, some were executed, but he, carrying the foot pain from youthful adventures, returned to his hometown. With the money he had saved, he bought a six-oared wooden boat, rented it to a poor boat owner, and transported cargo between Chadong and Chenzhou. Fortune favored him; within half a year the boat had no accidents. With the money he earned, he married a pale-faced, black-haired young widow with some property. Several years later, on this river, he owned four boats of various sizes, a shop, and two sons.

But this generous and unrestrained man, though his business prospered greatly, could not accumulate wealth like oil merchants because he loved making friends and was generous in helping those in urgent need. Having himself mixed through military life, he understood the hardships of travelers and the feelings of disappointed people. So whenever boat owners whose vessels were wrecked and went bankrupt, retired soldiers passing through, or scholarly wanderers came to this place seeking help, he would do his utmost to assist them. While earning money from the water, he scattered it generously in this way. Though this man had a slight problem with his feet, he could still swim; his walk was unsteady, yet he was so just and impartial in dealing with people. Water affairs were originally quite simple, all governed by custom. Whenever boats collided or one boat harmed another's interests, there were customary methods to resolve it. But to apply these customs and rules to settle everything required a central figure of high standing and great virtue. One autumn, the original administrator died, and Shunshun became his replacement. He was only fifty years old then, and being wise and reasonable, upright and peaceful, and not loving wealth, no one questioned his age.

Now his older son is eighteen and his younger son is sixteen. Both young men are sturdy as young bulls, able to pilot boats, swim, and travel long distances. Whatever young people from small towns and cities can do, they do, and do it all well. The older one, like their father, is bold and open-minded, not bound by convention or small proprieties. The younger one has a temperament closer to his pale-faced, black-haired mother, doesn't like to talk much, but has eyebrows that stand out beautifully, and at first glance one can see he is intelligent and rich in feeling.As the two brothers grew older, they needed to be trained in various aspects of life. Their father took turns sending the two children on travels to different places. When traveling downstream by boat, they would serve as crew members on their own vessels, sharing both hardship and comfort with others. When rowing, they chose the heaviest oars; when towing, they pulled the front rope or second rope. They ate dried fish, chili peppers, and sour pickled vegetables, and slept on hard cabin boards. When traveling upstream by land, they followed merchants and cargo traders through Xiushan, Longtan, and Youyang, conducting business regardless of cold, heat, rain, or snow, always wearing straw sandals and hurrying from station to station. They also wore short knives, and when necessary, they would draw their blades without hesitation, stand in an open space, and settle disputes through hand-to-hand combat with their opponents. The prevailing custom in their circle was that "one must use a knife to deal with enemies, and one must also use a knife to forge friendships." Therefore, whenever the need arose, they never missed an opportunity to use their knives. They learned about trade, social etiquette, how to live in new places, and how to use knives to protect their bodies and honor. The purpose of this education seemed to be to teach the two children courage and righteousness. As a result of this education, both became as sturdy as tigers, yet remained gentle and affectionate, neither arrogant nor frivolous, never bullying others by flaunting their power. Thus, when the father and his two sons were mentioned in the border region of Chadong, people always spoke of their name with respect.

When the father's two sons were still very young, he realized that his eldest son resembled him in every way, yet he unconsciously showed favoritism toward his second son. Due to this unintentional bias, he named his eldest son Tianbo and his second son Nuosong. The idea was that while Tianbo's protection might not prevent all friction in human affairs, what Nuosong received from the Nuo deity, according to local custom, could not be taken lightly. Nuosong was exceptionally beautiful. The boat families of Chadong, being poor at expressing such beauty, simply gave him the nickname "Yueyun." Although few had actually seen Yueyun, the general impression came from the young male lead character Yueyun in theatrical performances. Three

For more than a decade, the military affairs in the border region where the two provinces meet have been managed with emphasis on pacification and preservation, handled with proper methods, with no disturbances occurring. Water and land commerce have neither been halted by warfare nor affected by bandits, with everything maintaining perfect order and the people living contentedly. These people, except when suffering misfortune such as losing cattle at home, capsizing boats, or experiencing other major deaths and changes that cause them great sorrow, seem never to feel the misfortunes and struggles occurring in other parts of China.

The most bustling days of the year in the border town are the Dragon Boat Festival, the Mid-Autumn Festival, and the New Year. These three festivals have excited the people of this place for fifty or sixty years just as they do now, with no change whatsoever, and they remain the most meaningful days for the local residents.

On the Dragon Boat Festival, local women and children all wear new clothes, with a character "王" drawn on their foreheads using realgar mixed with wine. Any household can eat fish and meat on this day. Around eleven o'clock in the morning, all the people of Chadong have finished lunch. After eating, those living in the city lock their doors and the whole family goes out to the riverbank to watch the boat races. Those with acquaintances on River Street can watch from the doorways of the stilt houses; otherwise they stand at the customs gate or at various docks to watch. The dragon boats start from a point in the long pool and end at the customs gate, competing in a race. On this day, military officers, customs officials, and local dignitaries all watch the excitement at the customs gate. The boat racers have made preparations days in advance, dividing into groups and selecting sturdy, agile young men to practice maneuvers in the pool. The boats' shape differs greatly from ordinary wooden boats—they are uniformly long and narrow, with high-raised ends, and their hulls are painted with vermillion-colored lines. During normal times they are stored in dry caves by the riverbank, and when needed, they are dragged into the water. Each boat can seat twelve to eighteen oarsmen, plus one leader, one drummer, and one gong player. Each oarsman holds a short oar and, following the drum's rhythm, rows the boat forward. The leader sits at the bow, with a red cloth wrapped around his head, holding two small signal flags in his hands, waving them left and right to direct the boat's movements. The drummer and gong player usually sit in the middle of the boat. As soon as the boat moves, they begin beating the gong and drum in a simple rhythm to regulate the oarsmen's rowing pace. Since the boat's speed depends on the drum sound, when two boats compete intensely, the drum sounds like thunder. Combined with the cheers from both banks, it reminds one of Liang Hongyu beating the drum during the water battle at Lao Guan River, or Niu Gao beating the drum during the water capture of Yang Mo. Whichever boat reaches the front first receives a reward at the customs gate—a red cloth and a small silver medal. Regardless of which person on the boat receives it, it displays the glory of the entire crew's cooperation. Enthusiastic soldiers, whenever a boat wins, fire five hundred firecrackers by the water to celebrate the victory.

After the boat race, the garrison commander in the city, wishing to celebrate with the people and increase the joy of the festival, releases thirty green-headed, long-necked drake ducks with red cloth strips tied around their necks into the river.
