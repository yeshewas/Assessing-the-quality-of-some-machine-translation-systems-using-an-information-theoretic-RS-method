Paragraph 1: Preface to 'Border Town'

　　There is an unspeakable tenderness for farmers and soldiers—this feeling can be seen everywhere in all my works. I never hide this little feeling. I grew up in the small towns described in the work. My grandfather, father, and brothers were all registered in the military: those who died all died in their posts, and those who did not inevitably spent their entire lives in service. From the perspective of the world I have encountered, I will narrate their loves and hates, sorrows and joys, whether the pen is clumsy or not stray too far from the topic. Because they are upright and honest, some aspects of life are extremely great, some are extremely ordinary, some aspects are extremely beautiful, and some aspects are extremely trivial. When I write about them,—— to make them more human and approachable, I naturally write honestly. But because of this, the work inevitably becomes a useless endeavor.

　　According to current trends, literary theorists, critics, and most readers are very likely to develop unpleasant feelings toward such works. The former claims they are "not outdated," telling people that China does not need such works, while the latter is "too worried about falling behind" and currently unwilling to read such works. This is certainly true. What does "falling behind" mean? A somewhat rational person may never understand, but who among most people isn't afraid of being "left behind"? I have something I want to say: "This book is not written for this majority." Those who have read three to five Western-style books on literary theory and criticism, or have also read a large number of classical and modern world masterpieces, often have life experiences that often do not allow them to know a bit about another place in China or something different beyond being "erudite." Therefore, even if a work aligns with a certain literary theory, critics often praise it in various ways, but such criticism inevitably becomes an insult to the author. They neither wish to understand the true loves and hates, sorrows and joys of this people, nor can they explain the merits or faults of this work,—— this book was not written for them. As for art lovers, they are either university students or middle school students, living in densely populated cities in China, and often honestly and innocently devote part of their precious time to reading newly published literary books in China. They work together to create a habit governed by certain theorists, critics, clever publishers, and literary informants accustomed to lying and spreading rumors, whose lives are far removed from the world described in this work. They don't need such works, so the book doesn't want them to have it. Theorists have literary theories from publications from various countries to verify, so they have nothing to say; critics have writers and works they owe a bit of grievances, enough to praise and criticize them for a lifetime. Most readers, regardless of their interests or beliefs, have works to read. Precisely because we care about the general public, aren't there many people, supposedly for the general public, who are always like spinning tops there transforming? The publication of this book, whether or not abandoned by the theorists and critics who lead the majority, nor by the majority of readers who are led, has not completely abandoned it, but the author of this book has long been determined to abandon this "majority."

　　This book is only intended for those who have already left school, or have never been able to approach it, who still know some Chinese characters, and who live in positions that literary theory, criticism, and lies cannot reach, who care deeply about all the advantages and disadvantages of the entire nation in space and time, for reading. They truly know what rural areas are like today, want to know what rural areas were like in the past, and they are certainly willing to learn from this book a little bit about the countryside and soldiers in the world. The world I write about, even if they are completely unfamiliar to them, their tolerance, their passion for seeking comfort and knowledge from a book, will surely allow them to read it with ease. I do not stop there; I also prepare to give them a chance to contrast. In another work, I will mention the civil war of twenty years, causing some of the farmers who bear the brunt to lose their original simplicity, diligence, peace, and integrity, and what new things they have become. They suffered from heavy taxation, opium poisoning, and opium addiction, turning into poverty and laziness! I will simply narrate the worries of some ordinary people amid change as this nation is led by history toward an unknown fate, as well as the ideas and desires of "survival" and "how to survive" arising from malnutrition. My readers should be rational, and this rationality is based on concern for the changes in Chinese society, recognizing the greatness of the past and the current decline of this nation, each quietly engaged in the great cause of national rejuvenation. This work may only give them a touch of nostalgic nostalgia, a bitter smile, or a nightmare, but at the same time, it might still give them courage and confidence!

　　Traveling from Sichuan through Hunan, there is an official road to the east. When this official road neared the border of western Hunan and reached a small mountain town called "Chadong," there was a small stream, by which stood a white pagoda, beneath which lived a solitary household. This family only has an elderly man, a girl, and a yellow dog.

　　The small stream flows downstream, winding around the mountains, and after about three li, joins the great river of Chadong. If one crosses the creek and crosses the small hill, it takes only one li to reach the edge of Chadong City. The stream flows like the back of a bow, the mountain path is like a bowstring, so there is a slight difference in distance. The stream is about twenty zhang wide, and the riverbed is made of large stones. The still water is so deep it cannot reach the bottom of a pole, yet it remains crystal clear, and the fish swimming in the river can be counted in number. Since the creek was a vital passage between Sichuan and Hunan, the water often rose and fell. Due to financial constraints, it was impossible to build a bridge, so a square-head ferry was arranged. This ferry can carry about twenty passengers at a time, with many people crossing the river, and when the number is large, it can go back and forth repeatedly. At the bow of the ferry, a small bamboo pole was erected with a movable iron ring hanging from it. At both ends of the stream, water troughs held abandoned cables. When people crossed, they hung the iron rings on the old cables, and the boatmen would reach out to climb the rope, slowly pulling the boat across to the opposite bank. As the boat neared the shore, the ferry manager shouted, "Slow down, slow down!" and leapt ashore himself, pulling the iron rings. So the people, goods, cattle, and horses all came ashore, crossed the hill, and disappeared. The ferry crossing was state-owned, so the crossover workers did not have to pay for it. Feeling uneasy, someone grabbed a handful of money and threw it onto the boat's deck. The ferry handler picked it up one by one and still stuffed it into the man's palm, looking as serious as when he argued: "I've got the tolerance to talk, three dou of rice, seven hundred coins, that's enough." He hung bundles of the finest tobacco from the village around his belt, generously giving them to anyone who needed them. Sometimes, judging by the distant passerby's attention to the tobacco nearby, he would stick a small bundle of tobacco onto the person's bundle and say, "Aren't you going to smoke this? This is good, this is wonderful, tastes quite good, and it's perfect for gifting!" "The tea leaves are put into large vats, brewed with boiling water in June to quench the thirst of passersby.

He never thought about what his position meant for himself, but simply lived quietly and faithfully there. His only friends are a ferry and a yellow dog, and his only family is that girl.

But once she understood that people are indifferent, she would calmly play by the water's edge.

　　In Chadong, the city was built by the water and mountains; on the side near the mountain, the city walls stretched like a long snake, climbing along the mountain. On the waterside outside the city, a dock was set up by the river outside the city, where small sailboats could be moored. Everyone curses and shouts, carrying bundles, bedding, and rice jars, and enters the city through the ladders. Only when the water recedes does they exit through the city gate. 　　That river is the historically famous You River, now known as the Bai River. Both banks are surrounded by high mountains, and within the mountains are fine bamboo used for papermaking, which year-round produces a deep green color that captivates the eye. Many families near the water are surrounded by peach and apricot blossoms. In summer, the purple floral cloth clothes and pants that shine under the sunlight can serve as flags for people's residences. In autumn and winter, houses on cliffs and by the water come into view. A traveler with even a slight interest in poetry and painting, curled up on a small boat on this small river, on a thirty-day journey, will surely not feel bored, because miracles are everywhere—natural boldness and ingenuity—each part captivates and captivates.

　　The person at Zhangshui Wharf was named Shunshun, a man who had worked in the military during the Qing dynasty and served as a squad leader in the famous 49th Regiment during the revolution. Among fellow squad leaders, some became great figures or famous figures because of the revolution, some were executed and dismembered. Ascending along the dry road, they followed the freight of eastern Sichuan, passing Xiushan and Longtan, and conducting business in Youyang. Regardless of the weather, rain, or snow, they always wore straw sandals and hurried along the way. He also carried a short knife, so if he had no choice but to act, he would quickly pull the knife out, stand in an open space, wait for the opponent, and then fight that person hand-to-hand. The gang's culture is that "you must use a knife to deal with enemies, and you must use a knife to connect friends," so when a knife is needed, they never let it miss that small chance. Learning trade, socializing, living in a new place, and learning to use knives to protect both body and reputation—the purpose of education seems to be to teach the two children courage and loyalty. 　　At the border between the two provinces, those who have led local military affairs for over a decade have focused on pacification and preservation, handling matters properly, and no incidents have occurred. Water and land commerce was neither interrupted by war nor affected by banditry; everything was in perfect order, and the people lived contentedly. These people, apart from the loss of a cow at home, a capsizing, or other major fatal events that cause them to feel deeply saddened by a tragedy, it seems they will never feel the unfortunate struggles of other parts of China for the people of this border town.

　　The busiest days of the year in the border town are the Dragon Boat Festival, Mid-Autumn Festival, and the New Year. How the three festivals excited the locals thirty or fifty years ago has changed little to now, and they remain the most meaningful days for the local residents.

　　On Dragon Boat Festival, local women and children all wear new clothes, and on their foreheads are drawn with the character for 'king' dipped in realgar and wine. Anyone who comes to this day will surely be able to eat fish and meat. Around eleven in the morning, all the people of Chatong had lunch. After eating, those living in the city all locked their doors and left the city to watch the boats row. If you know someone on He Street, you can go to the entrance of the stilted houses and look around; otherwise, just stand at the customs gate and various docks to look. The river dragon boat starts somewhere in Changtan and ends before customs duties. Engaging in competitions and competitions. Because on this day, officers, tax collectors, and local officials with status all gathered in front of customs to watch the spectacle. Everyone had prepared for rowing several days in advance, dividing into groups and groups to select several strong, agile young men to practice advancing and retreating in the pool. The shape of the boat was very different from ordinary wooden boats; it was long and narrow, with both ends raised high, and the hulls painted with vermilion long lines. During the season, they were often left in dry caves by the river, and when needed, they were towed into the water. Each boat can carry twelve to eighteen rowers: one leader, one drummer, and one gong player. Each rower holds a short oar, slowing down to the beat of the drum to keep the rhythm, rowing the boat forward.
