Paragraph [1]: Preface to "Border Town"

For farmers and soldiers, I harbor an inexpressible warmth and love, a sentiment evident throughout all my works. I have never concealed this feeling. I grew up in the kind of small town described in my works. My grandfather, father, and brothers were all enlisted in the military: those who died invariably died in service, and those who did not would inevitably spend their entire lives in service. I narrate their loves and hates, joys and sorrows, based on the world I have encountered. Even if my pen is clumsy, it should not stray too far from the subject. Because they are upright, honest, some aspects of their lives are extremely grand, while others are extremely ordinary. Some aspects of their character are extremely beautiful, while others are extremely trivial. When I began to write about them, to make them more human and more relatable, I naturally wrote honestly. But because of this, this work might become a futile endeavor.

According to current trends, literary theorists, critics, and most readers are very likely to find such works unpleasant. The former claim to be "not outdated," telling people that China does not need such works, while the latter are "too afraid of being outdated" and are currently unwilling to read such works. This is certainly true. What is "outdated"? A rational person might never understand, but who among the majority isn't afraid of being "outdated"? I have something to say: "This book of mine is not written for this majority." People who have read three or five foreign books on literary theory and criticism, or who have also read a large number of classical and modern world masterpieces, often find that their life experiences do not allow them to know anything about another place and another matter in China, beyond their "erudition." Therefore, even if this work conforms to a certain literary theory, and critics praise it in various ways, such criticism still inevitably becomes an insult to the author. Since they do not wish to understand the true loves and hates, joys and sorrows of this nation, they cannot explain the merits and demerits of this work—this book is not written for them. As for literary enthusiasts, they are either university students or middle school students, distributed in densely populated cities across the country, often honestly and naively dedicating a precious part of their time to reading newly published literary books in China. They are controlled and dominated by a habit created through the concerted efforts of some theorists, critics, clever publishers, and literary newsmongers accustomed to lying and fabricating rumors. Their lives, at the same time, are truly too far removed from the world mentioned in this work. They do not need this kind of work, and this book does not hope to gain them. Theorists have literary theories from various countries' publications to refer to, so they are not at a loss for words. Critics have writers and works with whom they have minor grievances or favors, enough for them to praise or condemn for a lifetime. Most readers, regardless of their tastes or beliefs, have works to read. Precisely because of concern for the general public, aren't there many people who, supposedly for the general public, are always spinning like a top? Even if the publication of this book is not rejected by the leading majority of theorists and critics, and the led majority of readers do not completely abandon it, the author of this book has long intended to abandon this "majority."

This book of mine is only intended for those who "have left school, or have never had access to school, still know some Chinese characters, are engaged in occupations beyond the reach of literary theory, literary criticism, and lying and fabricating news, live in that society, and are extremely concerned about all the good and bad aspects of the entire nation in space and time." They truly know what the current countryside is like, and want to know what the past countryside was like. They will also be willing to learn about a small corner of the world's countryside and soldiers from this book. Even if the world I describe is entirely unfamiliar to them, their tolerance and their eagerness to seek comfort and knowledge from a book will surely enable them to read this book at their leisure. I will not stop here; I also intend to offer them an opportunity for comparison. In another work, I will mention the civil war of the past twenty years, showing what kind of new entity some farmers, who bore the brunt of it, became after their character and soul were crushed by great force, losing their original simplicity, diligence, peacefulness, and uprightness. How impoverished and lazy they became due to exorbitant taxes, levies, and the scourge of opium! I will provide a simple narration of the worries of ordinary people during the changes, and the concepts and desires of "living on" and "how to live on" arising from malnutrition, as this nation is led by history towards an unknown destiny. My readers should be rational, and this rationality should be based on a concern for the current social changes in China, an understanding of the nation's past greatness and present decline, and those who are quietly engaged in the great cause of national rejuvenation. This work may only give them a nostalgic sentiment, or a bitter smile, or perhaps a nightmare, but at the same time, it might also give them courage and confidence!

Paragraph [2]: Chapter 1

I

From Sichuan to Hunan, there is an official road to the east. When this official road approaches a small mountain town called "Chadao" near the border of western Hunan, there is a small stream, and by the stream stands a small white pagoda. Beneath the pagoda lives a solitary family. This family consists of only an old man, a girl, and a yellow dog.

The small stream flows down, winding around the mountains, and after about three li, it merges into the great river of Chadao. If one crosses the stream and walks over the small mountain, it is only one li to the edge of Chadao town. The stream is like the back of a bow, and the mountain path is like the bowstring, hence the slight difference in distance. The small stream is about twenty zhang wide, and its bed is made of large stones. Even if the quiet water is so deep that a pole cannot reach the bottom, it remains clear and transparent, and the fish swimming in the river can be counted. Since the small stream is a thoroughfare for traffic between Sichuan and Hunan, and the water level often rises and falls, a ferryboat with a square bow was arranged due to limited funds for building a bridge. This ferryboat can carry about twenty passengers, including people and horses, across the river at one time. When there are many people, it goes back and forth repeatedly. A small bamboo pole is erected at the bow of the ferryboat, with a movable iron ring hanging from it. A section of discarded cable is stretched across the water troughs at both ends of the stream. When someone wants to cross, they hook the iron ring onto the discarded cable, and the people on the boat reach out and pull themselves along the cable, slowly pulling the boat to the opposite bank. When the boat is about to reach the shore, the person managing the ferryboat shouts, "Slowly, slowly," and then leaps onto the shore, pulling the iron ring. Then all the people, goods, cattle, and horses get off and disappear over the small mountain. The ferry crossing is public property, so those who cross do not need to pay. If someone feels uneasy and throws a handful of money onto the boat deck, the ferryman will pick up each coin and put it back into that person's hand, with the serious expression of someone arguing: "I have enough rations, three dou of rice, seven hundred cash, that's enough. Who needs this!"

But it was no use. Everyone seeks peace of mind, and who would feel comfortable working without pay? Regardless, some people still offered money. The ferryman, however, couldn't bear it, and for the sake of his own peace of mind, he entrusted this money to someone to buy tea and pipe tobacco in Chadao. He would hang bundles of the finest pipe tobacco produced in Chadao from his belt, generously offering it to anyone crossing who needed it. Sometimes, if he judged from their demeanor that a traveler from afar was paying considerable attention to the pipe tobacco at his side, he would tie a small bundle of pipe tobacco to that person's satchel, saying, "Don't you smoke this? This is good, this is wonderful, it tastes great, and it's suitable for giving as a gift!" The tea, on the other hand, was put into large jars in June, steeped in boiling water, and offered to travelers to quench their thirst.

The person managing this ferryboat was the old man living under the pagoda. He had lived for seventy years, and from the age of twenty, he had been guarding this small stream. For fifty years, he had ferried countless people back and forth. Although he was so old, and should have been resting, heaven did not allow him to rest. It was as if he could not separate himself from this way of life. He never pondered the meaning of his duty to himself, but simply lived on quietly and faithfully. What replaced heaven, making him feel the power of life when the sun rose, and preventing him from thinking of dying with the sun when it set, was the girl by his side. His only friends were a ferryboat and a yellow dog; his only relative was that girl.

The girl's mother, the old ferryman's only daughter, fifteen years ago had a secret, illicit relationship with a Chadao soldier, behind her honest father's back. After she became pregnant, this garrison soldier wanted to elope with her downstream. But from the act of eloping, one would be abandoning his duty as a soldier, and the other would have to leave her lonely father. After some consideration, the soldier, seeing that she lacked the courage to run away and he himself could not ruin his reputation as a soldier, thought: if they couldn't be together in life, no one could stop them from dying together. He took poison first. The woman, however, cared for the life within her and couldn't bear it, unable to make a decision. The matter had already become known to her father, the ferryman, but he did not add a single weighty word, acting as if he had never heard of it, and continued to live his days very peacefully. The daughter, feeling both shame and pity, remained by her father's side. After the child in her womb was born, she went to the stream, drank a lot of cold water, and died. In a near miracle, this orphan had grown up, and in the blink of an eye, she was thirteen years old. Because the two mountains where they lived were full of bamboo, with a compelling green color, the old ferryman casually picked a name close to her, "Cui Cui," for this poor orphan.

Cui Cui grew up in the wind and sun, her skin turning dark. Surrounded by green mountains and clear waters, her eyes were as bright as crystal. Nature both nurtured and educated her, making her innocent and lively, like a small wild animal everywhere. She was also so well-behaved, like a muntjac on the mountain, never thinking of cruel things, never worrying, never getting angry. When strangers paid attention to her on the ferryboat, she would stare at them with her bright eyes, looking as if she could run into the deep mountains at any moment. But once she understood that they had no ill intentions, she would calmly play by the water again.

Regardless of the weather, fair or foul, the old ferryman would always be at the bow of the boat. When someone wanted to cross, he would bend slightly, and with both hands, pull the bamboo cable to ferry the boat across the small stream. Sometimes, when he was tired, he would lie down on a large rock by the stream and fall asleep. If someone on the opposite bank waved and called for the ferry, Cui Cui would not let her grandfather get up. Instead, she would jump onto the boat and quickly ferry the travelers across the stream for her grandfather, doing everything smoothly and never making a mistake. Sometimes, she, her grandfather, and the yellow dog would be on the boat together. When crossing, she would help her grandfather. As the boat approached the shore, her grandfather would greet the passengers, "Slowly, slowly," and the yellow dog would then leap onto the shore first, holding the rope in its mouth, as if it understood how to do its duty, and pull the boat tightly to the shore.

On clear and pleasant days, with no one to ferry, the grandfather and Cui Cui would sit on the large rock in front of their door, basking in the sun all day long. Or they would throw a piece of wood from a high place into the water, urging the yellow dog by their side to jump from the high rock and retrieve the wood. Or Cui Cui and the yellow dog would both prick up their ears, listening to the grandfather tell war stories from the town many years ago. Or the grandfather and Cui Cui would each hold a small bamboo flute to their lips, playing tunes for welcoming brides and sending off daughters. When a traveler arrived, the old ferryman would put down his bamboo pipe and follow the boat alone, ferrying people across the stream. The one on the rock, seeing the boat start to move, would then shout in a sharp voice:

"Grandpa, Grandpa, listen to me play, you sing!"

Grandpa would happily start singing in the middle of the stream, his hoarse voice and the sound of the bamboo pipe reverberating in the quiet air. The stream seemed to become a little livelier. (In fact, the echo of the singing made everything even quieter.)

Sometimes, those crossing were small calves from eastern Sichuan going to Chadao, or flocks of sheep, or a bride's sedan chair. Cui Cui would always insist on being the ferryman, standing at the bow, lazily pulling the cable, letting the boat slowly pass. After the cattle, sheep, and sedan chair landed, Cui Cui would follow them, standing on the small hilltop, watching them go far away before returning to the boat and pulling it closer to the bank near home. She would also softly imitate the bleating of lambs, the lowing of cows, or pick a handful of wild flowers to tie on her head, pretending to be a bride.

Chadao mountain town is only one li from the ferry crossing. When buying oil and salt, or when her grandfather had a drink during festivals, if her grandfather didn't go to town, the yellow dog would accompany Cui Cui into town to buy things. In the general store, there were large bundles of vermicelli, large jars of white sugar, firecrackers, and red candles, all of which left a deep impression on Cui Cui. When she returned to her grandfather, she would talk about these things for a long time. There were also many upstream boats by the river, with hundreds of boatmen busy loading and unloading goods. These boats were all much larger and more interesting than the ferryboat.Cui Cui was not easily forgotten. 2

The town of Chadong was built against the mountains and by the water. On the side facing the mountains, the city wall snaked up the slopes like a long serpent. On the side facing the water, space was left outside the city along the riverbank for docks, where small sampans moored. Downstream, they transported tung oil, green salt, and gallnuts for dyeing. Upstream, they carried cotton, cotton yarn, cloth, sundry goods, and seafood. A river street connected the various docks, where most houses were half on land and half in the water. Due to limited space, all these houses had stilt foundations. When spring floods swelled the river and the water gradually entered the street, the residents of the river street would each use long ladders, one end resting on the eaves and the other on the city wall. Everyone would curse and shout, carrying bundles, bedding, and rice jars, and climb the ladders into the city. They would only leave the city gate when the water receded. If the water came particularly fiercely in a certain year, one or two stilt houses along the river would inevitably be washed away by the flood. Everyone would stare blankly from the city walls. Those who suffered losses would also stare blankly, as if speechless about their misfortunes, similar to witnessing other irremediable calamities under nature's arrangement. During floods, from the city, one could see the suddenly widened river surface, the vast and surging water, carrying houses, cattle, sheep, and large trees floating down from upstream with the mountain water. Then, in areas where the current was slower, in front of the customs pontoon, people would often paddle small sampans. As soon as they saw an animal, a small log, or an empty boat floating in the middle of the river, or heard a woman or child crying out from a boat, they would quickly paddle their boat, intercepting the object downstream, tying it with a long rope, and then paddling it to the shore. These honest and brave people, like the local residents, loved profit and upheld justice. Whether rescuing people or objects, they acted with great agility and courage in a joyful and adventurous manner, making people unable to help but cheer for them.

That river was the historically famous You River, now called the Bai River. Downstream, where the Bai River merges with the Yuan River at Chenzhou, it becomes slightly turbid, suggesting the presence of mountain springs. If one traces it upstream, deep pools, three to five zhang deep, are all crystal clear to the bottom. Illuminated by daylight, the small white pebbles and patterned agate stones at the riverbed are all clearly visible. Fish swim back and forth in the water as if floating in the air. The banks are mostly high mountains, and within the mountains are many fine bamboos suitable for papermaking, which remain a deep emerald green all year round, striking to the eye. Houses near the water are often nestled among peach and apricot blossoms. In spring, one only needs to observe that wherever there are peach blossoms, there are houses, and wherever there are houses, one can buy wine. In summer, the dazzling purple cloth clothes and trousers, sun-dried, serve as flags indicating the presence of homes. When autumn and winter arrive, houses on cliffs and by the water are all clearly visible and pleasing to the eye. With yellow mud walls and dark tiles, their positions are always so appropriate and in harmony with the surrounding environment, giving a truly pleasant first impression. A traveler with a slight interest in poetry and painting, curled up in a small boat on this small river for a thirty-day journey, would certainly not feel bored, precisely because there are wonders everywhere, and nature's boldness and delicacy, in every aspect, captivate and enchant.

The source of the Bai River comes from the border of Sichuan. Small boats traveling upstream on the Bai River can reach Xiushan in Sichuan when the spring floods arrive. However, within Hunan's borders, Chadong is the last water terminal. Although the river surface at Chadong is about half a li wide, when the water recedes in autumn and winter, the flowing water in the riverbed is less than twenty zhang wide, the rest being just a stretch of bluestone. Since boats cannot proceed upstream from here, all import and export goods for eastern Sichuan are loaded and unloaded at this location. Export goods are carried by porters on their shoulders using fir wood carrying poles, and import goods are also bundled and carried by human labor from this place.

In this town, there was only one battalion of garrison soldiers, reorganized from the former Green Standard Army, and about five hundred households. (Among these households, besides a few who owned some mountain fields and oil mills, or small capitalists who lent money and hoarded oil, rice, and cotton yarn, the majority were families with military status who had settled here as garrison troops in earlier times.) There was also a likin bureau, whose office was in a small temple below the river street outside the city, often displaying a long banner. The director lived in the city. The battalion of soldiers was stationed at the old commander's yamen. Except for the bugler who played his bugle on the city wall every day, letting people know that troops were still stationed here, the other soldiers seemed to be non-existent. On winter days, going into the city, one would only see clothes and green vegetables drying in front of every house. Sweet potatoes, still on their vines, were often hung under the eaves. Sacks made of palm fiber, filled with chestnuts, hazelnuts, and other hard-shelled fruits, were also often hung under the eaves. Large and small chickens clucked and played in every corner of the houses. Occasionally, a man would be seen sawing wood or chopping trees with an axe on the threshold of his house, piling the chopped firewood into pagodas in the open yard. Or one might see several middle-aged women, dressed in stiffly starched blue cloth clothes, with white cloth embroidered aprons hanging on their chests, bending over and working while talking in the sunlight. Everything was always so quiet, and the lives of all the people passed in this simple solitude every day. A sense of tranquility increased people's capacity for "human affairs" and enhanced dreams. Those living in this small town must also, each in their allotted days, harbor inevitable expectations of love and hate regarding human affairs. But what did these people think? Who knows. Living in the higher parts of the city, one could gaze at the opposite bank and the river scenery from the doorstep. When boats arrived, one could see countless boat trackers from afar on the opposite bank. Some of these trackers also came from downstream, bringing fine pastries, foreign candies, and the like, which they would bring into the city to exchange for money upon landing. When a boat arrived, the children's imagination would be focused on those pulling the boat. As for the adults, hatching a nest of chicks, raising two pigs, asking a downstream boatman to bring back a pair of gold earrings, two zhang of official blue cloth, or a jar of good soy sauce, or a double-wick Mobil lamp shade, would occupy most of a housewife's mind.

Although this small town was so quiet and peaceful, as it was a commercial hub for eastern Sichuan, the small river street outside the city was a bit different. There were inns for merchants to stay, and barbershops that stayed put. In addition, restaurants, general stores, oil shops, salt warehouses, and clothing stores all had their own place, adorning this river street. There were also shops selling sandalwood winches, bamboo cables, and pot-and-pan shops for boats, and families who introduced sailors to the profession of working at the docks. On the long counter in front of small restaurants, there were often pan-fried golden-brown carp and tofu, decorated with red chili threads, lying in shallow bowls. In large bamboo tubes next to the bowls, there were bundles of red chopsticks. Anyone willing to spend a little money could sit down at the long counter in front of the door, pick up a pair of chopsticks, and then a woman with finely plucked eyebrows and powdered face would come over and ask, "Brother, sir, do you want sweet wine? Or spirits?" A man with a bit more fire, a sense of humor, and a slight interest in the proprietress, would pretend to be angry and say, "Drink sweet wine? I'm not a child, why ask me if I want sweet wine!" Then, strong spirits, scooped from a large jar with a bamboo tube and poured into an earthenware bowl, would immediately appear on the table beside him. General stores sold Mobil oil and foreign lamps that burned Mobil oil, as well as incense, candles, and paper. Oil shops hoarded tung oil. Salt warehouses stored green salt from fire wells. Clothing stores sold white cotton yarn, coarse cloth, cotton, and black crepe silk for head wraps. Shops selling boat supplies had everything imaginable, and occasionally, iron anchors weighing over a hundred catties would be placed outside on the roadside, waiting for customers to inquire about prices. Those specializing in introducing sailors and working at the docks kept their front doors open all day in their river street homes. Boat owners in blue satin jackets and rough-and-tumble sailors frequently came and went. The place resembled a teahouse but didn't sell tea, and wasn't an opium den but allowed smoking. Although the discussions here were about boat business, the loading and unloading of boats, and the pulling of boats by trackers, generally followed established rules, so there was no need for numerical discussions. Most of them came here for "social gatherings." Centered around the "dragon head manager," they discussed local current events, commercial conditions in the two provinces, and "new things" downstream. Most meetings and fundraising activities took place here, and when they took turns being the head of the association by rolling dice, it often happened here too. Two things often became their business: buying and selling boats, and buying and selling wives.

In large cities, certain parasites emerged with the development of commerce. Due to the needs of merchants and sailors, this small border town's river street also had such a group of people, gathered in some stilt houses. These women were either brought from nearby villages or were women who had drifted to Hunan with the Sichuan army. They wore fake foreign silk clothes, printed calico trousers, plucked their eyebrows into thin lines, and applied heavily scented, vulgar oil to their large hair buns. During the day, with nothing to do, they would sit at their doorways making shoes, embroidering double phoenixes with red and green silk threads on the shoe tips, or embroidering flower pouches for their sailor lovers, while watching passersby to pass the long day. Or they would lean against the window overlooking the river, watching sailors unload goods and listening to them sing while climbing the mast. In the evening, they would take turns entertaining merchants and sailors, diligently fulfilling all the duties of a "prostitute."

Due to the simple customs of the border region, even as "prostitutes," they were always so honest. When dealing with unfamiliar people, they had to be paid first before closing the door and getting wild. Once acquainted, money became optional. Most "prostitutes" relied on Sichuan merchants for their livelihood, but their emotional attachments were often with sailors. If the feelings were good, they would bite each other's lips and necks, swearing an oath and agreeing that "after parting, neither is allowed to fool around." For forty or fifty days, the one floating on the boat and the one remaining on shore would both spend those days blankly, tightly binding their hearts to the distant person. Especially the women, whose emotions were so sincere and foolish beyond description. If a man didn't return after the agreed time, she would often dream that the boat had docked, and a person would stagger from the gangplank onto the shore, running straight towards her. Or if she had doubts during the day, she would surely dream of the man singing on the mast in another direction, ignoring her. Those with weaker personalities would then drown themselves in the dream or swallow opium. Those with stronger personalities would grab a kitchen knife and rush towards the sailor. Although their lives were so distant from mainstream society, tears and joy, intertwined with love, hate, gain, and loss, permeated their lives, just like other young lives in other lands. Their entire being was steeped in that love and hate, feeling hot when cold, forgetting everything. If there were any differences, it was only that these people were more genuine and a bit more muddled. Short-term arrangements, long-term marriages, temporary closures—these transactions involving a woman's body, due to the simplicity of the local customs, were not considered vulgar or shameful by those involved, nor were they criticized or looked down upon by observers with scholarly notions. These people valued righteousness over profit and were trustworthy and self-disciplined. Even "prostitutes" were often more reliable than city dwellers who preached morality and shame.

The person in charge of the water terminal was named Shunshun, a character who had spent time in the military during the Qing Dynasty. During the revolution, he was a squad leader in the famous 49th Army Regiment. Among those who were also squad leaders, some became great and famous figures due to the revolution, while others were beheaded and dismembered. He, however, returned to his hometown with the foot pain he had acquired from youthful revelry, and with his accumulated savings, bought a six-oared white wooden boat, which he rented to a poor boat owner to transport goods between Chadong and Chenzhou. With good fortune, the boat had no accidents within half a year, so from his earnings, he married a pale-faced, black-haired young widow who had some property. A few years later, he owned four boats of various sizes on this river, a shop, and two sons.

However, this generous and uninhibited man, despite his successful career, could not become as wealthy as the oil merchants because he loved making friends, was generous, and always helped those in need. Having spent time in the military, he understood the hardships of travelers and the feelings of the disheartened. Therefore, he did his best to help any boatmen who went bankrupt due to boat accidents, passing retired soldiers, or scholars on their travels who came to him for help after hearing of his reputation. He earned money from the water and then freely spent it. Although he had a slight foot problem, he could still swim; walking was difficult for him, but he was fair and selfless. Everything on the water was originally very simple, all governed by custom. If one boat collided with another, or if one boat hindered the interests of another person or boat, there were customary methods to resolve it. However, to manage everything according to these customary rules, a central figure of high age and great virtue was needed. One autumn, the original person in charge died, and Shunshun became his replacement. At that time, he was only fifty years old, and because he was clear-headed, reasonable, upright, peaceful, and not greedy for money, no one doubted his age.

By now, his elder son was eighteen and the younger sixteen. Both young men were as sturdy as young bulls, capable of steering boats, swimming, and walking long distances. They could do everything a young person from a small town could do, and they excelled at all of it. The elder son, like his father, was bold and open-minded, not bound by conventional trivialities. The younger son's temperament was closer to that of his pale-faced, black-haired mother; he was not talkative, but his eyebrows were exceptionally refined and outstanding.It was immediately clear that he was intelligent and emotional.

As the two brothers grew older, their father, needing to train them in various aspects of life, took turns sending them on trips. When traveling downstream by boat, they often joined his boat as crew, sharing the hardships with others. When rowing, they chose the heaviest oar; when towing, they pulled the lead or second rope. They ate dried fish, chili peppers, and fermented vegetables, and slept on hard cabin planks. When traveling upstream by land, they followed the Sichuan-East cargo, doing business through Xiushan, Longtan, and Youyang. Regardless of cold, heat, rain, or snow, they always wore straw sandals and hurried from station to station. They also carried short knives, and if forced into a confrontation, they would swiftly draw their knives, stand in an open space, and wait for their opponent, then resolve it with hand-to-hand combat. The gang's custom was that "to deal with enemies, one must use a knife; to connect with friends, one must also use a knife," so when a knife was needed, they never let the opportunity pass. Learning trade, social etiquette, how to live in a new place, and how to use a knife to protect their bodies and honor—the purpose of their education seemed to be to teach the two children courage and loyalty. As a result of this education, both became as strong as tigers, yet they were also amiable and approachable, neither arrogant nor ostentatious, nor did they bully others. Thus, when the father and two sons were mentioned in the border town of Chadong, everyone spoke of their name with respect.

When his two sons were very young, the father understood that his elder son was similar to himself in every way, but he showed a slight favoritism towards the second son. Due to this unconscious partiality, he named the elder son Tianbao and the younger son Nuosong. The meaning was that Tianbao, blessed by heaven, might encounter some friction in human affairs, but as for what the Nuo god sent, according to local custom, people could not slight it in the slightest. Nuosong was very beautiful, and the boatmen of Chadong were clumsy at praising such beauty, only knowing to give him the nickname "Yue Yun." Although no one had ever seen Yue Yun with their own eyes, the general impression of him, derived from the young actor Yue Yun on stage, conveyed a similar spirit.

Three

For over ten years, the local military authorities at the border of the two provinces focused on maintaining peace and order, and their handling of affairs was effective, preventing any major incidents. Water and land commerce was neither halted by war nor affected by bandits; everything was extremely orderly, and the people lived contentedly. These people, unless a cow died in their home, a boat capsized, or some other major death occurred, causing them great sorrow due to misfortune, seemed to be forever unaware of the struggles and misfortunes in other parts of China.

The busiest days in the border town were the Dragon Boat Festival, Mid-Autumn Festival, and New Year. These three festivals, which excited the local people thirty to fifty years ago, remain unchanged to this day, still being the most significant days for the residents.

On Dragon Boat Festival day, local women and children all wore new clothes, and a character "王" (king) was drawn on their foreheads with realgar wine. Every household would eat fish and meat on this day. Around 11 AM, all the people of Chadong would have lunch. After eating, those living in the town would lock their doors and the entire family would go out of town to the river to watch the boat races. Those who knew people on River Street could watch from the doorways of the stilt houses; otherwise, they would stand at the customs office entrance and various docks. The dragon boats in the river started at a certain point in the long pool and finished in front of the customs office, competing against each other. On this day, military officers, customs officials, and local dignitaries all gathered in front of the customs office to watch the excitement. Preparations for the boat races had begun several days earlier, with groups and teams selecting a number of strong and agile young men to practice maneuvering in the pool. The boats were very different from ordinary wooden boats, uniformly long and narrow, with high, upturned ends. Their hulls were painted with vermilion lines. Normally, they were stored in dry caves by the river and dragged into the water when needed. Each boat could hold twelve to eighteen paddlers, one leader, one drummer, and one gong player.
